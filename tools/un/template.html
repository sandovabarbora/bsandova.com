Research · politics · Czechia at the UN, 2014–2025

Czech votes at the UN changed little between governments, except on Israel and Palestine

Registered result: across the changes of government in 2017 and 2021, Czechia voted with the EU majority in the UN General Assembly on about {{cz_mean}} % of recorded votes, as the other members did, and no change met the registered rule for a shift. Described after the results: the area where Czechia most often parts from the EU majority is Israel and Palestine, where its yes votes fell from {{ipy_sobotka}} to {{ipy_babis}} to {{ipy_fiala}} % in the periods of governments of different parties, while the other members stayed near {{ipo_fiala}} %. The test for the third Babiš government is registered and pending.

When a state asks for a recorded vote in the UN General Assembly, every member's vote is kept, and the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library publishes these records[[un-data]]. The EU members coordinate in New York and usually vote alike, so how often a member votes with the rest is a plain measure of where it stands among them. Research on these records more often estimates each state's position on a scale[[bailey]]; agreement can be read vote by vote. It asks whether Czechia's agreement with the EU majority, and with the United States, Russia, Hungary and Slovakia, changed between the periods of its governments by more than the other EU members' agreement changed over the same votes. The design was registered before any Czech vote was read; this page reports the changes of 2017 and 2021.

The answer for those two changes is no visible change. Czechia voted with the majority of the other members on {{cz_sob}} % of the votes of the Sobotka period, {{cz_bab}} % of the Babiš period and {{cz_fia}} % of the Fiala period; the other members' mean over the same votes was {{co_sob}}, {{co_bab}} and {{co_fia}} %. Relative to them, Czech agreement changed by {{a_est}} points in 2017 ({{a_label}}) and {{b_est}} points in 2021 ({{b_label}}), and neither change met the registered rule for a shift. The intervals do not rule out a change of a few points either, and most of the 2021 figure rests on the votes of the emergency special sessions. Over the past decade, then, a change of government in Prague did not come with a visible change in how Czechia voted at the UN overall. That is the yardstick for the third Babiš government: a shift of more than a few points would be something that did not happen in the past decade.

Israel and Palestine, described after the results

The votes on which Czechia left the EU majority grew from {{d_sobotka}} in the Sobotka period to {{d_babis}} in the Babiš period and {{d_fiala}} in the Fiala period, and in the Fiala period {{t_fiala_israel_palestine}} of them concerned Israel and Palestine. The positions on all votes of that topic were counted after the results (table). There were {{ipv_sobotka}}, {{ipv_babis}} and {{ipv_fiala}} such votes in the three periods, so the rise in departures did not come with more of them. The other members voted yes in {{ipo_sobotka}}, {{ipo_babis}} and {{ipo_fiala}} % of their positions, a share that changed little. Czechia's yes-share fell from {{ipy_sobotka}} to {{ipy_babis}} to {{ipy_fiala}} %, and its no votes went from {{ip_sobotka_N}} to {{ip_babis_N}} to {{ip_fiala_N}}.

The three periods are those of governments of different parties: ČSSD with ANO and KDU-ČSL under Bohuslav Sobotka[[sobotka]]; governments led by Andrej Babiš's ANO[[babis-bio]][[babis1]][[babis2]], the second with ČSSD's chair as first deputy prime minister[[hamacek]]; and ODS, KDU-ČSL, TOP 09, the Pirates and STAN under Petr Fiala[[fiala-coalition]][[fiala]]. New texts on Gaza came to the vote only from October 2023, and the data cannot say what accounts for either step. The votes differ between periods, so the comparison is not like for like; the other members' flat yes-share is the only comparator. Read this way, the Czech exception is one issue, across parties, rather than a pattern of left and right or of one government.

Described after the results, not registered (tools/un/positions.py). Votes of the Israel and Palestine topic by the rule of tools/un/departures.py, defined after the results; it pools different texts in each period. The other members' shares are of all their positions on these votes.

Published accounts describe Czech support for Israel as long-standing. According to Čejka, it goes back to Czechoslovakia's arms and training for the new Israeli army in 1948–1949, and during the presidency of Václav Havel Czechoslovakia and then the Czech Republic “became one of the most prominent supporters of Israel”[[cejka]]. In November 2012 the General Assembly made Palestine a non-member observer state by 138 votes to 9 with 41 abstentions[[un2012]]; according to Čejka, no state in Europe other than Czechia fully supported Israel's position[[cejka]]. Dyduch found in 2018 that the Visegrád governments had “frequently abstained from voting in the UN General Assembly or even voted against” on the conflict[[dyduch]]. In 2014–2017, however, the recorded votes did not set Czechia apart from the EU majority on these texts: it left the EU majority on {{ip_dep_sobotka}} of them, and its yes-share matched the others'. On 21 December 2017, in the gap between the Sobotka and Babiš periods, Czechia abstained on the emergency-session resolution on the status of Jerusalem, saying it supported the EU position but did not believe the draft would contribute to the peace process[[ga11995]]; the resolution titled “Jerusalem” of 1 December 2021, on which it voted no, is a different text, under the item on the situation in the Middle East.

During a visit to Israel in October 2023, Prime Minister Fiala said that “the Czech Republic stands by Israel and will stand by it as long as necessary”[[fiala-visit]]. Czechia, with Austria and eight others, voted against the call for a humanitarian ceasefire of 12 December 2023[[un2023]]. On 11 December 2024 it was one of nine members voting against the demand for a ceasefire in Gaza; Hungary was another, and Austria voted yes[[un2024b]]. The ministry's stated reason was that the resolution “fails to unequivocally link a durable ceasefire to the release of all the hostages”[[mzv2024]]; in June 2025 Czechia abstained on a resolution on Gaza, stating that the text “fails to unequivocally condemn Hamas’ unspeakable atrocities of 7 October”[[mzv2025]]. These are the ministry's reasons, and the data do not test them. The resolution of May 2024 on Palestine's rights in the UN passed by 143 votes to 9 with 25 abstentions[[un2024a]]; the data file records Czechia's vote against it.

What to look for in the third Babiš government

Andrej Babiš's third government took office on {{cab_25}}, with Petr Macinka as foreign minister[[macinka]]. Its registered test compares Czechia's agreement with the EU majority in the Fiala period ({{fia_from}} – {{fia_to}}) with the votes cast from {{q1_from}} to {{q1_to}}, net of the other members' change, by the same frozen code, and does the same for the four partners. Votes cast before registration are listed in stage B, untested. Version 5 of the file, ending in December 2025, came out in February 2026, so results are expected around February 2027; if no version covering December 2026 is out by 30 April 2027, the change log will say so.

The test is the overall figure, and it may well come out underpowered. With about {{pw_q1_votes_lo}} to {{pw_q1_votes_hi}} votes in one autumn, the power analysis put the median member's 90 % interval at up to {{pw_q1_width}} points, so for a typical member a change of about {{margin}} points can be told from ordinary movement. Czechia's standard error in the two earlier changes was {{cz_se_lo}} to {{cz_se_hi}} points, against {{med_se_lo}} to {{med_se_hi}} for the median member, which fits departures clustered in a few agenda items. Scaling by that ratio, a rough projection computed in article.py and not registered, gives a Czech 90 % interval about {{proj_w_lo}} to {{proj_w_hi}} points wide, around the {{margin2}}-point bound beyond which a result is underpowered. Czech agreement in the Fiala period, {{cz_fia}} %, leaves only {{headroom}} points for higher agreement. If Czech votes do move away from the EU, the past decade suggests looking at particular issues rather than the overall figure.

The author stated her expectation on 15 October 2026, before any Czech vote was read: “I expect a greater lean towards 'Trumpist' policy, and perhaps a greater lean towards Hungary, Russia and Slovakia, though with the Russian invasion of Ukraine in mind” (in Czech: „očekávám větší náklon k 'trumpistické' politice a možná větší náklon Maďarsko, Rusko, Slovensko, ale s ohledem na ruskou invazi na Ukrajinu“). It concerns the partners in the third Babiš period and will be checked there; she stated no expectation for the EU majority or for the earlier changes, so nothing on this page tests it.

Data

The file is version 5 of the UN's General Assembly Voting Data, published in February 2026, with one row for each member's vote on each resolution adopted by a recorded vote[[un-data]]. From {{sob_from}} to the end of the file on 30 December 2025 it holds {{votes_all}} recorded votes. Paragraph votes, amendments and failed drafts are not in it. A position is yes, no or abstain; a member that did not vote has none. The EU is the 27 members of February 2020 in every period, without the United Kingdom.

The periods follow the cabinets, each appointed with its foreign minister, who instructs the mission in New York. Each later period starts {{buffer}} days after the appointment, because the December plenary adopts texts voted in committee under the old government. The Sobotka period runs from {{sob_from}}, the day the cabinet was appointed, to {{sob_to}}: {{sob_votes}} votes, on {{sob_split}} of which the other EU members did not all vote alike. The Babiš period runs from {{bab_from}} to {{bab_to}} ({{bab_votes}} votes, {{bab_split}} split), and the Fiala period from {{fia_from}} to {{fia_to}} ({{fia_votes}} votes, {{fia_split}} split). The first Babiš cabinet, appointed on {{cab_17}}, lost its confidence vote and is pooled with the second; the Fiala cabinet was appointed on {{cab_21}}. The {{buf17_votes}} and {{buf21_votes}} votes in the two gaps belong to neither period.

How this was done

On each vote, Czechia's agreement with a position is 1 if it voted the same way, 0.5 if one of the two abstained and the other did not, and 0 for yes against no. The EU majority is the most common position of the other EU members that voted, and a tie drops the vote. For a partner such as Russia, the position is that partner's own vote.

The registered estimate, β, is a difference in differences on votes. On each vote, it takes Czechia's agreement minus the mean agreement of the other {{eu_others}} members, each of them scored against the majority of the members other than itself and Czechia; β is the mean of that difference over the later period minus its mean over the earlier one. A β of −1 point means that Czechia's agreement fell by one point more, or rose by one point less, than the other members' over the same votes, so a change in the agenda or in a partner's own votes is not counted as a Czech change. Intervals come from resampling agenda items, the families of resolutions that return every year, within each period ({{draws}} draws).

The same β is computed with each other EU member in Czechia's place. The rank p is the share of all members, Czechia included, whose β divided by its standard error is at least as far from zero as Czechia's. With {{eu_n}} members, a p of {{p_cut}} or less puts Czechia among the two or three members whose change stands out most.

Each β takes the first label that applies. It is lower or higher agreement after the change if β is at least {{minimum}} points from zero, its 95 % interval excludes zero and p is at most {{p_cut}}; no change larger than {{margin}} points if its 90 % interval lies within ±{{margin}} points; underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than {{margin2}} points, so that the previous rule could not have applied; and inconclusive otherwise. The margin was set from a power analysis run on the other members only, before any Czech vote was read: for changes like the two of stage A, the median member's 90 % interval was {{pw_q2_width}} points wide, and nine in ten members moved by less than {{pw_q2_beta}} points relative to the rest.

Checks, partners and the numbers of members

Seven checks were registered with the estimate, listed in the robustness table; two need a word. The confidence-date check starts the later periods {{buffer}} days after the confidence votes of {{conf_17}} and {{conf_21}}, and the US check cuts the Fiala period at {{us_change}}, when the US government changed. Czechia's share of votes it did not cast is reported, because a government that avoids votes would be invisible to β. A cross-check against UNGA-DM, a database of General Assembly decisions compiled independently of the UN file[[ungadm]], tests the positions themselves.

When the partner is Hungary or Slovakia, that member is neither in Czechia's place nor among the comparison members, so its row ranks {{ranked_partner}} members instead of {{ranked_eu}}. Other members are ranked only if they voted on at least {{min_after}} votes of the later period. The partners give twelve labels over the three changes, eight of them in this stage, reported without a correction for multiplicity; none is read on its own.

Votes on which Czechia left the EU majority

The registered list holds every vote on which Czechia's position differed from the majority of the other EU members: {{d_sobotka}} of {{v_sobotka}} votes in the Sobotka period ({{rate_sob}} %), {{d_babis}} of {{v_babis}} in the Babiš period ({{rate_bab}} %) and {{d_fiala}} of {{v_fiala}} in the Fiala period ({{rate_fia}} %). The totals count votes on which Czechia voted and the majority was not tied; unlike β, the counts do not subtract what the other members did.

The votes were grouped into four topics after the results had been seen, by words in the agenda item and the title, in this order: nuclear weapons and disarmament; Israel and Palestine, including the Golan and UNRWA, the UN agency for Palestine refugees; racism, meaning the follow-up to the Durban conference; and everything else. In the Sobotka period, {{t_sobotka_nuclear}} of the {{d_sobotka}} departures were on nuclear weapons and {{t_sobotka_racism}} on the Durban follow-up, and {{tw_sobotka_ip}} on Israel and Palestine. In the Babiš period, {{t_babis_israel_palestine}} were on Israel and Palestine, {{t_babis_nuclear}} on nuclear weapons, {{t_babis_racism}} on the Durban follow-up and {{t_babis_other}} on other topics. In the Fiala period, {{t_fiala_israel_palestine}} of the {{d_fiala}} ({{fia_ip_share}} %) were on Israel and Palestine, among them the annual resolutions on settlements, East Jerusalem, the question of Palestine and UNRWA and the emergency-session votes on Gaza.

Most departures were half a step away from the majority. In the Fiala period, Czechia abstained where most of the EU voted yes on {{ay_fiala}} votes and voted no where most abstained on {{na_fiala}}. On {{o_fiala}} votes it voted no while most of the other members voted yes, all of them on Israel and Palestine. In the Sobotka period there were {{o_sobotka_w}}, and in the Babiš period {{o_babis}}: {{bab_opp}}.

Robustness

The checks are not used to relabel an estimate (§7). Under the same rule, applied to their intervals with the primary estimate's rank p, some would carry a different label: {{relabelled}}. None would meet the rule for a shift. The largest moves come from split votes only, the votes on which a member's departures have room to show: β is {{split_a}} points for 2017 and {{split_b}} points for 2021, with 90 % intervals of {{split_a_ci}} and {{split_b_ci}}, from {{split_a_n}} and {{split_b_n}} votes in the later periods. Without the emergency special sessions, the 2021 estimate is {{reg_b}} points. With the United States, the Fiala period cut at {{us_change}} gives {{usa_cut}} points ({{usa_cut_ci}}). The other checks stay within {{chk_dev}} points of the registered estimates.

What this does not show

It does not show what a government did. A UN vote is cast by the mission on instructions, and this design cannot say who decided a vote, or whether a change came from the government, the ministry, the officials or the agenda. Each period mixes changes of prime minister, coalition and foreign minister. The foreign minister was Lubomír Zaorálek, nominated by ČSSD, through the Sobotka period[[zaoralek]]. In the Babiš period it was Martin Stropnický from December 2017 to June 2018[[babis1]]; Jan Hamáček, chair of ČSSD, ran the ministry from June to October 2018 and again in April 2021[[hamacek]]; Tomáš Petříček was minister from October 2018 to April 2021 and Jakub Kulhánek from April 2021[[babis2]]. In the Fiala period it was Jan Lipavský[[fiala]], and in the third Babiš government it is Petr Macinka, chair of Motoristé sobě[[macinka]].

Positions on failed drafts, amendments and single paragraphs are not seen. Agreement sits near its ceiling, so a handful of votes moves the estimate; the 2021 estimate rests largely on the emergency special sessions. The count of positions on Israel and Palestine pools different texts in each period, and the ministry's stated reasons cover only 2 of the {{ipv_fiala}} votes of that topic in the Fiala period. The partners change too: the United States in January 2025, Slovakia in October 2023, and Hungary held a parliamentary election in April 2026, so the partner estimates mix Czech changes with theirs. The headline question rests on one autumn's votes, about {{pw_q1_votes_lo}} to {{pw_q1_votes_hi}}. The topic grouping and the position count were made after the results and are reading aids, not tests.
