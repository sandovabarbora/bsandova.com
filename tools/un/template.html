Research · politics · Czechia at the UN, 2014–2025

Czechia's votes at the UN: did agreement with the EU change between governments?

In recorded votes of the UN General Assembly, Czechia voted with the majority of the other EU members on {{cz_lo}} to {{cz_hi}} % of votes in the periods of each of its last three governments, as did the other members on average ({{co_lo}} to {{co_hi}} %). Relative to the other members, its agreement changed by {{a_est}} points from the Sobotka to the Babiš period and by {{b_est}} points from the Babiš to the Fiala period, and neither change meets the registered rule for a shift. The question about the third Babiš government is registered and waits for the votes of late 2026.

In the UN General Assembly, member states vote on resolutions that range from nuclear disarmament to the budget of the UN's agency for Palestine refugees. When a state asks for a recorded vote, every member's vote is kept, and the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library publishes these records1. The EU members coordinate in New York and usually vote alike, so how often a member votes with the rest is a plain measure of where it stands among them. Research on these records more often places states on a scale estimated from all of their votes2; this study keeps to agreement, which can be read vote by vote.

Over the years this stage covers, Czechia had three governments: Bohuslav Sobotka's from January 20143, Andrej Babiš's first and second from December 201745, and Petr Fiala's from December 2021 to {{cab_25}}6, when Andrej Babiš's third government took over with a new foreign minister7. The study asks whether Czechia's agreement with the EU majority, and with the United States, Russia, Hungary and Slovakia, changed between the periods of these governments by more than the other EU members' agreement changed over the same votes. Such a change is an association with a period: a new government comes with a new foreign minister, new officials and a moving world agenda, none of them separated here.

The design was registered before any Czech vote in the data was read. This page reports its first stage: the two earlier changes, in 2017 and in 2021, and the votes on which Czechia left the EU majority. The headline question, about the third Babiš government, is tested on votes cast from {{q1_from}} to {{q1_to}}, after registration, and is still open.

Data

The file is version 5 of the UN's General Assembly Voting Data, published in February 2026, with one row for each member's vote on each resolution adopted by a recorded vote1. From {{sob_from}} to the end of the file on 30 December 2025 it holds {{votes_all}} recorded votes. Paragraph votes, amendments and failed drafts are not in it. A position is yes, no or abstain; a member that did not vote has none. The EU is the 27 members of February 2020 in every period, without the United Kingdom.

The periods follow the cabinets, each appointed with its foreign minister, who instructs the mission in New York. Each later period starts {{buffer}} days after the appointment, because the December plenary adopts texts voted in committee under the old government. The Sobotka period runs from {{sob_from}}, the day the cabinet was appointed and the start of the data used, to {{sob_to}}: {{sob_votes}} votes, on {{sob_split}} of which the other EU members did not all vote alike. The Babiš period runs from {{bab_from}} to {{bab_to}} ({{bab_votes}} votes, {{bab_split}} split), and the Fiala period from {{fia_from}} to {{fia_to}} ({{fia_votes}} votes, {{fia_split}} split). The first Babiš cabinet, appointed on {{cab_17}}, lost its confidence vote and is pooled with the second45; the Fiala cabinet was appointed on {{cab_21}}6. The {{buf17_votes}} and {{buf21_votes}} votes in the two gaps belong to neither period.

How this was done

On each vote, Czechia's agreement with a position is 1 if it voted the same way, 0.5 if one of the two abstained and the other did not, and 0 for yes against no. The EU majority is the most common position of the other EU members that voted, and a tie drops the vote. For a partner such as Russia, the position is that partner's own vote.

The registered estimate, β, is a difference in differences on votes. On each vote, it takes Czechia's agreement minus the mean agreement of the other {{eu_others}} members, each of them scored against the majority of the members other than itself and Czechia; β is the mean of that difference over the later period minus its mean over the earlier one. A β of −1 point means that Czechia's agreement fell by one point more, or rose by one point less, than the other members' over the same votes, so a change in the agenda or in a partner's own votes is not counted as a Czech change. Intervals come from resampling agenda items, the families of resolutions that return every year, within each period ({{draws}} draws).

The same β is computed with each other EU member in Czechia's place. The rank p is the share of all members, Czechia included, whose β divided by its standard error is at least as far from zero as Czechia's. With {{eu_n}} members, a p of {{p_cut}} or less puts Czechia among the two or three members whose change stands out most.

Each β takes the first label that applies. It is lower or higher agreement after the change if β is at least {{minimum}} points from zero, its 95 % interval excludes zero and p is at most {{p_cut}}; no change larger than {{margin}} points if its 90 % interval lies within ±{{margin}} points; underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than {{margin2}} points, so that the previous rule could not have applied; and inconclusive otherwise. The margin was set from a power analysis run on the other members only, before any Czech vote was read: for changes like the two of stage A, the median member's 90 % interval was {{pw_q2_width}} points wide, and nine in ten members moved by less than {{pw_q2_beta}} points relative to the rest.

Checks, partners and the numbers of members

Seven checks were registered with the estimate, listed in the robustness table; two need a word. The confidence-date check starts the later periods {{buffer}} days after the confidence votes of {{conf_17}} and {{conf_21}}, and the US check cuts the Fiala period at {{us_change}}, when the US government changed. Czechia's share of votes it did not cast is reported, because a government that avoids votes would be invisible to β. A cross-check against UNGA-DM, a database of General Assembly decisions compiled independently of the UN file8, tests the positions themselves.

When the partner is Hungary or Slovakia, that member is neither in Czechia's place nor among the comparison members, so its row ranks {{ranked_partner}} members instead of {{ranked_eu}}. Other members are ranked only if they voted on at least {{min_after}} votes of the later period. The partners give twelve labels over the three changes, eight of them in this stage, reported without a correction for multiplicity; none is read on its own.

Votes on which Czechia left the EU majority

The registered list holds every vote on which Czechia's position differed from the majority of the other EU members: {{d_sobotka}} of {{v_sobotka}} votes in the Sobotka period ({{rate_sob}} %), {{d_babis}} of {{v_babis}} in the Babiš period ({{rate_bab}} %) and {{d_fiala}} of {{v_fiala}} in the Fiala period ({{rate_fia}} %). The totals count votes on which Czechia voted and the majority was not tied; unlike β, the counts do not subtract what the other members did.

To read the list, the votes were grouped into four topics after the results had been seen, by words in the agenda item and the title, in this order: nuclear weapons and disarmament; Israel and Palestine, which includes the Golan and UNRWA, the UN agency for Palestine refugees; racism, meaning the follow-up to the Durban conference; and everything else. The table view of fig. 3 gives every vote with its topic. In the Sobotka period, {{t_sobotka_nuclear}} of the {{d_sobotka}} departures were on nuclear weapons and {{t_sobotka_racism}} on the Durban follow-up, and {{tw_sobotka_ip}} on Israel and Palestine. In the Babiš period, {{t_babis_israel_palestine}} were on Israel and Palestine, {{t_babis_nuclear}} on nuclear weapons, {{t_babis_racism}} on the Durban follow-up and {{t_babis_other}} on other topics. In the Fiala period, {{t_fiala_israel_palestine}} of the {{d_fiala}} ({{fia_ip_share}} %) were on Israel and Palestine: the resolutions on Israeli settlements, on Israeli actions in occupied East Jerusalem and the rest of the occupied territory, on the question of Palestine and on UNRWA, which recur every year, and the votes of the emergency special session on the occupied Palestinian territory, among them those on Gaza.

Most departures were half a step away from the majority. In the Fiala period, Czechia abstained where most of the EU voted yes on {{ay_fiala}} votes and voted no where most abstained on {{na_fiala}}. On {{o_fiala}} votes it voted no while most of the other members voted yes, all of them on Israel and Palestine. In the Sobotka period there were {{o_sobotka_w}}, and in the Babiš period {{o_babis}}: {{bab_opp}}.

A long-standing position

The departures on Israel and Palestine are not read here as the policy of one government. Czech governments of different parties have long been among Israel's closest supporters in Europe. Čejka traces this from Czechoslovakia's supply of arms and training to the new Israeli army in 1948–1949 to the presidency of Václav Havel, during which Czechoslovakia and then the Czech Republic “became one of the most prominent supporters of Israel”9. In November 2012, before the data used here begin, the General Assembly made Palestine a non-member observer state by 138 votes to 9 with 41 abstentions10; in Europe, Čejka notes, no state other than Czechia fully supported Israel's position9. In December 2017, in the gap between the Sobotka and Babiš periods, Czechia abstained on the resolution on the status of Jerusalem; its representative said it supported the EU position but did not believe the draft would contribute to the peace process11. A study of the Visegrád countries in 2018 found that their governments had “frequently abstained from voting in the UN General Assembly or even voted against” when the Israeli–Palestinian conflict was reviewed12.

In the Fiala period, after the attack of 7 October 2023, Prime Minister Fiala said in Israel that “the Czech Republic stands by Israel and will stand by it as long as necessary”13. Czechia and Austria were among the ten members that voted against the General Assembly's call for a humanitarian ceasefire of 12 December 202314, and among those, with Hungary, against the demand for a ceasefire in Gaza of 11 December 202416. The ministry's stated reason in December 2024 was that the resolution “fails to unequivocally link a durable ceasefire to the release of all the hostages”17; in June 2025 Czechia abstained on a resolution on Gaza, stating that the text “fails to unequivocally condemn Hamas’ unspeakable atrocities of 7 October”18. The resolution of May 2024 on Palestine's rights in the UN passed by 143 votes to 9 with 25 abstentions15; the data file records Czechia's vote against it.

Counted after the results, on the votes of the Israel and Palestine topic (table), there were {{ipv_sobotka}} such votes in the Sobotka period, {{ipv_babis}} in the Babiš period and {{ipv_fiala}} in the Fiala period, so the rise in departures does not come from more of them. The other members voted yes in {{ipo_sobotka}}, {{ipo_babis}} and {{ipo_fiala}} % of their positions on them, a share that changed little, although their majority was yes on a larger part of the votes in the Fiala period ({{ipm_fiala}} %, against {{ipm_sobotka}} and {{ipm_babis}} % before). Czechia's own positions moved more. It voted yes on {{ip_sobotka_Y}} of the {{ipv_sobotka}} votes in the Sobotka period ({{ipy_sobotka}} %), {{ip_babis_Y}} of {{ipv_babis}} in the Babiš period ({{ipy_babis}} %) and {{ip_fiala_Y}} of {{ipv_fiala}} in the Fiala period ({{ipy_fiala}} %), and no on {{ip_sobotka_N}}, {{ip_babis_N}} and {{ip_fiala_N}}. The move away from yes began in the Babiš period and went further in the Fiala period, under governments of different parties, as new texts on Gaza came to the vote from October 2023; the data cannot say what accounts for it.

Robustness

The checks are not used to relabel an estimate (§7). Under the same rule, applied to their intervals with the primary estimate's rank p, some would carry a different label: {{relabelled}}. None would meet the rule for a shift. The largest moves come from split votes only, the votes on which a member's departures have room to show: β is {{split_a}} points for 2017 and {{split_b}} points for 2021, with 90 % intervals of {{split_a_ci}} and {{split_b_ci}}, from {{split_a_n}} and {{split_b_n}} votes in the later periods. Without the emergency special sessions, the 2021 estimate is {{reg_b}} points. With the United States, the Fiala period cut at {{us_change}} gives {{usa_cut}} points ({{usa_cut_ci}}). The other checks stay within {{chk_dev}} points of the registered estimates.

The third Babiš government, still to be tested

The headline question is registered and not yet answered. Its β compares Czechia's agreement with the EU majority in the Fiala period, {{fia_from}} to {{fia_to}}, with the votes cast from {{q1_from}} to {{q1_to}}, net of the other members' change, with the same frozen code; the partners are compared in the same way. The votes from {{cab_25}} to registration are public, but no Czech position among them has been read; stage B lists them as a separate block, untested. Version 5 of the file, ending in December 2025, came out in February 2026, so results are expected around February 2027; if no version covering December 2026 is out by 30 April 2027, the change log will say so.

The result will take the labels above. With about {{pw_q1_votes_lo}} to {{pw_q1_votes_hi}} votes in one autumn, the power analysis put the median member's 90 % interval at up to {{pw_q1_width}} points, so for a typical member a change of about {{margin}} points or more can be told from ordinary movement and one of a single point cannot. Czechia is not a typical member here. Its standard error in the two stage A changes was {{cz_se_lo}} to {{cz_se_hi}} points, against {{med_se_lo}} to {{med_se_hi}} for the median member, which fits departures clustered in a few agenda items. Scaling the median member's interval by that ratio, a rough projection computed in article.py and not registered, gives a Czech 90 % interval about {{proj_w_lo}} to {{proj_w_hi}} points wide, around the {{margin2}}-point bound beyond which a result is underpowered, so the headline question may well come out underpowered for Czechia.

Lower agreement after the change would need a fall of at least {{minimum}} points relative to the other members, a 95 % interval below zero and a rank p of {{p_cut}} or less; Czech agreement in the Fiala period, {{cz_fia}} %, leaves only {{headroom}} points for higher agreement.

The author stated her expectation on 15 October 2026, before any Czech vote was read: “I expect a greater lean towards 'Trumpist' policy, and perhaps a greater lean towards Hungary, Russia and Slovakia, though with the Russian invasion of Ukraine in mind” (in Czech: „očekávám větší náklon k 'trumpistické' politice a možná větší náklon Maďarsko, Rusko, Slovensko, ale s ohledem na ruskou invazi na Ukrajinu“). It concerns the partners in the third Babiš period, part of stage B; she stated no expectation for the EU majority or the earlier changes, so nothing on this page tests it.

What this does not show

It does not show what a government did. A UN vote is cast by the mission on instructions, and this design cannot say who decided a vote, or whether a change came from the government, the ministry, the officials or the agenda. Each period mixes changes of prime minister, coalition and foreign minister. The foreign minister was Lubomír Zaorálek, nominated by ČSSD, through the Sobotka period19. In the Babiš period it was Martin Stropnický from December 2017 to June 20184; Jan Hamáček, chair of ČSSD, ran the ministry from June to October 2018 and again in April 202120; Tomáš Petříček was minister from October 2018 to April 2021 and Jakub Kulhánek from April 20215. In the Fiala period it was Jan Lipavský6, and in the third Babiš government it is Petr Macinka, chair of Motoristé sobě7.

Positions on failed drafts, amendments and single paragraphs are not seen. Agreement sits near its ceiling, so a handful of votes moves the estimate; the 2021 estimate rests largely on the emergency special sessions. The partners change too: the United States in January 2025, Slovakia in October 2023, and Hungary held a parliamentary election in April 2026, so the partner estimates mix Czech changes with theirs. The headline question rests on one autumn's votes, about {{pw_q1_votes_lo}} to {{pw_q1_votes_hi}}. The grouping of departures by topic and the count of positions on Israel and Palestine were made after the results and are reading aids, not tests.
