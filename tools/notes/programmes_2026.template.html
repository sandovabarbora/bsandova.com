What Prague's 2026 programmes can do in one term

Six programmes for the October 2026 Prague city election, read against one rule: what the City of Prague can do within four years under its own powers.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Every programme promises far more housing than Prague can build in four years; the ones that promise starts are closer to what is possible.
  • No large transport project in any programme can be finished in one term; most programmes say they will prepare them.
  • Almost every programme promises rules for Airbnb or a higher tourist fee, which the state decides.
  • This rates feasibility only, not whether the goals are good, and it is not a voting recommendation.

How the programmes were read

The City of Prague is a municipality and a region in one{{ref:L131}}. It decides its own budget and property, including city flats and land; it issues its own building regulations, including parking minimums; it orders public transport and sets fares{{ref:L194}}; it runs secondary schools{{ref:L561}} and the municipal police{{ref:L553}}. The state decides by law on rent and short-term rentals{{ref:U104}}, sets the ceiling of the tourist fee, which is 50 Kč a person a night{{ref:L565}}, builds the motorway ring{{ref:L13}} and the railways{{ref:L77}}, and runs the university hospitals{{ref:L372}}. A municipality may set a low-emission zone, effective at least twelve months after it is announced{{ref:L201}}, and a local coefficient on property tax by area or type of property, but not on empty flats{{ref:L338}}. The city districts run kindergartens and primary schools and manage many city flats.

Each programme was read in full, and its most concrete and consequential promises were rated against those powers, against the scale of the city budget and against the time building takes: yes, likely, partly (with the part that can be done), not in one term, or decided by the state or another body. One fixed reference sets the housing scale: Prague completes about 6 000 homes a year, 4.5 per 1 000 residents on the 2015–2024 mean, and the time from permit to completion there is about 11 months longer than in the rest of Czechia{{ref:P4}}. Factual claims in the programmes, such as the money in the city's accounts or counts of flats, were not checked; each section lists them. The ratings were drafted with the help of a language model under one fixed brief for all six programmes, and the legal points they rest on were checked against the statutes cited. Rows were chosen per programme, so the counts in the header describe this reading and do not rank the lists.

What the programmes share

Of the {{N}} promises read, {{DOABLE}} are within the city's powers and can be done in one term, {{PARTLY}} can be done only in part, {{NO}} cannot be done in one term, and {{OTHER}} are decided by the state or another body.

Housing is the main topic of every programme and its weakest point. Promises of 10 000 flats a year cannot be met when about 6 000 are completed now, most of them by private builders, and even 8 000 city flats would be more than the whole market completes in a year. Promises worded as starts, 2 000 city flats or 1 000 a year, are closer to what a term allows. Large transport projects, metro D (its first section is planned for 2032{{ref:PIR}}), a ring metro line, the city ring road, tunnels and new bridges, cannot be finished in four years in any programme, and most programmes say they will prepare them. Rules for Airbnb and a higher tourist fee appear in almost every programme, but the city has no legal power to restrict short-term rentals, and the fee's ceiling is set by law. The steps most clearly in the city's own hands are its building regulations, its parking and fares, the rules for allocating city flats and its own budget rules.
