What changes for Czech TV and radio without the licence fee?

ČT and ČRo, Czech public television and radio, are paid mainly from licence fees: 150 CZK a month for a household with a TV, 55 CZK with a radio, unless exempt[[act348]].

A government bill, Sněmovní tisk 231, would replace them with a fixed contribution from the state budget[[bill]]. Would the broadcasters get more or less, and who would carry the cost?

The bill would end the fee for every household; whether that lightens or adds to the load depends on who pays the taxes that replace it.

Its stage was checked on 3 November 2026: the bill as introduced sits in committee until at least 24 December 2026[[bill]], and press reports expect a start during 2027 or from January 2028[[press]].

The money for ČT and ČRo

Current law raises the fees by 6 % each time inflation adds up to 6 %; the bill raises its 2027 amount only after 10 %. At the forecast, the fees would rise on 1 July 2028 and the bill's amount not at all by 2030.

The gap is about {{x_share}} of what current law would give over the four years. Payers held fixed means the number of fee payers stays at today's; if it fell, current law would bring in less and the gap would shrink.

The broadcasters' 2026 budgets expect {{b_today}} from the fees; the bill gives {{bill27}} for 2027[[bill]].

The bill's amounts, {{bill_ct_bn}} for ČT and {{bill_cro_bn}} for ČRo, equal the broadcasters' fee income of 2024, before the fee rise of 1 May 2025, its explanatory report says[[bill]].

By the rules set before the results, the bill gives less money in all 12 combinations of inflation, clause reading and starting point tried.

Who would pay

Today a household is exempt if its net income is below 2.15 times its living minimum and it declares this; some people with disabilities and some organisations are exempt too[[act348]].

If nobody were exempt, the fee would average {{pay_e0}} a year per household: {{b0_d1}} of a poorest-tenth household's income, {{b0_d10}} of a richest-tenth one's.

From the average mix of taxes, a household in the poorest tenth would pay {{pa_d1}} a year, one in the richest {{pa_d10}}.

Against income, today's fee weighs up to {{y_hi}} times as heavily on the poorest tenth as on the richest; budget funding from the average mix of taxes, {{z}} times. "Up to", because exemptions are counted by entitlement: if all of the poorest tenth were exempt, they would pay nothing today.

The tax mix matters because VAT takes more from households that spend most of what they have, as poorer ones do, and income tax takes more from higher earners. So VAT alone would give {{z_vat}} and income tax alone {{z_pit}}.

Firms and public bodies pay about {{f_today}} a year today, what is left of fee revenue after households; after, about {{f_after}} would fall on them through company tax, and the two figures are not like for like.

By the rules set before the results, under budget funding the poorest tenth would carry a higher share of its income than the richest; against today, it depends on how many poor households are exempt.

What this does not show

  • Published averages for groups of households, not household records; exemptions counted by entitlement.
  • VAT and excise fall wholly on households, company tax on firms; the state could also borrow or cut spending.
  • Inflation is the ČNB summer 2026 forecast; the study is run again if the autumn report is out by 30 November 2026.
  • Only the bill as introduced; nothing on the broadcasters' independence or quality.

How this was done

The rules and labels were registered with signed tags before any result was computed[[cpi]].

The author has said publicly that she favours keeping the licence fee and raising it; the study was registered before any outcome table was opened so that this view could not shape the result, and the design states which way each simplification pushes the result relative to it.

Y (today) and Z (after) divide the poorest tenth's payment as a share of its income by the richest tenth's. A ratio above 1.1 means the poorest tenth carries a higher share of its income than the richest, below 0.9 a lower share, a "similar" band in between; a ratio divided by 0 (Y = 0) counts as above the band. There are no intervals: the inputs are published aggregates, and the uncertainty is the registered range, which is not a probability.

The money in full: rules, the 12 combinations and the paths by year

Three paths for 2027–2030 are compared for ČT and ČRo together. Under current law the monthly fees rise by 6 % on 1 July of the year after the one in which the sum of annual inflation since the last increase exceeds 6 %. The second path is the bill with its clause: the 2027 amounts are carried forward, and rise by 10 % from the year after cumulative inflation since 2026 exceeds 10 percentage points. The third path is the bill with no rise at all.

The clause's "cumulative" inflation has two readings, the sum of the annual rates and the compounded rate, and both are in the range. Inflation is the ČNB summer 2026 forecast ({{inf25}} % in 2025, {{inf26}} % in 2026 and {{inf27}} % in 2027), with 2.0 % for the years it does not cover. The headline X is current law minus the bill with its clause, summed over 2027–2030 in 2026 prices. The 12 combinations are: inflation one percentage point lower, as forecast and one percentage point higher; the two readings; and two starting points, the 2026 budgets and the 2025 outturn of fee revenue indexed by the legal fee path. With the 2025 outturn as the starting point and the forecast inflation, the bill gives {{x_b}} less in 2026 prices, payers held fixed.

At the forecast the bill's clause does not trigger by 2030, so the bill with and without its clause give the same money. Across the range the clause triggers only when inflation is one percentage point higher than forecast: from 2030 under the sum reading and from 2029 under the compounded reading. In those four combinations current law steps twice, on 1 July 2028 and 1 July 2030, and the bill still gives less. With inflation one percentage point lower, current law steps once, on 1 July 2029, and the bill's clause does not trigger.

Who pays in full: payer shares, exemptions, taxes, firms, seniors and the table by decile

Today's payment is the fee times the share of households registered as payers. Those shares are {{pi_tv}} for TV and {{pi_r}} for radio, from {{payers_tv}} and {{payers_r}} household payers in the 2025 annual reports against {{households}} households. The law exempts households whose net income is below 2.15 times their living minimum, but only after they declare it, so how many are exempt in fact is unknown. Two bounds are used, both counting exemptions by entitlement. In the no-exemption case nobody is exempt. In the exempt-decile case a whole decile is exempt if its mean income is below the threshold, and decile 1 if no decile is; in this run only decile 1 was exempt, and its payers are spread over the other nine deciles.

After, the bill's total is split across taxes in proportion to the state budget's own 2025 tax revenue: VAT {{vat}}, excise duties {{excise}}, personal income tax {{pit}} and corporate income tax {{cit}}. VAT and excise are given to households by their spending on each category, from ČSÚ's 2025 spending survey by quintile; personal income tax by the income tax each decile pays; and corporate income tax to firms. Social insurance is left out because it is earmarked for pensions and health care.

Counting exemptions by entitlement, not by actual take-up, the poorest tenth carries between {{y_lo}} and {{y_hi}} times the share of its income that the richest tenth does today; the low end is crude, because it treats all of the poorest tenth as exempt, so it pays nothing.

Funded from the state budget at the average tax structure it would be {{z}} times; from VAT alone it would be {{z_vat}} times, and from personal income tax alone {{z_pit}} times. That average-structure figure rests on four assumptions: VAT and excise fall wholly on households, corporate income tax stays with firms, social insurance is left out, and both deciles of a spending quintile spend the same; the checks below change the last three of them. If the amount is borrowed, current households carry nothing now.

At the average tax structure the poorest tenth would carry a higher share of its income than the richest, {{ba_d1}} against {{ba_d10}}. The burden falls at every step but one, from decile 6 ({{ba_d6}}) to decile 7 ({{ba_d7}}), where it rises a little. With the poorest tenth exempt, deciles 2 to 10 pay {{pay_e1}} a year on average.

Against the fee with nobody exempt, budget funding is less regressive: Z is {{z_over_y}} times Y. Against the fee with the whole poorest tenth exempt, the poorest tenth pays nothing today and something after. The true exempt share lies between the two bounds, and the data cannot place it.

Firms

Firms appear only as one aggregate. Firms and public bodies pay {{f_today}} a year today, which is what remains of the budgeted fee revenue after households, public bodies included. The fee schedule of §5 of the act applied to the registered employers gives {{f_sched}}, so the remainder is {{f_ratio}} times that. The two may differ: not every registered firm is registered for the fee, the remainder includes public bodies (§3(4)) and rental-vehicle fees, and the register counts registered units, not full-time equivalents.

After, firms and public bodies would carry {{f_after}}, the corporate-income-tax share of the total, against {{f_today}} today, and the two figures are not like for like.

Seniors

Seniors appear only as a description, for the two household types in which ČSÚ's tables identify them. One-person households aged 65 or more ({{s1_hh}}, mean net income {{s1_inc}} a year) pay on average {{s1_b0}} of their income with nobody exempt and {{s1_b1}} with the poorest tenth exempt. Two-adult households with at least one person aged 65 or more ({{s2_hh}}, {{s2_inc}}) pay {{s2_b0}} and {{s2_b1}}.

Neither group's mean income is below the exemption threshold, so no senior group is exempt by the decile rule, although individual seniors in them may be entitled to it. The figures cover only these two household types, today only. A press claim that seniors pay about 40 % of fee revenue has no traceable source and is not tested.

All registered checks and the direction of each simplification

Two checks guard the arithmetic. Household payments plus the firm remainder equal the budgeted fee revenue, {{hh_rev}} plus {{f_today}} making {{b_today}}, by construction, so that identity only guards the code. The bill's total is allocated to households and firms to the crown, 7 807 592 000 CZK, in all {{n_alloc}} allocations that carry a number, after rounding by largest remainder.

The registered checks rerun the analysis with one thing changed, and each keeps the primary labels but one: the check with no exemption for any decile, the upper end of Y, {{y_hi}}. By the same rules its labels differ from the primary ones: today, "{{lbl_9d_today}}"; and for the change, "{{lbl_9d_change}}". The design does not let a check change a label, so both are reported without choosing between them.

Starting current law's inflation window in 2026 instead of 2025, the bill gives {{c1_x}} less in 2026 prices with payers held fixed, still "bill lower", with a range down to {{c1_min}} bn CZK. On the household side, a uniform VAT rate gives Z of {{c3u}}, other taxes allocated like VAT {{c3o}}, and income tax allocated by employment income {{c4w}} or by net income {{c4n}}. Counting every household as a payer leaves Y unchanged.

The design also expected a direction for three simplifications, and the checks show which way each went. Including social insurance in the pool lowers Z to {{c9a}}, so leaving it out, as the primary does, gives the higher Z; the design had expected the opposite, while saying the sign was not known. Putting half of corporate income tax on households lowers Z to {{c9b}}, as the design expected. Spreading spending within quintiles by interpolation, which the design expected to lower Z, raises it slightly, to {{c9c}} against {{z}}. All three keep the label "higher".

A mean of household-level ratios needs household records, and the tables give only decile and quintile means. Applying the 2026–2027 tax-sharing percentages needs gross national lines for every tax, and the closing account has none for personal income tax.

Rules, deviations and exposures

Rules

Every ceiling, rounding and allocation is done in integers or exact fractions. The current-law rule restarts its window at the year of an increase and the bill's clause at the year of the crossing, as the two texts say; a second trigger within 2027–2030 is computed. The household deciles come from ČSÚ's income-survey table that ranks households by net money income per modified OECD consumption unit; total household income is income per consumption unit times consumption units per household. The living minimum is that in force on 1 January 2026 (4 860 CZK a month for a single person). The state-budget tax lines of the 2025 closing account are already net of the shares of regions and municipalities and are used as they stand.

Deviations and exposures, told in full in the change log

The firm "range" of the design collapsed to a single value, because the register's size bands match the fee schedule, so a plausibility test would always fail; before any value was seen, firms were made descriptive, the remainder against the schedule-implied amount. Checks 6 and 7 were found not computable before the code tag. The snapshot tag fees-data-step sits on the snapshot commit, which holds no code, so the parsers are frozen only by the code tag. At the snapshot step a web search used to find ČRo's 2025 annual report returned a summary stating ČRo's realised 2025 fee revenue, {{cro25}}; it is an input of the 2025-outturn starting point, which sets the low end of the Q-A range, and no rule or choice depended on it. Masked prints of some source lines showed the number of digits of a few figures to the drafting assistant, and the reviewer of the code saw the digit-group shape of some rows of the state closing account; both are recorded in the design, before the code tag, and none was used to choose a rule. The first run at the tag fees-code stopped before computing any result, because rounding in the published spending table made some residuals slightly negative; the guard now allows that rounding, nothing else changed, and the run of record uses the new tag fees-code-2. The rise of the living minimum to 5 500 CZK from 1 October 2026 is not used, as the pinned rule says. The expected delay of the bill's start was recorded before any result was computed.
