Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • In every one of the 12 registered combinations of inflation, of the reading of the bill's inflation clause and of the 2026 starting point, the bill gives less than current law, between {{x_min}} and {{x_max}} bn CZK over four years in 2026 prices. At the forecast inflation the fee rises once, on 1 July 2028, and the bill's contribution never rises by 2030.
  • Who carries more depends on who is exempt today. If nobody is exempt, the fee takes {{y_hi}} times as large a share of income from the poorest tenth as from the richest, more than budget funding at the average tax structure ({{z}}). If every household of the poorest tenth is counted as exempt, the crude upper bound of entitlement, it pays nothing today and budget funding weighs more on it.
  • If the money came from VAT alone, the ratio would be {{z_vat}}, close to a fee with no exemptions; from income tax alone it would be {{z_pit}}.
  • The study is arithmetic on published averages, not on household records, and it compares the bill as introduced, which press reports expect to start later than 1 January 2027. It says nothing about the independence, quality or reach of the broadcasters.

Česká televize (ČT) and Český rozhlas (ČRo) are paid mainly from licence fees. Every household with a TV set pays 150 CZK a month and every household with a radio 55 CZK, and firms pay by number of employees[[act348]]. A government bill, Sněmovní tisk 231, would repeal the fees and pay both broadcasters a contribution from the state budget instead: 5 741 826 000 CZK for ČT and 2 065 766 000 CZK for ČRo in 2027, 7 807 592 000 CZK together[[bill]].

The bill raises two questions of arithmetic. How much would the broadcasters get under it, compared with what current law would give them? And who would carry the cost, households of different incomes and firms, compared with who carries it now? This study answers both from published figures, under rules fixed before any outcome table was opened.

The bill's stage was checked on 3 November 2026. It passed its first reading on 24 October 2026 and went to committees. On 30 October the Committee on Media and Culture interrupted its deliberation, and further deliberation is possible from 24 December 2026[[bill]]. Press reports of 1 November 2026 expect it to take effect during 2027 or from January 2028, not on 1 January 2027 as introduced[[press]]. Everything below is conditional: it describes the bill as introduced, over 2027–2030, and would change if the bill is amended.

How this was done

For the money (Q-A), three paths for 2027–2030 are compared for ČT and ČRo together. Current law starts from the fee-revenue line of each broadcaster's 2026 budget, {{ct26}} for ČT and {{cro26}} for ČRo[[ctbudget]][[crobudget]], and then moves only by the law's own rule: the monthly fees rise by 6 % on 1 July of the year after the one in which the sum of annual inflation since the last increase exceeds 6 %. The number of payers is held fixed, which favours current law, because a fall in payers would lower that path. The second path is the bill with its clause: the 2027 amounts are carried forward, and rise by 10 % from the year after cumulative inflation since 2026 exceeds 10 percentage points. The third path is the bill with no rise at all.

The clause's "cumulative" inflation has two readings, the sum of the annual rates and the compounded rate, and both are in the range. Inflation is the ČNB summer 2026 forecast, with 2.0 % for the years it does not cover, and every amount is deflated to 2026 prices[[cnb]][[cpi]]. The headline X is current law minus the bill with its clause, summed over 2027–2030 in 2026 prices. The range runs over 12 combinations: inflation one point lower, as forecast and one point higher; the two readings; and two starting points, the 2026 budgets (A) and 2025 realised fee revenue indexed by the legal fee path (B).

For who pays (Q-B), the unit is the household, in the ten income deciles of ČSÚ's 2025 income survey (SILC), whose income refers to 2024[[silc]]. The burden of a decile is its average payment divided by its mean net household income. The summary measure is the burden of the poorest tenth divided by the burden of the richest tenth: Y today, Z after.

Today's payment is the fee times the share of households registered as payers. Those shares are {{pi_tv}} for TV and {{pi_r}} for radio, from {{payers_tv}} and {{payers_r}} household payers in the 2025 annual reports against {{households}} households, an average of {{pay_e0}} a year per household. The law exempts households whose net income is below 2.15 times their living minimum, but only after they declare it, so how many are exempt in fact is unknown. Two bounds are used. E0 exempts nobody. E1 exempts a whole decile if its mean income is below the threshold, and decile 1 if no decile is; in this run only decile 1 was exempt, and its payers are spread over the other nine deciles, at {{pay_e1}} each.

After, the bill's total is split across taxes in proportion to the state budget's own 2025 tax revenue[[szu]]: VAT {{vat}}, excise duties {{excise}}, personal income tax {{pit}} and corporate income tax {{cit}}. VAT and excise are given to households by their spending on each category, from ČSÚ's 2025 spending survey by quintile[[spending]]; personal income tax by the income tax each decile pays; and corporate income tax to firms. Social insurance is left out because it is earmarked for pensions and health care. Two other ways of raising the money, VAT alone and income tax alone, are reported but do not enter the label, and borrowing would put nothing on current households now.

The money for ČT and ČRo

If the bill passes as introduced, ČT and ČRo would receive {{x}} less (2026 prices) over 2027–2030 than under current law. Under current law the two would get {{p1_real}} over the four years in 2026 prices and under the bill {{p2_real}}. The label is bill lower: in all 12 registered combinations the difference is between {{x_min}} and {{x_max}} bn CZK in 2026 prices, in the bill's disfavour, so the sign does not depend on inflation, on the reading of the clause or on the starting point. With the 2025 outturn as the starting point (baseline B) and the forecast inflation, it is {{x_b}}.

The two inflation rules explain the gap over time. At the forecast inflation ({{inf25}} % in 2025, {{inf26}} % in 2026 and {{inf27}} % in 2027), current law's sum passes 6 % in 2027, at {{law_sum}} points, so the fees would rise on 1 July 2028 to 159 and 59 CZK a month. The bill's clause counts from 2026, and by 2029 its sum reaches {{bill_sum}} points, short of the 10 points it needs under either reading, so the contribution stays at the 2027 amount to 2030 and the bill with and without its clause give the same money.

Across the range the clause triggers only when inflation is one point higher than forecast: from 2030 under the sum reading and from 2029 under the compounded reading. In those four combinations current law steps twice, on 1 July 2028 and 1 July 2030, and the bill still gives less. With inflation one point lower, current law steps once, on 1 July 2029, and the bill's clause does not trigger.

Who pays today and who would pay after

The fee is a flat amount per household, so its share of income falls as income rises. With nobody exempt (E0), it takes {{b0_d1}} of the poorest tenth's mean income and {{b0_d10}} of the richest tenth's, and it falls at every step from one decile to the next. Today the poorest tenth of households carries between {{y_lo}} and {{y_hi}} times the share of its income that the richest tenth does (the range is the two exemption bounds; exemptions are counted by entitlement, not by actual take-up, and the lower bound of {{y_lo}} is crude because E1 treats every household of the poorest tenth as exempt, so it pays nothing). The label is depends on the assumption, because one bound lies above the "similar" band and the other below it.

Funded from the state budget at the average tax structure it would be {{z}} times; if the money came from VAT alone it would be {{z_vat}} times, and from personal income tax alone {{z_pit}} times. If the amount is borrowed, current households carry nothing now. The label after is higher: at the average tax structure the poorest tenth would carry a higher share of its income than the richest, {{ba_d1}} against {{ba_d10}}, or {{pa_d1}} and {{pa_d10}} a year per household. The burden falls with income at every step but one, from decile 6 ({{ba_d6}}) to decile 7 ({{ba_d7}}), where it rises a little (fig. 1).

Whether budget funding is more or less regressive than the fee, measured by this ratio of the poorest tenth's share to the richest tenth's, therefore depends on the exemption assumption, and the change is labelled depends on the assumption. Against the fee with nobody exempt, budget funding is less regressive: Z is {{z_over_y}} times Y. Against the fee with the whole poorest tenth exempt, the poorest tenth pays nothing today and something after. The true exempt share lies between the two bounds, and the data cannot place it.

Two parts of the result point in different directions for the fee. A fee with no exemptions is more regressive than funding from the average mix of taxes; yet the bill gives the broadcasters less money in every combination, and funding from VAT alone, at {{z_vat}}, would be nearly as regressive as a fee with no exemptions, while funding from income tax alone would take a far smaller share of income from the poorest tenth than from the richest ({{z_pit}} times).

How far to trust it

Two checks guard the arithmetic. Household payments plus the firm remainder equal the budgeted fee revenue, {{hh_rev}} plus {{f_today}} making {{b_today}}, by construction, so that identity only guards the code. The bill's total is allocated to households and firms to the crown, 7 807 592 000 CZK, in all {{n_alloc}} allocations that carry a number, after rounding by largest remainder.

The registered checks rerun the analysis with one thing changed. Starting current law's inflation window in 2026 instead of 2025 gives {{c1_x}}, still bill lower, with a range down to {{c1_min}} bn CZK. On the household side, a uniform VAT rate gives Z of {{c3u}}, other taxes allocated like VAT {{c3o}}, and income tax allocated by employment income {{c4w}} or by net income {{c4n}}. Counting every household as a payer leaves Y unchanged. All keep the primary labels.

Four checks push the other way from the main simplifications. Including social insurance in the pool gives Z of {{c9a}}, putting half of corporate income tax on households {{c9b}}, and spreading spending within quintiles by interpolation {{c9c}}; all three keep the label higher. The fourth, no exemption for any decile, is the upper end of Y, {{y_hi}}, and by the same rules its labels differ from the primary ones: today, {{lbl_9d_today}}; and for the change, {{lbl_9d_change}}. The design does not let a check change a label, so both are reported without choosing between them.

Two checks could not be computed. A mean of household-level ratios needs household records, and the tables give only decile and quintile means. Applying the 2026–2027 tax-sharing percentages needs gross national lines for every tax, and the closing account has none for personal income tax.

What this means

For anyone weighing the bill, the measured quantity for the broadcasters is X, {{x}} less over 2027–2030 in 2026 prices, with a range of {{x_min}} to {{x_max}} bn CZK across the registered inputs. That holds only for the bill as introduced, starting in 2027, at the ČNB summer forecast and with the number of payers held fixed.

For households, the measured quantities are two ratios. Funded from the average mix of state taxes, the poorest tenth would carry {{z}} times the share of its income that the richest tenth carries. Today that ratio lies between {{y_lo}} and {{y_hi}}, depending on how many poor households are exempt, so the data do not say whether the switch would lighten or add to the load on the poorest tenth. The mix of taxes also matters: VAT alone gives {{z_vat}}, income tax alone {{z_pit}}.

The study does not say what the broadcasters would do with the money, or whether the change would affect their independence. Those questions are outside it.

What this does not show

The deciles are modelled from published means, not from household records, which are not available. Exemptions are counted by entitlement, so the true exempt share is lower than under E1. Spending is published by quintile, so both deciles of a quintile are given the same spending, which makes consumption taxes look more regressive. The SILC income is that of 2024, the taxes of 2025, the fee of 2026 and the bill's amount of 2027, and nothing is re-based between them.

Tax incidence follows textbook assumptions. VAT and excise are put entirely on households, an upper bound, since firms, the state and tourists pay some of them; corporate income tax stays with firms and is not passed on to prices or wages; and social insurance is excluded, with a check that includes it. A contribution from the budget is also not a tax increase: the government may pay it from a different mix of taxes, from debt or by cutting other spending.

Inflation is the ČNB summer 2026 forecast, because the autumn report was not out at the snapshot. The design's cutoff is 30 November 2026: if the autumn report is published by then, it replaces the summer one and the study is run again, and this page will be updated.

The bill may change in committee, in its amounts, its clause or its start, and press reports already expect a later start[[press]]. The comparison keeps the window 2027–2030 as introduced and does not model a delay. Out of scope are the independence, quality and reach of the broadcasters, the coalition's May 2026 proposal of further exemptions for people aged 75 or more, holders of ZTP cards and small firms, and the European Commission's possible review of the contribution as state aid.

Method, details and reproduction

Method and disclosures

Author's position

The author has said publicly that she favours keeping the licence fee and raising it; the study was registered before any outcome table was opened so that this view could not shape the result, and the design states which way each simplification pushes the result relative to it.

Rules

Every ceiling, rounding and allocation is done in integers or exact fractions. The current-law rule restarts its window at the year of an increase and the bill's clause at the year of the crossing, as the two texts say; a second trigger within 2027–2030 is computed. The household deciles come from SILC Tab. 3a, ranked by net money income per modified OECD consumption unit; total household income is income per consumption unit times consumption units per household. The living minimum is that in force on 1 January 2026 (4 860 CZK a month for a single person). The state-budget tax lines of the 2025 closing account are already net of the shares of regions and municipalities and are used as they stand.

Deviations, told in full in the change log

The firm "range" of the design collapsed to a single value, because the register's size bands match the fee schedule, so a plausibility test would always fail; before any value was seen, firms were made descriptive, the remainder against the schedule-implied amount. Checks 6 and 7 were found not computable before the code tag. The snapshot tag fees-data-step sits on the snapshot commit, which holds no code, so the parsers are frozen only by the code tag. Masked prints of some source lines showed the number of digits of a few figures, recorded in the design, before the code tag. The first run at the tag fees-code stopped before computing any result, because rounding in the published spending table made some residuals slightly negative; the guard now allows that rounding, nothing else changed, and the run of record uses the new tag fees-code-2. The rise of the living minimum to 5 500 CZK from 1 October 2026 is not used, as the pinned rule says. The expected delay of the bill's start was recorded before any result was computed.
