How often do two 22s come at once? Tram bunching in Prague, 2025

Anyone who has waited ten minutes for a tram and then watched two of the same line arrive together knows bunching. The usual account of it comes from buses: a vehicle that falls a little behind finds more people waiting at each stop, takes longer to load them and falls further behind, while the one following finds fewer and catches up, until the two travel together78. On that account the spacing between two vehicles is unstable: a small deviation grows by itself. Part 1 of this series found where Prague's trams record their delay. This part asks what happens to the spacing between two trams of the same line as they travel, and whether it breaks down by itself.

The question

A pair here is two trams of the same line and route that follow each other, scheduled between four and fifteen minutes apart, the frequent service people use without a timetable. Their spacing at a stop is the gap between their arrivals divided by the gap the timetable plans there, so 1 means on plan, 0.5 means half the planned gap, and anything under 0.25 counts as bunched. The pair is followed from the first stop where the two trams are consecutive to the end of the line, or until a third tram of the line comes between them. Convoys of three are therefore not analysed, and line 22 is not singled out; the two 22s of the title stand for any such pair.

Three questions were written down before any spacing was computed. First, amplification: does a pair that is off plan at one stop move further off at the next? Second, places: do sudden closings, a pair going from at least half the planned gap to under a quarter of it in one stop, happen more often at some platforms than their traffic alone would give, and are those platforms the same in odd and even weeks? Third, whether the closings follow the segments where part 1 found the delay gain most spread out. Each question has its own rule. A question is "supported" if its 95 % interval lies wholly on the predicted side of its line. For amplification it is "not supported" if the 90 % interval lies wholly within ±0.01 per stop, but an interval wholly below zero is listed as "inconclusive"; for the places, their stability and the link to part 1, if the interval lies wholly on the other side of its line or, for the link, wholly within ±0.10. Anything else is "inconclusive".

Data

The trams are those of the author's thesis: Prague Integrated Transport's stop-time history from Golemio2, 15 March to 8 September 20251, with the screening of the earlier studies for planned closures and gaps in the feed. A trip counts if it runs its line's usual sequence of stops with a row at every one of them, which {{modal_lo}}–{{modal_hi}} % of trips do each month. The other {{drop_lo}}–{{drop_hi}} % are dropped: short turns, diversions and any trip with a missing row. Those are plausibly the more disturbed trips, so the shares of bunching below are a floor. The first and last stop of each line are left out too, because the timetable sets the departure there. Route variants, a line in one direction with its end stops, with fewer than 1 000 pairs over the season, {{thin}} of them, mostly rare variants, are left out, which leaves {{patterns}} variants and {{pairs}} pairs. The arrivals of a pair share an arrival second in 0.0 % of stops, and they look measured rather than copied from the timetable: only a fraction of a percent equal it to the second.

Amplification from stop to stop

The measure of amplification is how much a pair's spacing changes from one stop to the next per unit it is off plan at the first. Measured directly, noise in the arrival times pulls it below zero by itself: an arrival recorded a few seconds late makes the spacing look off at one stop and corrected at the next. The design first proposed to remove that by comparing with shuffled data, but tests on made-up data before the results showed that the shuffle also removes the noise it was meant to keep, so the registered estimate was changed, before any spacing was read, to an instrumental-variable one in the spirit of Anderson and Hsiao (1981): the spacing two stops back stands in for the spacing one stop back, and shares none of its recording noise if that noise is not carried from stop to stop4. The data support this: the autocorrelations of the changes at two and three stops are {{ac2}} and {{ac3}}. The first-stage coefficient of the spacing one stop back on the spacing two stops back is {{fs}} (t = {{fs_t}}), so the instrument is strong.

Over {{transitions_m}} million steps from one stop to the next, the amplification is {{g}} per stop (95 % CI {{g_lo}} to {{g_hi}}). The interval lies wholly below zero, which the design lists under "inconclusive", as a case where correction outweighs amplification; the 90 % interval, {{g90_lo}} to {{g90_hi}}, also lies wholly within the margin of ±0.01 per stop set for "not supported". The clause for an interval below zero is the specific one and governs, so the prediction of amplification is {{g_label}}. Version 1 of this page said "{{g_label_v1}}", because the code checked the margin first and so applied the registered rule in the wrong order. As a size, the pooled value is practically zero: every pooled specification, intervals included, lies within ±{{g_max}} per stop, and the sign flips without the timing-point stops ({{g_tp}}) or on departures ({{g_dep}}). With the spacing three or four stops back as the instrument the estimate is {{g3}} and {{g4}}; resampled by week its interval is {{gw_lo}} to {{gw_hi}}. Measured plainly, without the instrument, it would be {{g_ols}}, and the design's original shuffled comparison {{g_reg}}: both further from zero mainly because of the recording noise (fig. 1).

The small negative value is not uniform. Without the {{tp}} stops where trams stand much longer than the network's usual half-minute, or are planned with a pause of a minute or more, the estimate is {{g_tp}} ({{g_tp_lo}} to {{g_tp_hi}}), wholly above zero: about a tenth of the registered margin at most. Variants do not relabel the primary result, but this one would read as "supported" had it been primary. Dropping those stops also drops every step whose chain touches them. Using departures instead of arrivals, where both trams' departures are recorded (about {{dep_share}} % of stops), it is {{g_dep}}. A reading consistent with these, formed after the results: that trams are held at those stops, which pulls the spacing back, and that between them it drifts with a very slight tendency to grow. That trams are held there is an inference from dwell, not a documented fact. Estimated line by line after publication, the amplification ranges from {{gl_min}} (line {{gl_min_line}}) to {{gl_max}} (line {{gl_max_line}}) across the {{gl_n}} lines with at least 50 000 instrumented steps, a cut-off chosen after seeing the numbers, and from {{gla_min}} (line {{gla_min_line}}) to {{gla_max}} (line {{gla_max_line}}) with the two smaller lines included.

Bunches build slowly, towards the end

Trams leave the terminus close to plan, and the spacing spreads as they go: its variance grows from {{var1}} in the first tenth of a line to {{var10}} in the last, and the share of pair passes arriving bunched or swapped grows with it, from {{d1}} % near the start to {{d10}} % near the end (fig. 2). The registered benchmark for this curve shuffles each tram's stop-to-stop changes between pairs of the same route, hour and day, which keeps the size of the changes and removes any link between them. Near zero it is not a valid benchmark: shuffled pairs swap order in {{s10swap}} % of passes in the last tenth, which rails do not allow ({{o10swap}} % observed). Compared as registered, without swaps, the observed last tenth is {{d10u}} % bunched against {{s10u}} % shuffled, although the observed spacing varies less ({{var10}} against {{var10s}}); with swaps pooled, a choice made after the results, the two are close ({{d10}} % and {{s10}} %). The comparison therefore cannot say whether bunches build faster or slower than independent drift would build them. Over the whole line, {{bunched}} % of pair passes arrive under a quarter of the planned gap apart and {{gap}} % more than 1.75 times it; with looser cut-offs of half and one and a half times the planned gap, conventions of headway-reliability work not checked against the exact tables of the Transit Capacity and Quality of Service Manual, {{b05}} % and {{g15}} %. That manual measures headway adherence relative to the scheduled headway9.

Most bunches form slowly. Of the {{ever}} pairs that are bunched at some stop, {{ever_pct}} % of all pairs, {{gradual_pct}} % arrive there from a spacing between a quarter and a half of the planned gap, closing a little at a time; {{sudden_pct}} % arrive from at least half of it in one stop; and {{first_pct}} % are already bunched at the first stop where the pair is observed. Once bunched, a pair tends to stay so: {{stay}} % of bunched pairs are still bunched at the next stop. These counts were made after the results.

Bunching follows the day. On weekdays it is highest in the afternoon peak and lowest late at night (fig. 3).

Sudden closings, and where they happen

The design defined the birth of a bunch as a sudden closing: a pair going from at least half the planned gap to under a quarter of it between two stops. That turned out to be rare, {{births}} in {{eligible_m}} million steps, and to miss most bunches, which close gradually. The question about places is therefore about sudden closings, not about bunching in general.

Platforms were ranked by their rate of sudden closings in odd weeks, and the tenth with the highest rates held {{Sb}} % of the even-week closings, and the other way round; if closings fell anywhere with equal chance per eligible pair-stop, that tenth would hold {{Sb0}} %. The ratio is {{ratio}} (95 % CI {{r_lo}} to {{r_hi}}), above the registered 1.25, so concentration is {{r_label}}, on {{rbirths}} closings at the ranked platforms ({{births}} in all). It depends on the rule: with the stricter rule registered as a check, a fall from at least three quarters of the gap, the ratio is {{r075}}, with an interval reaching down to {{r075_lo}} ({{r075_label}}); counting closings to under a fifth of the gap it is {{r020}}, to under a third {{r033}} (fig. 4). It also depends on one line. Line 9 has {{l9_births}} % of the closings on {{l9_exp}} % of the eligible pair-stops, and without it the ratio is {{no9}} (95 % CI {{no9_lo}} to {{no9_hi}}), no longer wholly above 1.25; these two numbers were computed after the results. A stricter null, added after publication as a post-hoc check, reassigns the closings at random only among eligible pair-stops of the same route variant ({{rp_strata}} of them), so that each variant keeps its own rate: the hottest tenth would then hold {{Sb0p}} %, and the ratio is {{rp}} (95 % CI {{rp_lo}} to {{rp_hi}}, from {{boot}} bootstrap draws with {{nulls}} null draws each, fewer than the registered 999). Its lower bound stays {{rp_vs}} 1.25, so the registered "supported" survives this post-hoc check, but the reading of the platforms as places that make bunches does not. Within the same line it is {{rl}} ({{rl_lo}} to {{rl_hi}}). The platform ranking is therefore partly a line effect; stops shared by several lines, which the design said it could not separate, would show up like this too.

The concentration is also not specific to closings. Sudden re-openings, the reverse event ({{r_deaths}}× exposure, 95 % CI {{r_deaths_lo}} to {{r_deaths_hi}}, on {{deaths}} events), the registered placebo that counts closings backwards along the line, which picks up much the same events ({{r_rev}}×, {{r_rev_lo}} to {{r_rev_hi}}), and closings to under a third of the gap ({{r033}}×, {{r033_lo}} to {{r033_hi}}, {{b033}} events) concentrate as much or more than the registered closings. The design wrote that strong agreement with the reversed order would point to noise or fixed structure rather than a forward process; the reversed ratio is as high, though its ranking agrees with the forward one only weakly ({{fwd_bwd}}), as so few events allow. What concentrates is rare one-stop events in general, partly by line, and the registered label does not make some platforms places where bunches are made.

Whether the same platforms stand out in odd and in even weeks could not be told. The split-half correlation is {{rho}} ({{rho_lo}} to {{rho_hi}}), below the registered 0.50, which makes the stability question {{rho_label}} by the registered rule, although a split-half correlation understates the full-sample one56. The label is uninformative: with about one closing per platform over the season, and none at {{zero_pct}} % of them, the registered 0.50 bar was out of reach at this number of events. In a simulation on the real exposures, with a fixed hottest tenth of platforms whose closing rate is five times the rest, the expected split-half correlation is {{sim5}}, with ten times it is {{sim10}}, and with no hotspot at all {{sim1}}, while the observed value is {{rho}}. That is far above what no hotspots would give and is what a stable fivefold hot tenth would give: the data point to stable hotspots or a stable line effect, since without line 9 the correlation is {{rho_no9}}, on {{b_no9}} closings; the registered 0.50 could not have been reached either way. With the looser rule of closing under a third of the gap ({{b033}} closings) the correlation is {{rho033}}. The simulation was run after the results.

Closings and re-openings are not symmetric: {{births}} sudden closings against {{deaths}} re-openings, and {{stay}} % of bunched pairs are still bunched at the next stop. What is symmetric is the volatility of platforms: across platforms, the share of steps where the spacing falls by more than a quarter of the planned gap and the share where it rises by as much are strongly related ({{dr}}), large steps of either sign that mostly do not end in a bunch, and the rate of sudden closings follows their combined share ({{vol}}). These relations were computed after the results. Fig. 5 maps how often the spacing changes suddenly on each segment. The platform with the most closings is Národní divadlo, {{nd_b}} against about {{nd_e}} expected from its traffic; the segment with the most is {{seg1}} ({{seg1_b}}), then {{seg2}} ({{seg2_b}}). Of the eight platforms with most closings, {{tp_top}} are among the {{tp}} stops with long dwell.

Sudden closings are also more frequent on segments where part 1 found the delay gain most spread out across passes: across {{q3_seg}} segments, keyed by their stop names, the rank correlation is {{q3}} ({{q3_lo}} to {{q3_hi}}), so the link is {{q3_label}} by the rule, but not informative, because it is built in: a closing is a large difference between two trams' gains on one segment and the spread of gains measures the same thing; with the mean gain instead of its spread the correlation is {{q3_mean}}. The interval resamples days only, and a stop name can join several platforms, so it is narrower than it should be.

Buses

City buses, routes 100 to 299, were analysed alongside with their own labels. Over {{bpairs}} pairs their amplification is {{bg}} per stop ({{bg_lo}} to {{bg_hi}}), wholly above zero, so for buses the prediction of amplification is {{bg_label}}: a bus off its spacing tends to move further off at the next stop. With the instrument two, three and four stops back it is {{bg2}}, {{bg3}} and {{bg4}}, and the autocorrelation of the changes at one stop is {{bac}}, so the result does not rest on one lag. The effect is small, about a fifth of a percent per stop, with the sign the textbook account predicts78, and {{bg}} is about the size of the spread across the tram specifications ({{t_min}} to {{t_max}}, a spread of {{t_spread}}), so the contrast between trams and buses should not be over-read. {{bbunched}} % of their pair passes arrive bunched, and the order of two buses swaps in {{bswap}} % of passes, which may be buses passing each other or arrivals matched wrongly. Their {{bbirths}} sudden closings concentrate strongly on some stops ({{bratio}}× exposure, {{br_lo}} to {{br_hi}}, {{br_label}}); bus pairs are noisier, since stops are shared by many lines and routes branch, which the design said in advance. Whether those stops are stable is {{brho_label}}: the split-half correlation is {{brho}}, and its point estimate lies just above its own interval, so it should not be read as the centre. Why buses might differ was not measured; they share the road with cars and can pass each other, and the timing-point rule marks only {{btp}} bus stops, against {{tp}} of 624 tram stops (describe.json, describe-bus.json).

What this means

On Prague's trams in 2025, the spacing between two trams of a line showed practically no amplification from stop to stop: within ±{{g_max}} per stop in every pooled specification, though single lines range more widely, slightly negative over all stops and slightly positive without the stops with long dwell, which the registered rule labels {{g_label}}. Bunches built up gradually towards the ends of lines; whether faster or slower than independent drift would build them, the registered shuffle cannot say, because it lets trams pass each other. The textbook mechanism of a late vehicle falling ever further behind was not visible in the stop-to-stop changes of trams, and for buses a small positive value appeared that is of the size of the spread among the tram estimates. The rare sudden closings concentrate on some platforms, as other rare one-stop events do, and partly by line; the data locate them but do not explain them. The shares of bunching are a floor, because trips with a missing row were dropped.

What this does not show

The amplification is a net figure. Trams are timetabled and are probably held at some stops, so a value near zero means that whatever feeds bunching and whatever corrects it cancel out here, not that nothing feeds it. The pairs are two trams of one line scheduled four to fifteen minutes apart, formed near the start of the line; passengers on a stop shared by several lines see a combined spacing this does not measure, and convoys of three are not analysed. {{low_pairs}} pairs scheduled three to four minutes apart were too few to say anything. The sudden closings are located, not explained; incidents, junctions and stops shared by several lines look alike here. The descriptions of how bunches form, of the platforms' volatility, of line 9 and of the power of the stability test were made after the results, and the null within route variants and the estimates by line after publication. Spacing between trams is not passengers' waiting time, and one spring and summer is not every season.
