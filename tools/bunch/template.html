Picture a tram stop in the centre of Prague. The board promised a 22, and for ten minutes nothing came. Then two 22s pull in together, nose to tail.

Bunching has a well-known explanation, and it comes from buses. A bus that falls a little behind finds more people waiting at each stop. Boarding them takes longer, and it slips further behind. The bus following it finds fewer people and catches up, until the two travel together78. On that account the spacing between two vehicles is unstable, and a small slip grows by itself.

Part 1 of this series found where Prague's trams record their delay. This part follows two trams of the same line along their route and watches the gap between them, to see whether it breaks down by itself.

For trams, the gap barely grew or shrank from stop to stop, though the registered rule labels that answer {{g_label}}. Bunches were rare and built up slowly towards the end of a line.

The question

A pair here means two trams of the same line and route, one following the other, scheduled between four and fifteen minutes apart. That is the frequent service people use without checking a timetable. No line is singled out, and the two trams of the opening scene stand for any such pair.

Their spacing at a stop is the gap between their arrivals divided by the gap the timetable plans there. A value of 1 means on plan. A value of 0.5 means half the planned gap, and anything under 0.25 counts as bunched.

A pair is followed from the first stop where the two trams run one after the other to the end of the line, or until a third tram of the line slips in between them. Convoys of three are therefore not analysed.

Three questions were written down before any spacing was computed. The first is amplification, whether a pair that is off plan at one stop drifts further off at the next.

The second is about places. A sudden closing is a pair going from at least half the planned gap to under a quarter of it in one stop. The question is whether these happen more often at some platforms than their traffic alone would give, and whether those platforms are the same in odd and even weeks.

The third asks whether the closings follow the segments, the stretches of track between two stops, where part 1 found the delay gain most spread out.

Each question has its own rule for the label it gets. A question is "supported" if its 95 % interval, the range the data leave plausible, lies wholly on the predicted side of its line. Amplification is "not supported" if the 90 % interval lies wholly within ±0.01 per stop, a margin set as too small to matter. An interval wholly below zero is listed as "inconclusive", though. The places, their stability and the link to part 1 are "not supported" if the interval lies wholly on the other side of its line or, for the link, wholly within ±0.10. Anything else is "inconclusive".

Data

The trams come from the author's thesis. They are Prague Integrated Transport's stop-time history from Golemio2 for 15 March to 8 September 20251, screened as in the earlier studies for planned closures and gaps in the feed.

A trip counts only if it runs its line's usual sequence of stops with a row at every one of them. Each month {{modal_lo}}–{{modal_hi}} % of trips do. The other {{drop_lo}}–{{drop_hi}} % are dropped, which means short turns, diversions and any trip with a missing row. These are plausibly the more disturbed trips, so the shares of bunching below are a floor.

The first and last stop of each line are left out as well, because the timetable sets the departure there. A route variant is a line in one direction with its end stops. Variants with fewer than 1 000 pairs over the season are also left out. There are {{thin}} of them, mostly rare ones, which leaves {{patterns}} variants and {{pairs}} pairs.

The two arrivals of a pair share an arrival second in 0.0 % of stops. The times look measured rather than copied from the timetable, since only a fraction of a percent equal it to the second.

Amplification from stop to stop

Amplification measures how much a pair's spacing changes from one stop to the next for each unit it is off plan at the first. A positive value would mean a small slip tends to grow. A negative one would mean it tends to be corrected.

Measured directly, it is pulled below zero by noise in the arrival times alone. An arrival recorded a few seconds late makes the spacing look off at one stop and corrected at the next.

The design first proposed to remove that by comparing with shuffled data. Tests on made-up data before the results showed that the shuffle also removes the noise it was meant to keep. So, before any spacing was read, the registered estimate was changed to an instrumental-variable one, a stand-in method in the spirit of Anderson and Hsiao (1981).

The spacing two stops back stands in for the spacing one stop back. It shares none of that stop's recording noise, as long as the noise is not carried from stop to stop4. The changes two and three stops apart are barely related (autocorrelations {{ac2}} and {{ac3}}), which fits that assumption. The stand-in also tracks the real thing closely. The first-stage coefficient of the spacing one stop back on the spacing two stops back is {{fs}} (t = {{fs_t}}), so the instrument is strong.

Over {{transitions_m}} million steps from one stop to the next, the spacing barely changed, a result the registered rule labels {{g_label}}. The amplification is {{g}} per stop (95 % CI {{g_lo}} to {{g_hi}}).

That interval lies wholly below zero, which the design lists under "inconclusive", as a case where correction outweighs amplification. The 90 % interval, {{g90_lo}} to {{g90_hi}}, also lies wholly within the margin of ±0.01 per stop set for "not supported". The clause for an interval below zero is the specific one and governs, so the prediction of amplification is {{g_label}}.

Version 1 of this page said "{{g_label_v1}}". The code had checked the margin first and so applied the registered rule in the wrong order.

In size, the pooled value is practically zero. Every pooled specification, each a different way of setting up the estimate, lies within ±{{g_max}} per stop, intervals included. The sign flips without the timing-point stops, where trams wait longer ({{g_tp}}), or on departures ({{g_dep}}).

With the spacing three or four stops back as the stand-in the estimate is {{g3}} and {{g4}}. Resampled by week, its interval is {{gw_lo}} to {{gw_hi}}. Measured plainly, without the instrument, it would be {{g_ols}}, and the design's original shuffled comparison gives {{g_reg}}. Both sit further from zero, mainly through the recording noise (fig. 1).

The timing points are {{tp}} stops where trams stand much longer than the network's usual half-minute, or are planned with a pause of a minute or more. Without them the small negative value turns into {{g_tp}} ({{g_tp_lo}} to {{g_tp_hi}}). That is wholly above zero and at most about a tenth of the registered margin. Variants do not relabel the primary result, but this one would read as "supported" had it been primary. Dropping those stops also drops every step whose chain touches them.

On departures instead of arrivals the estimate is {{g_dep}}. Both trams' departures are recorded at about {{dep_share}} % of stops.

One reading, formed after the results, fits these numbers. Trams may be held at those stops, which pulls the spacing back, and between them the spacing drifts with a very slight tendency to grow. That trams are held there is an inference from how long they stand (dwell), not a documented fact.

Single lines vary more. Estimated line by line after publication, the amplification ranges from {{gl_min}} (line {{gl_min_line}}) to {{gl_max}} (line {{gl_max_line}}) across the {{gl_n}} lines with at least 50 000 instrumented steps, a cut-off chosen after seeing the numbers. With the two smaller lines included it ranges from {{gla_min}} (line {{gla_min_line}}) to {{gla_max}} (line {{gla_max_line}}).

Bunches build slowly, towards the end

Trams leave the terminus close to plan, and their spacing spreads as they go. Its variance, a measure of that spread, grows from {{var1}} in the first tenth of a line to {{var10}} in the last.

The share of pair passes, one pair reaching one stop, that arrive bunched or swapped grows with it. It is {{d1}} % near the start and {{d10}} % near the end (fig. 2).

The registered benchmark for this curve is a shuffle. Each tram's stop-to-stop changes are swapped between pairs of the same route, hour and day. That keeps the size of the changes and removes any link between them.

Near zero it is not a valid benchmark. In the last tenth, shuffled pairs swap order in {{s10swap}} % of passes, which rails do not allow ({{o10swap}} % observed). Compared as registered, without swaps, the observed last tenth is {{d10u}} % bunched against {{s10u}} % shuffled. The observed spacing varies less, though ({{var10}} against {{var10s}}).

With swaps pooled, a choice made after the results, the two are close ({{d10}} % and {{s10}} %). So the comparison cannot say whether bunches build faster or slower than independent drift would build them.

Over the whole line, both bunches and big gaps are rare. Of all pair passes, {{bunched}} % arrive under a quarter of the planned gap apart and {{gap}} % more than 1.75 times it. With looser cut-offs of half and one and a half times the planned gap the shares are {{b05}} % and {{g15}} %. Those cut-offs are conventions of headway-reliability work, the study of how evenly vehicles are spaced. They were not checked against the exact tables of the Transit Capacity and Quality of Service Manual, which measures headway adherence relative to the scheduled headway9.

Counted pair by pair, most bunches form slowly. Of all pairs, {{ever_pct}} % ({{ever}}) are bunched at some stop. Most of these, {{gradual_pct}} %, close a little at a time from a spacing between a quarter and a half of the planned gap. Another {{sudden_pct}} % get there from at least half of it in one stop, and {{first_pct}} % are already bunched at the first stop where the pair is observed.

Once bunched, a pair tends to stay that way. Of bunched pairs, {{stay}} % are still bunched at the next stop. These counts were made after the results.

Over the day, bunching on weekdays is highest in the afternoon peak and lowest late at night (fig. 3).

Sudden closings, and where they happen

The design defined the birth of a bunch as a sudden closing, a pair going from at least half the planned gap to under a quarter of it between two stops. Such closings turned out to be rare, {{births}} in {{eligible_m}} million steps. They also miss most bunches, which close gradually. The question about places is therefore about sudden closings and not about bunching in general.

Platforms were ranked by their rate of sudden closings in odd weeks. The tenth with the highest rates then held {{Sb}} % of the even-week closings, with the same done the other way round. If closings fell anywhere with equal chance per eligible pair-stop, in step with traffic alone (exposure), that tenth would hold {{Sb0}} %.

So the hottest platforms hold more closings than their traffic would give. The ratio is {{ratio}} (95 % CI {{r_lo}} to {{r_hi}}), above the registered 1.25, so concentration is {{r_label}}. It rests on {{rbirths}} closings at the ranked platforms ({{births}} in all).

The result depends on the rule. With the stricter rule registered as a check, a fall from at least three quarters of the gap, the ratio is {{r075}}, with an interval reaching down to {{r075_lo}} ({{r075_label}}). Counting closings to under a fifth of the gap it is {{r020}}, and to under a third {{r033}} (fig. 4).

It also depends on one line. Line 9 has {{l9_births}} % of the closings on {{l9_exp}} % of the eligible pair-stops. Without it the ratio is {{no9}} (95 % CI {{no9_lo}} to {{no9_hi}}), no longer wholly above 1.25. These two numbers were computed after the results.

A stricter null, the no-hotspot baseline, was added after publication as a post-hoc check. It reassigns the closings at random only among eligible pair-stops of the same route variant ({{rp_strata}} of them), so that each variant keeps its own rate. Under that null the hottest tenth would hold {{Sb0p}} %.

Against that baseline the ratio is {{rp}} (95 % CI {{rp_lo}} to {{rp_hi}}). It comes from {{boot}} bootstrap draws, resamples of the days, with {{nulls}} null draws each, fewer than the registered 999. Its lower bound stays {{rp_vs}} 1.25. So the registered "supported" survives this post-hoc check, but the reading of the platforms as places that make bunches does not.

Within the same line the ratio is {{rl}} ({{rl_lo}} to {{rl_hi}}). The platform ranking is therefore partly a line effect. Stops shared by several lines, which the design said it could not separate, would show up like this too.

The concentration is not specific to closings either. Sudden re-openings, the reverse event, concentrate as much or more than the registered closings ({{r_deaths}}× exposure, 95 % CI {{r_deaths_lo}} to {{r_deaths_hi}}, on {{deaths}} events).

So does the registered placebo, a dummy run that counts closings backwards along the line and picks up much the same events ({{r_rev}}×, {{r_rev_lo}} to {{r_rev_hi}}). So do closings to under a third of the gap ({{r033}}×, {{r033_lo}} to {{r033_hi}}, {{b033}} events).

The design wrote that strong agreement with the reversed order would point to noise or fixed structure rather than a forward process. The reversed ratio is as high. Its ranking agrees with the forward one only weakly ({{fwd_bwd}}), as so few events allow.

What concentrates is rare one-stop events in general, partly by line. The registered label does not turn some platforms into places where bunches are made.

Whether the same platforms stand out in odd and in even weeks could not be told. The split-half correlation, how closely the odd-week ranking matches the even-week one, is {{rho}} ({{rho_lo}} to {{rho_hi}}). That is below the registered 0.50, which makes the stability question {{rho_label}} by the registered rule, though the label is uninformative. A split-half correlation also understates the full-sample one56.

The events were too few. With about one closing per platform over the season, and none at {{zero_pct}} % of them, the registered 0.50 bar was out of reach at this number of events.

A simulation on the real exposures shows how far out of reach. A fixed hottest tenth of platforms with a closing rate five times the rest gives an expected split-half correlation of {{sim5}}. At ten times the rest it is {{sim10}}, and with no hotspot at all {{sim1}}. The observed value is {{rho}}.

That is far above what no hotspots would give and is what a stable fivefold hot tenth would give. It fits stable hotspots or a stable line effect, since without line 9 the correlation is {{rho_no9}}, on {{b_no9}} closings. The registered 0.50 could not have been reached either way. With the looser rule of closing under a third of the gap ({{b033}} closings) the correlation is {{rho033}}. The simulation was run after the results.

Closings and re-openings are not symmetric. There are {{births}} sudden closings against {{deaths}} re-openings, and {{stay}} % of bunched pairs are still bunched at the next stop.

How jumpy the spacing is at a platform (its volatility) is symmetric, though. Across platforms, the share of steps where the spacing falls by more than a quarter of the planned gap and the share where it rises by as much are strongly related ({{dr}}). These are large steps of either sign, and most do not end in a bunch. The rate of sudden closings follows their combined share ({{vol}}). These relations were computed after the results.

Fig. 5 maps how often the spacing changes suddenly on each segment. The platform with the most closings is Národní divadlo, with {{nd_b}} against about {{nd_e}} expected from its traffic. The segment with the most is {{seg1}} ({{seg1_b}}), followed by {{seg2}} ({{seg2_b}}). Of the eight platforms with most closings, {{tp_top}} are among the {{tp}} stops with long dwell.

Sudden closings are also more frequent on segments where part 1 found the delay gain most spread out across passes, though that link is built into the measures. Across {{q3_seg}} segments, keyed by their stop names, the rank correlation is {{q3}} ({{q3_lo}} to {{q3_hi}}). So the link is {{q3_label}} by the rule, but not informative.

A closing is a large difference between two trams' gains on one segment, and the spread of gains measures the same thing. With the mean gain instead of its spread the correlation is {{q3_mean}}. The interval resamples days only, and a stop name can join several platforms, so it is narrower than it should be.

Buses

City buses, routes 100 to 299, were analysed alongside, with their own labels. For buses a slip in spacing tends to grow a little from stop to stop, by an amount the size of the spread among the tram estimates.

Over {{bpairs}} pairs their amplification is {{bg}} per stop ({{bg_lo}} to {{bg_hi}}), wholly above zero, so for buses the prediction of amplification is {{bg_label}}. A bus off its spacing tends to move further off at the next stop.

With the spacing two, three and four stops back as the stand-in the estimate is {{bg2}}, {{bg3}} and {{bg4}}. The autocorrelation of the changes at one stop is {{bac}}. So the result does not rest on one lag.

The value is small, about a fifth of a percent per stop, with the sign the textbook account predicts78. At {{bg}} it is about the size of the spread across the tram specifications ({{t_min}} to {{t_max}}, a spread of {{t_spread}}). So the contrast between trams and buses should not be over-read.

Of bus pair passes, {{bbunched}} % arrive bunched. The order of two buses swaps in {{bswap}} % of passes, which may be buses passing each other or arrivals matched wrongly.

Their {{bbirths}} sudden closings concentrate strongly on some stops ({{bratio}}× exposure, {{br_lo}} to {{br_hi}}, {{br_label}}). Bus pairs are noisier, since stops are shared by many lines and routes branch, as the design said in advance.

Whether those stops are stable is {{brho_label}}. The split-half correlation is {{brho}}, and its point estimate lies just above its own interval, so it should not be read as the centre.

Why buses might differ was not measured. They share the road with cars and can pass each other. The timing-point rule also marks only {{btp}} bus stops, against {{tp}} of 624 tram stops (describe.json, describe-bus.json).

What this means

On Prague's trams in 2025, the gap between two trams of a line barely grew or shrank from stop to stop, though the registered rule labels this {{g_label}}.

That gap stayed within ±{{g_max}} per stop in every pooled specification. Single lines range more widely.

Over all stops the value was slightly negative. Without the stops with long dwell it was slightly positive.

Bunches built up gradually towards the ends of lines. Whether they built faster or slower than independent drift would build them, the registered shuffle cannot say, because it lets trams pass each other.

The textbook picture of a late vehicle falling ever further behind was not visible in the stop-to-stop changes of trams.

For buses a small positive value appeared, of the size of the spread among the tram estimates.

The rare sudden closings concentrate on some platforms, as other rare one-stop events do, and partly by line. This analysis locates them but does not explain them.

The shares of bunching are a floor, because trips with a missing row were dropped.

What this does not show

The amplification is a net figure. Trams are timetabled and are probably held at some stops. A value near zero means that whatever feeds bunching and whatever corrects it cancel out here. It does not mean that nothing feeds it.

The pairs are two trams of one line scheduled four to fifteen minutes apart, formed near the start of the line. Passengers at a stop shared by several lines see a combined spacing that this does not measure. Convoys of three are not analysed.

The {{low_pairs}} pairs scheduled three to four minutes apart were too few to say anything.

The sudden closings are located, not explained. Incidents, junctions and stops shared by several lines look alike here.

Some parts came after the results. These are the descriptions of how bunches form, of the platforms' volatility, of line 9 and of the power of the stability test. The null within route variants and the estimates by line came after publication.

Spacing between trams is not passengers' waiting time. And one spring and summer is not every season.
