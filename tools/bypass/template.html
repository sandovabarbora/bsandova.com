Research · roads · Czech bypasses and police-recorded accidents, 2016–2025

Are towns calmer after the bypass opens?

Inside {{treated}} Czech towns, police recorded about a fifth fewer injury crashes after a bypass opened than in towns still waiting, but with so few towns the data can't rule out no change at all.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • After a bypass opened, the police recorded fewer injury accidents inside the town, with a larger relative fall on the old through road in a check, while the count outside the built-up area went up by about one a year per town and the municipality's total did not change detectably. Every one of these intervals is wide, and the registered label is underpowered: with {{treated}} towns, only a change of about {{q1_sdc}} % could have been told from zero.
  • One registered check came out backwards: with damage-only crashes counted too, accidents seemed to rise. The trail led to Prague, a comparison town that dominates the counts and whose recorded minor crashes fell by half. Without Prague, an analysis added after the results and not registered, that check is {{wp_1_all_accidents_q1}} and the injury result {{wp_q1}} (95 % {{wp_q1_ci95}}), still underpowered.
  • The author expected accidents to move onto the bypass. The pattern is consistent with that without supporting it.
  • All of this is association around the opening date in a small, recent set of towns, not a measured effect of the bypass.

Picture the main street of a small Czech town. The national road runs down it, past the bakery and the school, and lorries rumble through all day. Then a bypass opens around the edge of town and the through traffic moves out there.

A calmer main street is the hope, and one question is whether the crashes simply move out to the new road. This study took the {{treated}} towns whose bypass opened between 2020 and 2024 and set them beside {{comparison}} towns still waiting for theirs. It used every accident the Czech police recorded from 2016 to 2025[[police]]. The questions, the list of bypasses and the rules for reading the result were fixed before any accident was counted for a place or a year.

Inside the towns, crashes where someone was hurt fell against the waiting towns, though there are too few towns to be sure. In the three years after opening, injury accidents in the built-up area, the stretch between the town's name signs, changed by {{q1_est}} relative to them. That is about {{q1_d}} a year per town.

On the old main road, the one the bypass took over, the fall looks larger. A registered check, an extra test fixed in advance that cannot change the label, gives {{c_10_old_through_road_q1}} there, about {{c_10_old_through_road_q1_d}} a year.

Out past the name signs, where the bypass runs, the count went the other way, up by {{q2_out}} a year per town. Put inside and outside together and the whole municipality changed by {{q2_est}}, with no detectable change either way (fig. 1).

On the bypasses themselves the police recorded {{q3_rate}} injury accidents per kilometre of road each year, {{q3_junc}} % of them at junctions. A Norwegian study of 20 bypasses found 19 % fewer injury accidents[[elvik]], close to the Czech estimate.

The margins are wide. For crashes in town, the 95 % interval runs from {{q1_ci95}}. That covers both a fall by half and no change, so the registered label is underpowered. With {{treated}} towns, only a change of about {{q1_sdc}} % could have been told from zero.

Put plainly, relative to the waiting towns, injury crashes in town fell by about a fifth and rose out by the bypass, but neither change is clear of zero. They describe how recorded accidents moved around the opening date, not what the bypass itself did.

How far to trust it

The towns are few and recent. The public police files start in 2016. Before any count, the author chose not to request older records, which would have added bypasses opened since 2008, and under the registered rule that made this recent set, stage A, the headline.

Because towns got their bypass when permits and money were ready, the waiting towns are a comparison, not a control. The comparison also leans on one city, Prague.

The registered run stopped twice before producing an estimate. Both causes were fixed and logged, and the code frozen again, before any estimate was read. Everything added after the results (the Prague tabulation, the re-estimate without Prague, the map and the example town) is marked as such and changes no registered label.

The {{treated}} towns

The towns are spread across the country, from Karviná in the east to Mariánské Lázně in the west (fig. 2). {{map_i}} sit on class I roads, built by the state, and {{map_ii}} on class II roads, built by the regions. Several bypasses serve two or three municipalities. Most towns are small. In half of them the police recorded {{map_median_pre}} or fewer injury accidents a year in town before works began. One town's before and after is not an estimate. It has no comparison and moves with chance from year to year.

One town, picked by a rule: {{ex_name}}

One town was picked by a rule set before its counts were read. It is the class I town with the most years after opening, ties broken by the earliest opening date. That is {{ex_name}} on road {{ex_road}}. Its bypass ({{ex_row}}) opened on {{ex_opened}} after works began in {{ex_start}}, the only class I bypass opened in 2020.

Between its name signs the police recorded no injury accident in any year from 2016 to 2025. So it adds nothing to Q1 and is a reminder that some towns are villages. In the whole municipality, crossed by road I/19, they recorded {{ex_muni_pre}}, before and during construction, then {{ex_muni_post}} injury accidents in the {{ex_post_n}} full years after opening. On the bypass road, which need not lie wholly inside the municipality, they recorded {{ex_q3}} in those years. One town proves nothing, and {{ex_name}} is here only because the rule picked it.

The check that came out backwards

Thirteen registered checks repeat the analysis with one thing changed. Most land near the headline or below it (fig. 4). Check 1 did not. Counting every recorded accident, including crashes where only cars were damaged, it gave {{c_1_all_accidents_q1}} inside the towns and {{c_1_all_accidents_q2}} for the municipalities. At face value, crashes rose sharply where injury crashes fell.

A reversal this large needed explaining. After the results, the counts behind it were tabulated by place and year, estimating nothing (tools/bypass/describe.py).

The police field descriptions show no change in how severity was coded over the decade[[police]]. The treated towns' own damage-only counts had changed by {{tr_dmg_ch}}, close to the {{oc_dmg_ch}} of the other comparison towns. The difference lay in one comparison town, Prague.

Prague is in the group because the Úvaly bypass on road I/12 ({{prague_row}}), whose works began in 2025, reaches into its territory, and the design counts every municipality a bypass serves. That gives Prague {{pr_share_dmg}} % of the comparison towns' damage-only accidents in town and {{pr_share_inj}} % of their injury accidents. In a Poisson model the yearly baseline follows those counts.

Between 2016–2019 and 2023–2025, Prague's recorded damage-only accidents fell by half ({{pr_dmg_ch}}), while its injury accidents rose ({{pr_inj_ch}}) (fig. 3). Against a fall that size, any town whose minor crashes held steady looks like a rise.

Most of Prague's fall is in the smallest crashes, with recorded damage up to 100 000 CZK ({{pr_small_ch}} from 2016 to 2025). Those above that line held steady in nominal crowns. Below that limit the police need not be called to a crash where nobody is hurt[[threshold]], so whether it is recorded is up to the drivers. The pattern fits fewer minor crashes being reported or recorded in Prague better than fewer crashes. The records cannot tell the two apart, and nothing read for this study names a change in Prague's practice.

Leaving Prague out tests this reading. At the author's request, after the results and not registered, Q1, Q2 and check 1 were estimated again by the frozen code with only Prague's row removed from the comparison towns. Úvaly stays (tools/bypass/without_prague.py).

Inside the towns, check 1 fell from {{c_1_all_accidents_q1}} to {{wp_1_all_accidents_q1}}. For the municipalities it fell from {{c_1_all_accidents_q2}} to {{wp_1_all_accidents_q2}}. The injury result moved much less, from {{q1_est}} to {{wp_q1}}. Its 95 % interval is {{wp_q1_ci95}}, and the registered rule would still call it {{wp_q1_label}}. Q2 became {{wp_q2}}. So the reversed sign of check 1 depends on Prague being a comparison town, and the injury result much less. None of this replaces a registered label.

The author's expectation

On 15 October 2026, before she had looked at any accident data but after hearing of the Norwegian result, the author wrote that she expected the accidents to move onto the bypass (in Czech: „předpokládala bych, že se přesunou na obchvat“).

The model gives {{q1_d}} injury accidents a year inside the town and {{q2_out}} outside it, per town. The bypass roads carry {{q3_rate}} per kilometre and year. Neither the inside nor the outside change is clear of zero, and Q2 as a whole is {{q2_est}} (95 % {{q2_ci95}}). The offset share was not reported, by rule, and Q3 has no comparison. So the pattern is consistent with her expectation without supporting it.

What this means

For a decision about a bypass, the number to look at is the change in injury accidents recorded in town in the first three years after opening, against towns still waiting. It is {{q1_est}}, with a 95 % interval from {{q1_ci95}}. That range holds both the Norwegian fifth and no change.

The comparison leans on Prague, but in an unregistered re-estimate without Prague the change is {{wp_q1}} (95 % {{wp_q1_ci95}}). So the conclusion does not turn on that city.

The study cannot say how much of any fall the bypass itself accounts for. It also says little about the bypass road, where the rate is descriptive. Telling a fall of a fifth from none would need more towns, such as stage B with the older records.

What this does not show

It does not show an effect of bypasses. Towns got their bypass when its permits and money were ready, not at random. The opening also came with other changes, such as new junctions, a downgraded old road and name signs that move the edge of the built-up area. Police recording can vary between places as well as over time, as Prague shows. Accidents in neighbouring municipalities are not counted in Q2, and {{q3_nogeo}} of the {{bypasses}} bypasses have no mapped line for Q3.

Method, details and reproduction

Method: data, model, labels and what was seen before registration

Data

The accidents are the record-level files of the Police of the Czech Republic for 2016 to 2025, one row per accident, with date, severity, the built-up-area flag (p5a), road number and S-JTSK coordinates[[police]]. An injury accident is one in which at least one person was killed, seriously or slightly injured; damage-only accidents enter only check 1. Each accident is placed in a municipality by the RÚIAN polygons[[ruian]]. Inside the built-up area means the record's own flag, as recorded in the year of the accident.

The bypasses come from a list built by an AI agent from construction facts only, without any accident or traffic count: what was built, where, when works started and when the road opened[[list]]. Only full bypasses of class I and II roads count, that is roads that pass the built-up area entirely on one side and take the through traffic. A treated town needs two calendar years in the police files before its works started, so stage A holds the towns whose works started in 2018 or later. After the exclusions written into the design (missing dates, other road works in the town, a second bypass, a boundary change of more than 2 %), {{treated}} towns on {{bypasses}} bypasses remain, {{class_i}} on class I roads and {{class_ii}} on class II; they opened in {{open_years}}. The comparison group has {{comparison}} towns on {{comparison_byp}} bypasses that opened in 2025 or later or are planned at a dated stage, each serving only until its works start; treated towns also serve before their own works.

How this was done

The model is a Poisson regression of each town's yearly count of injury accidents on town and year effects, with separate indicators for each cohort of opening years (bins {{cohorts}}) in each construction year and each year after opening, the extended two-way fixed-effects estimator for staggered openings[[wooldridge]], fitted in Python with statsmodels[[software]]. The baseline is the town's years before construction started; construction years and the opening year are kept apart from it. The Q1 estimate is the exponent of the average of the coefficients for post years 1 to 3, weighted by the treated town-years in each cell, minus one: a ratio of mean counts, not an average of towns' own changes.

Intervals come from {{draws}} bootstrap draws of whole bypasses with all their towns and years, treated and comparison towns resampled separately, seed {{seed}}. A draw in which the Poisson fit of Q1 or Q2 failed or had a cell without accidents was redrawn ({{redrawn_sep}} times); a check whose own fit failed in an accepted draw is left missing for that draw. Each question takes the first registered label that applies: fewer or more accidents after opening if the 95 % interval excludes zero and the estimate is at least 10 % from it; no change larger than 15 % if the 90 % interval lies within ±15 %; underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than 30 points; inconclusive otherwise. Under stage A the first two labels would carry the words “with differing trends before construction” and the third could not be given, because the pre-trend test is uninformative by rule.

The registered run stopped twice before producing an estimate; both causes were fixed after the code freeze and logged on 26 October 2026: the run command lacked two geodata packages that the town step imports, and the town-trend fit of check 9 failed inside one bootstrap draw outside the guard the main fit already had. The fit now sits inside that guard and the code was frozen again; the run restarted from draw 1 with the same seed, so the accepted draws are the same as they would have been.

The design discloses what was seen before registration (§0). The author had not looked at accident data around any bypass. A feasibility check opened the police files of 2018, 2022 and 2024 for their national row counts, field descriptions and coordinate coverage only, and reported the Norwegian study, which the author heard of before stating her expectation. While the list was built, one agent briefly displayed six rows of a regional prioritisation table whose columns include accident scores, and another opened a 2009 study ranking candidate bypasses and stopped at its safety criteria; neither was used. The treated window was widened and class III kept out after counts of the list by class and year were seen, which are construction facts. A methods reviewer read the draft design, without accident data.

Stage A: why it is the headline and what it covers

The design registered two data stages: stage A on the public police files from 2016, and stage B adding the accident records of 2006–2015 held by CDV, the transport research centre, which would have brought in bypasses whose works started from 2008. The rule was that stage B would be the headline if its records arrived by 31 March 2027. On 16 October 2026, before any accident count was computed, the author decided not to request them, which by the same rule fixed stage A as the headline; any analysis of the older records would be reported as added after registration.

All {{treated}} towns opened their bypass between 2020 and 2024, in two cohorts, so the post years fall in 2021–2025: {{post1}} towns contribute a first year, {{post2}} a second and {{post3}} a third. Each town has two to seven years before construction. Leads −3 and −2 exist for {{lead3}} and {{lead2}} towns ({{lead_any}} with either); their coefficients are {{lead3_b}} and {{lead2_b}} and the Wald test gives p = {{wald_p}}, but under stage A the design declared the test uninformative whatever it showed, as passing it would not show parallel trends. The placebo, which moves each town's construction start two years earlier and uses only years before construction, gives {{pl_est}} (95 % {{pl_ci}}, cluster-robust by bypass, {{pl_towns}} towns).

Q1: injury accidents inside the town

Relative to towns whose works had not started, injury accidents inside the built-up area of the treated towns changed by {{q1_est}} in post years 1 to 3 (90 % interval {{q1_ci90}}, 95 % {{q1_ci95}}), or {{q1_d}} accidents per town and year (95 % {{q1_d_ci}}). The 95 % interval includes zero, so the estimate does not meet the rule for fewer accidents, and the 90 % interval is {{q1_w}} points wide, more than the 30 points the rule allows, so the label is underpowered. The design named underpowered as the likely label with fewer than about 40 treated towns.

Q2: the whole municipality

On the whole territory of the municipality, which holds most of each bypass, injury accidents changed by {{q2_est}} (90 % {{q2_ci90}}, 95 % {{q2_ci95}}), again underpowered, with a 90 % interval {{q2_w}} points wide. In accidents per town and year the model splits this into {{q2_in}} inside the built-up area and {{q2_out}} outside it (95 % {{q2_out_ci}}), {{q2_d}} in all (95 % {{q2_d_ci}}). The offset share, the part of the inside change matched by the outside change, was registered to be reported only if Q1's 95 % interval excluded zero; it does not, so the share is not reported. Neighbouring municipalities are not counted.

Q3: accidents on the bypass roads

Q3 is descriptive and carries no label: a bypass is a different road, with a different length and traffic, so its rate cannot be compared with Q1. An accident counts on the bypass if it lies within {{q3_buffer}} m of the mapped line, outside the built-up area and on the bypass's road number. For the {{q3_geo}} bypasses with OpenStreetMap ways[[osm]], the police recorded {{q3_acc}} injury accidents over {{q3_kmy}} kilometre-years after opening, {{q3_rate}} per kilometre and year, ranging from {{q3_min}} to {{q3_max}} by bypass; {{q3_junc_n}} of them ({{q3_junc}} %) were at a junction or in its zone. The other {{q3_nogeo}} bypasses, all on class II roads, have no mapped line in the list and are left out, so Q3 rests mostly on class I roads. Before construction, the old through road inside the town had {{q3_old}} injury accidents per town and year ({{q3_old_acc}} accidents in {{q3_old_ty}} town-years), a rate per town, not per kilometre, as the old road's length is not in the list.

Prague and the damage-only counts in detail

In single years, Prague's records with a recorded damage of up to 100 000 CZK went from {{pr_small_16}} in 2016 to {{pr_small_22}} in 2022 and {{pr_small_25}} in 2025, while those above 100 000 CZK were {{pr_big_16}} and {{pr_big_25}}; outside Prague the small ones changed by {{rest_small_ch}} and the large ones by {{rest_big_ch}}. The bands are nominal, and rising prices move accidents into the top band everywhere. The limit for calling the police rose to 200 000 CZK on 1 July 2025, after Prague's fall had begun in 2020. Damage-only collisions with game fell nationally from {{wild_22}} in 2022 to {{wild_25}} in 2025, mostly outside built-up areas, which bears on check 1 for the municipality rather than inside the town.

Inside the treated towns, damage-only accidents on the old through road went from {{ph_pre_old}} per town and year before construction to {{ph_post_old}} in post years 1 to 3, in step with injury accidents ({{ph_pre_inj}} to {{ph_post_inj}} on all roads).
