A hit lasts three times as long in its own language, and tickets barely follow income

Exploratory: across five artists, songs lasted about {{m1_x}} times as long in countries that speak their language, a figure carried by one Spanish song (English songs alone: {{en_x}} times), and ticket prices rose only {{m2_th}} % for each 1 % of the host country's income.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Put the five artists side by side and a hit lasted about {{m1_x}} times as long where people speak its language, allowing for countries where every hit lingers and for songs that are simply bigger.
  • That figure mixes about {{en_x}} times for the English songs with {{es_x}} times for one Spanish song, Bad Bunny's, whose same-language pairs are all still in the charts; without the songs still charting it is {{ck_without_still_charting}}, close to the English estimate.
  • Tour prices rose only {{m2_th}} % for each 1 % of host-country income, so the same show costs several times more days of GDP per head in poorer countries; without Bad Bunny's tours the elasticity is {{drop_min}}.
  • In Czechia the English-language songs did better than the model expects and the Korean and Spanish ones worse, but that is the pattern in most countries that speak none of the five languages, not a Czech trait.
  • This is exploratory: the inputs were already seen in parts 1–5, five artists are not a sample of pop, and more artists singing in other languages could overturn either result.

Parts 1 to 5 of Pop, measured asked the same questions of five artists one at a time, and each part could say only how its own artist compared with a reference: other number ones, other countries' hits, other cities. Set side by side, the five parts allow two questions none of them could answer alone. The first is whether a song lasts longer in countries that speak its language. Ferreira and Waldfogel showed, for half a century of national charts, that listeners favour music from their own country and language even as music trade has grown1; streaming data, which economists have used to measure how streaming changes music sales2, let the question be asked song by song and day by day. The second is whether concert prices follow the incomes of the places a tour visits. Krueger described how the concert market prices superstars3, and Courty and Pagliero the pricing styles promoters use, including how much prices vary across seats and shows4; a tour that plays the Philippines, Mexico and Switzerland in one year shows whether its prices move with the country or stay put. Both questions are answered here with fixed-effects regressions on the parts' published results.

Data

The first model uses every pair of an artist and a country in which the artist's focal song, the part's song with the most global streams (for Bad Bunny, the most-streamed one with a full record), entered the national Spotify Top 200: {{m1_n}} pairs across five artists and {{m1_clusters}} countries, each with the song's days in that chart. Pairs where the song never charted are not in the data, so the model describes how long a song lasted once it had entered, not whether it entered. {{m1_still}} of the pairs are songs still in the chart, whose days are a lower bound. The five focal songs are As It Was (Harry Styles), Cruel Summer (Taylor Swift), Dynamite (BTS), DtMF (Bad Bunny) and BIRDS OF A FEATHER (Billie Eilish). Each pair is coded for whether the song's language is an official national language of the country: English for Harry Styles, Taylor Swift and Billie Eilish, Spanish for Bad Bunny, Korean for BTS; English-speaking countries are those where English is an official national language, Spanish-speaking those of Spain and Latin America, and Korean only South Korea. {{m1_same}} pairs share a language.

The second model uses every Boxscore entry (Billboard's record of concert grosses, as reported on Wikipedia) with gross and tickets sold from the five artists' tours, as in the parts' ticket question: {{m2_n}} entries from {{m2_tours}} tours in {{m2_clusters}} countries, with entries that counted online viewers left out. The outcome is the average ticket, gross divided by tickets sold, and the regressor the host country's GDP per capita in current dollars for the year of the show.

How this was done

For language, the model is log(days in the chart) = artist effect + country effect + β × same language. The country effects absorb everything about a country that makes all hits linger or pass, such as how many people use Spotify and how fast its chart turns over; the artist effects absorb how strong each focal song is everywhere. What is left to identify β is the difference, within a country, between songs in its language and songs not in it, compared with the same difference elsewhere: Spain comparing Bad Bunny with the English-language songs, the United States comparing the English-language songs with BTS and Bad Bunny. exp(β) is the multiple of days. A second version lets the effect differ for English and Spanish songs; the Korean effect rests on one country and is shown but not read.

For prices, the model is log(average ticket) = tour effect + θ × log(GDP per capita). The tour effects absorb each tour's general price level, so θ compares the cities within a tour: an elasticity of 0 means a tour charged the same everywhere, 1 that it charged in proportion to local incomes. A second version adds the venue's capacity per night. In both models the standard errors are clustered by country, which lets the errors of the same country's rows be related, since the same country appears for several artists5; the 95 % confidence intervals (CI) are built from them. The models, the coding of languages and the checks were committed before anything was estimated, but the inputs had already been seen in parts 1–5, so the results are exploratory: they show what the data say under rules fixed in advance, not a test of a hypothesis made before the data.

Language keeps a hit

A song lasted {{m1_x}} times as long in a country that speaks its language as in one that does not, holding fixed the country and the song (95 % CI {{m1_lo}}–{{m1_hi}}). Split by language, English songs in English-speaking countries lasted {{en_x}} times as long ({{en_lo}}–{{en_hi}}, {{en_pairs}} pairs), and Bad Bunny's Spanish song in Spanish-speaking countries {{es_x}} times as long ({{es_lo}}–{{es_hi}}, {{es_pairs}} pairs). The Korean estimate, {{ko_x}} times, rests on South Korea alone and says nothing on its own. The English effect is the smaller and less certain of the two. Part of the gap may be the coding: of the {{en_countries}} English-coded countries in the data, seven (Hong Kong, India, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, Singapore and South Africa) mostly speak other languages, which would pull the English estimate down. Another reading, an interpretation the data do not test, is that English is the default language of global pop: an English song is foreign in fewer places, so the gap between home and abroad is narrower.

Every check fixed in advance leaves the estimate above one, but the checks are not equally informative. Without the songs still in the chart, whose days are lower bounds, the multiple is {{ck_without_still_charting}} ({{ck_without_still_charting_lo}}–{{ck_without_still_charting_hi}}); coding BTS's Dynamite as English, which it is sung in, it is {{ck_dynamite_as_english}}; treating the United States as Spanish-speaking for Bad Bunny, because of its large Spanish-speaking population, {{ck_us_spanish_for_bad_bunny}}; and without Luxembourg, whose one-day number ones distort the parts' ratios, {{ck_without_luxembourg}}. Every interval stays above one. The smallest estimate is the one without songs still charting: those include all {{es_still}} of Bad Bunny's pairs in Spanish-speaking countries, so this check has no Spanish same-language pair at all and rests on the English songs and the one Korean pair, and it comes out close to the English-only estimate of {{en_x}}. The rise from about {{en_x}} to {{m1_x}} times in the main model comes from one Spanish song whose same-language days are all still running.

Which pairs carry the effect is easy to see in the countries where the five songs meet. In the United Kingdom the three English songs lasted {{d_gb_harry_styles}}, {{d_gb_taylor_swift}} and {{d_gb_billie_eilish}} days, BTS's {{d_gb_bts}} and Bad Bunny's {{d_gb_bad_bunny}}; in the United States the English songs between {{d_us_harry_styles}} and {{d_us_taylor_swift}} days, BTS's {{d_us_bts}}. In South Korea BTS's song lasted {{d_kr_bts}} days and Bad Bunny's {{d_kr_bad_bunny}}. The Spanish-speaking countries are the subtler case. Bad Bunny's song lasted {{d_es_bad_bunny}} days in Spain and {{d_mx_bad_bunny}} in Mexico, every day since it came out, while Harry Styles's lasted {{d_es_harry_styles}} and {{d_mx_harry_styles}}: within those countries alone, the Spanish song does not win, because it is younger and has not had time to. What sets it apart is how quickly it passed everywhere else, {{d_gb_bad_bunny}} days in the United Kingdom and {{d_cz_bad_bunny}} in Czechia. The artist effect measures a song's strength across all countries, so the same-language effect is the gap between how a song did at home and how it did abroad, compared across the five; for Bad Bunny that gap is the widest.

What the model does not show is why. Radio plays songs in the country's language; playlists are built by country; a song people understand may stay with them longer; local promotion follows the language too. The data cannot separate these, and they are all part of what "language" means here. They do show that the effect is not the familiar point that some countries love music in general, nor that some songs are simply bigger: both are held fixed.

Czechia, against the model

The model predicts how long each song should have lasted in Czechia, from how long hits last there and how strong each song was. Against that prediction, Czech listeners kept Taylor Swift's song {{cz_taylor_swift}} longer, Billie Eilish's {{cz_billie_eilish}} and Harry Styles's {{cz_harry_styles}}, and kept BTS's {{cz_bts}} and Bad Bunny's {{cz_bad_bunny}}. Because the model has a country effect, the five Czech residuals cancel out on the log scale: some songs must sit above the prediction and some below, so these say how the songs did relative to each other in Czechia, not that Czech listeners kept hits longer or shorter overall. Czech is not English, so none of the five pairs counts as same-language. Nor is the pattern Czech: in {{oth_en_wins}} of the {{oth_n}} countries that speak none of the five languages and have both kinds of song, the English songs did better against the model, on average, than the Korean and Spanish ones (a comparison added after an audit, not registered). That is the model missing something common to English songs outside English-speaking countries, and Czechia follows it. With five songs these are descriptions of five pairs, not estimates of a Czech taste.

Tickets barely follow income

Within a tour, a city ten times richer paid on average only {{ten_x}} times as much per ticket: the income elasticity of the average ticket is {{m2_th}} (95 % CI {{m2_lo}} to {{m2_hi}}, {{m2_n}} entries). Put the other way round, a country ten times poorer paid about {{ten_pct}} % less per ticket, so the same show cost its fans about {{burden_x}} times as many days of income, measured here by GDP per head, a rough stand-in for what fans earn. Adding the venue's capacity per night barely changes it ({{m2b_th}}). Dropping each artist in turn keeps it between {{drop_min}} (without {{drop_min_a}}) and {{drop_max}}. Tours do charge less in poorer countries, but far less than in proportion to income: prices are set, roughly, in dollars, with a modest discount.

Across the five artists' tours, the host countries' GDP per head, averaged over the years in which the tours played there, runs from {{inc_lo}} dollars a year in {{inc_lo_c}} to {{inc_hi}} in {{inc_hi_c}}, a ratio of about {{inc_span}}; the model itself uses each show's year. At an elasticity of {{m2_th}}, a tour that charged 100 dollars in {{inc_hi_c}} would charge about {{example_price}} dollars in {{inc_lo_c}}: a ticket {{span_price}} times cheaper in a country {{inc_span}} times poorer, and so about {{span_burden}} times dearer measured in days of income. That is the arithmetic behind the large ticket squares in parts 1, 3, 4 and 5.

The artists' estimates differ, though their intervals overlap. Bad Bunny's is the highest, {{e_bad_bunny}} ({{e_bad_bunny_lo}} to {{e_bad_bunny_hi}}), over {{e_bad_bunny_c}} countries from {{bb_lo_c}} to {{bb_hi_c}}. Harry Styles's is {{e_harry_styles}} ({{e_harry_styles_lo}} to {{e_harry_styles_hi}}) and Billie Eilish's {{e_billie_eilish}} ({{e_billie_eilish_lo}} to {{e_billie_eilish_hi}}), an interval that includes zero. BTS's estimate, {{e_bts}} ({{e_bts_lo}} to {{e_bts_hi}}), rests on {{e_bts_n}} entries in {{e_bts_c}} countries and is too uncertain to say whether its prices followed income at all; a BTS ticket in Mexico cost its fans more than a week of GDP per head. Taylor Swift's estimate, {{e_taylor_swift}} ({{e_taylor_swift_lo}} to {{e_taylor_swift_hi}}), rests on one tour of {{e_taylor_swift_c}} rich countries and has an interval too wide to read. With {{e_bts_c}} and {{e_taylor_swift_c}} countries, these two clustered intervals are also less reliable than the usual rules of thumb assume5. Every interval except Taylor Swift's stays well below one: none of the other four artists' tours came close to pricing in proportion to income.

What these models can and cannot separate

Three limits shape how far the language result goes. First, the model sees a pair only once the song entered the country's chart. Songs that never entered a chart abroad are missing, and those are mostly the ones that did worst away from their language, so the effect among songs that charted is, if anything, smaller than the effect on whether a song charts at all. Second, the language groups are tied to artists: every Spanish pair is Bad Bunny's and the only Korean pair is BTS's in Korea. The artist effects remove how strong each song is overall, but they cannot tell a language effect from something else that sets Bad Bunny's audience apart, such as the regional strength of reggaeton. Third, with {{m1_clusters}} country clusters the clustered standard errors are reliable by the usual rules of thumb5, but for the language groups they rest on fewer contrasts: {{en_pairs}} English pairs and {{es_pairs}} Spanish ones.

The price model has its own limits. Tour effects remove each tour's level, so the elasticity comes from cities within a tour; a promoter who priced a whole leg in a poorer region lower would show up partly in the tour effect, and push the estimate down. The average ticket mixes cheap and dear seats, and a tour that sold more cheap seats in poorer countries would show a lower average without changing any one price, which would push the estimate up. The data cannot say which of the two dominates. Neither model is causal: they describe how the five artists' songs and tours behaved, under rules fixed before the estimates were seen.

What changed after registration

  • The design reports β as exp(β) − 1 in percent; this page reports the same quantity as a multiple, exp(β): {{m1_x}} times is {{m1_pct}}.

What this means

Read together, the five parts say two things that none says alone. A hit lasts longer where people speak its language, about {{m1_x}} times as long once the country and the song are held fixed, though that figure is carried by one Spanish song still in the charts; for the English songs it is about {{en_x}} times. And concert tours price in something close to one currency: within a tour, prices fall only a little where incomes are low, so the same night out takes several times more days of GDP per head in the Philippines or Honduras than in Norway. Both findings are exploratory, from five artists picked for a series, and both rest on the parts' own data and limits. Every check fixed beforehand stays above one, and that is enough to make them worth testing on more artists with a design written before the data.

What this does not show

Five artists are not a sample of pop, and their languages are tied to the artists: the Spanish effect is Bad Bunny's, the Korean one BTS's in one country. Language is coded by official national language, which misses large minorities, as the check on the United States shows only in part. The model covers songs once they entered a chart, not whether they entered. Days in the chart are Spotify's and are not days in a row. The price model uses the average ticket, which mixes every seat; it cannot see resale prices, fees outside the gross, or who bought the tickets. GDP per capita is a rough stand-in for what fans earn. And neither model says why: radio, playlists, promotion, understanding and price-setting habits are all inside the estimates.
