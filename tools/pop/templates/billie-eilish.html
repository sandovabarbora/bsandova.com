Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Her two biggest hits, from 2019 and 2024, are each among the most durable number ones of the streaming era.
  • The 2024 song outlasted local hits in most countries, by most in Iceland, Switzerland and the Baltic states; in Czechia by a little less than the world's middle.
  • Her listening sits between a single-hit artist and a fan-driven catalogue: a handful of songs carry half of it.
  • Her shows sold out; where a few did not, longer runs sold a little better, but the data cannot say more.
  • A ticket in the Philippines cost more than two weeks of the average income; in Prague about one day.

This is the fifth and last artist part of Pop, measured, which asks five questions of five artists: how long the artist's biggest song lasted against every global number one, where it lasted longest, whether the listening is one song or many, whether longer runs in one venue sold less of their tickets, and what a ticket cost in days of local income. The questions draw on work on the life of chart songs1, on the shift of musicians' income to live shows2, on concert pricing3 and on streaming's effect on music sales4. Billie Eilish is the series' youngest artist and the only one whose whole career has happened on streaming: her first hits came in 2017, the year the daily chart data used here begin. That makes her the clearest case of the question at the heart of the series, how long a hit lasts when listening is counted every day, and she has two candidates for it, released five years apart.

Data

The data are the series' common collection, made once on {{kworb_date}}: for the global chart and {{kworb_charts}} national Spotify charts, every song that has entered the Top 200 with its days, peak and total streams, and the artist's page with every song's streams6. By the series rule, the focal song is her song with the most global streams, {{focal_title}} (2024); it peaked at number one. The reference set is the {{q1_n}} global number ones since 2017 whose start can be seen.

For the tours, the Wikipedia articles on the When We All Fall Asleep Tour (2019), Happier Than Ever, The World Tour (2022–2023) and Hit Me Hard and Soft: The Tour (2024–2025) list Billboard's Boxscore figures7: {{q3_entries}} entries covering {{q3_shows}} shows, nearly all in arenas, from a first tour of mid-sized halls to two arena tours. None of her entries covers several venues or counts online viewers. Income is the World Bank's GDP per capita in current dollars for the country and year8.

How this was done

The methods are those of the series, fixed before any data of this part were parsed. How long a song lasted is its total days in the global Top 200; songs still in the chart are treated as unfinished by the Kaplan–Meier estimator5, and a song's place is the share of number ones estimated to last longer, with a 95 % bootstrap interval. For countries, the focal song's days in each national chart are divided by the median days of that country's own number ones; where the song is still in the chart the ratio is a lower bound. Runs are compared with the share of tickets sold by Spearman's correlation, under the rule set in advance that if nine entries in ten sold out, the data show demand at or above capacity and the correlation is not read. Ticket prices are gross divided by tickets sold, then divided by a day of the host country's GDP per capita. All results are descriptive.

How long her hits lasted

By {{kworb_date}}, {{focal_title}} had spent {{focal_days}} days in the global Top 200 and was {{focal_still}}. {{focal_longer}} of number ones since 2017 are estimated to last longer than it has so far (95 % CI {{focal_lo}} to {{focal_hi}}), so it has outlasted about five in six. Her other number ones: {{other_ones}}. bad guy, from 2019, ended its run at almost the same place on the curve as {{focal_title}} stands now. Among the series' artists, she is the only one with two number ones that each outlasted about five in six of all the others.

The curve behind these numbers is steep at first and long at the end: half of all number ones had left the global chart by {{q1_median}} days, {{s730}} were still in after two years and {{s1000}} after a thousand days. Both of her long hits are in the long end, and {{focal_title}}, still in the chart after more than two years, may yet pass bad guy. Her shorter number ones, a Bond theme and singles from her albums, behaved like typical number ones, which is what makes the two long ones stand out.

Where it lasted

{{focal_title}} entered {{q2_countries}} national charts and outlasted the local median number one in {{q2_above}} of them. Relative to local hits it stood out most in Iceland ({{is_ratio}}×), Switzerland ({{ch_ratio}}×) and the three Baltic states, Latvia ({{lv_ratio}}×), Lithuania ({{lt_ratio}}×) and Estonia ({{ee_ratio}}×), where local number ones are short-lived; Luxembourg tops the registered ranking at {{lu_ratio}}× for the reason given in part 1, a median number one of {{lu_median}} days. By days alone it lasted longest in Australia ({{au_days}} days) and, a day behind, in the United Arab Emirates and Hong Kong ({{ae_days}}), all charts where it is still in. The pattern is close to Harry Styles's in part 1, whose biggest hit stood out in the same small European markets: English-language pop that lingers where the local chart turns over fast.

In Czechia it spent {{cz_days}} days in the chart against {{cz_median}} for the median Czech number one: {{cz_ratio}} times as long (95 % CI {{cz_lo}}–{{cz_hi}}), {{cz_rank}}th of {{q2_ranked}} countries, a little below the middle; by days alone Czechia is {{cz_rank_days}}th. Slovakia, at {{sk_ratio}}×, and Poland, at {{pl_ratio}}×, kept it longer relative to their own hits. It lasted less than the local median number one in Mexico ({{mx_ratio}}×) and in Japan ({{jp_ratio2}}×), where number ones last a median of {{jp_median}} days, and barely entered the charts of Turkey and India.

The series fixed in advance that each part would report Czechia's place, so that the five can be set side by side. Measured against the median Czech number one, Czech listeners kept Harry Styles's biggest song {{s_harry_styles_cz_ratio}} times as long ({{s_harry_styles_cz_rank}}th of {{s_harry_styles_n}} countries), Taylor Swift's {{s_taylor_swift_cz_ratio}} times ({{s_taylor_swift_cz_rank}}th of {{s_taylor_swift_n}}), BTS's {{s_bts_cz_ratio}} times ({{s_bts_cz_rank}}th of {{s_bts_n}}), Bad Bunny's {{s_bad_bunny_cz_ratio}} times ({{s_bad_bunny_cz_rank}}th of {{s_bad_bunny_n}}) and Billie Eilish's {{s_billie_eilish_cz_ratio}} times ({{s_billie_eilish_cz_rank}}th of {{s_billie_eilish_n}}). Czechia sits near the middle for the three English-language pop artists and well below it for a Korean group and a Spanish-language artist. That is the plainest result the series has about Czech listening: it is English-language and close to the world's average in how long it keeps a global hit, and it lets hits in other languages pass quickly.

One song or a catalogue

Kworb lists {{q4_songs}} songs on her Spotify page. {{q4_top}} holds {{q4_top_share}} of her streams; {{q4_second}} and {{q4_third}} follow, and the top three together hold {{q4_top3}}. Half of all her streams come from {{q4_half}} songs, and {{q4_over1}} songs each hold one percent or more. The Gini coefficient over songs is {{q4_gini}}. That places her between the series' other artists: Harry Styles's half came from six songs, BTS's from 41 and Bad Bunny's from 46. A handful of her songs, from every album, carry most of her listening, which matches what the first question found: her durability is spread over several songs, not held by one.

How many nights a city can fill

Her three tours, {{q3_tours}}, played {{q3_shows}} shows in {{q3_entries}} Boxscore entries. The longest run was {{long_nights}} nights at {{long_venue}} in {{long_city}}. {{q3_sold_out}} of entries sold 99.5 % of their tickets or more, which crosses the threshold set in advance: demand was at or above capacity nearly everywhere, and the data cannot say how far above. Across her entries, longer runs sold a slightly larger share: the correlation between nights and the share sold is {{q3_rho}} (95 % CI {{q3_lo}} to {{q3_hi}}), an interval that excludes zero. Under the registered rule it is reported and not read further, because with nine entries in ten at capacity it rests on the few that were not. If anything, it points away from the idea that a city tires of a long run. The lowest share, {{q3_min}}, was in {{least_city}} on {{least_tour}} in {{least_year}}. The median entry grossed {{q3_rev_night}} million dollars a night.

Gross divided by tickets sold rose with each tour, from {{tp_when_we_all_fall_asleep_tour}} dollars a ticket on the When We All Fall Asleep Tour in 2019 to {{tp_happier_than_ever_the_world_tour}} on Happier Than Ever and {{tp_hit_me_hard_and_soft_the_tour}} on Hit Me Hard and Soft, as she moved from halls to arenas; the tickets still sold out.

What a ticket cost, in days of income

Across {{q5_countries}} countries, the average ticket cost {{q5_top_days}} days of the local GDP per capita in the {{q5_top}} ({{q5_top_price}} dollars), the highest figure in the series, and {{q5_second_days}} in {{q5_second}}; Malaysia followed at {{t_mys_days}} and Mexico at {{t_mex_days}}. At the other end, in {{q5_last}}, the average ticket of {{q5_last_price}} dollars was {{q5_last_days}} of a day. In {{q5_over2}} countries a ticket cost more than two days of income, all of them in South-East Asia or Latin America; in {{q5_under1}} it cost less than one. The cheapest ticket in dollars was in {{cheap}}, {{cheap_price}} dollars, on her first tour, and the dearest {{dear_price}}: a ratio of {{q5_price_x}}, against about {{q5_days_x}}-fold in days of income.

She played Prague at the {{prague_venue}} on {{prague_date}}: {{prague_sold}} tickets sold and {{prague_rev}} dollars gross, an average of {{t_cze_price}} dollars, {{t_cze_days}} days of Czech income. That is more, relative to income, than in any western European country she played and less than in Poland ({{t_pol_days}}). Harry Styles's Prague show of 2022, in part 1, cost an average of {{s_harry_styles_cz_tprice}} dollars, {{s_harry_styles_cz_tdays}} of a day of that year's Czech income: in three years and one artist later, the same hall charged a Czech fan noticeably more of a day's income. Two shows are not a trend, and the artists differ, but the direction matches the rise in her own prices from tour to tour. GDP per capita is not a fan's income, and the people at an arena show earn more than the average; the figure shows what the price was set against rather than what each fan felt.

What changed after registration

  • 2 October 2026: two data definitions were added to the series design after the results of parts 2–5 were first computed, one for attendance figures that cover several venues and one for entries that count online viewers. Neither applies to any Billie Eilish entry.
  • Countries are also ranked by days alone, as in part 1, because small markets with many one-day number ones inflate the ratio.

What this means

Measured: two of Billie Eilish's songs, five years apart, each outlasted about five in six global number ones, and the newer one is still in the chart. It outlasted local hits in most countries, by most in small European markets, and in Czechia a little less than the world's middle. Her listening is carried by a handful of songs rather than one or hundreds. Her shows sold out, and the same arena ticket cost Filipino fans more than two weeks of the average income. Across the series, she is the artist whose staying power is least tied to one release: a durable hit is a pattern in her work rather than a single event.

What this does not show

Spotify is one service among several. Days in the chart are not days in a row, and the countries' charts started at different dates; where the song is still in the chart, its days and ratios are lower bounds. Nothing here says why a song lasted, and the Bond theme and album singles were released in different ways from the long hits. A sold-out show hides how many more wanted tickets. GDP per capita is a rough stand-in for what fans earn, and in countries where incomes are spread widely, such as the Philippines, the people at an arena earn well above the average, so the gap between countries overstates what fans at the shows felt.
