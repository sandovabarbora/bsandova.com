  • His 2025 album's title song has been in the chart of almost every Spanish-speaking country every day since it came out.
  • He has more global number ones than anyone else in the series, and his listening is spread over dozens of songs rather than one.

This is the fourth part of Pop, measured, which asks five questions of five artists: how long the artist's biggest song lasted against every global number one, where it lasted longest, whether the listening is one song or many, whether longer runs in one venue sold less of their tickets, and what a ticket cost in days of local income. The questions draw on work on the life of chart songs1, on the shift of musicians' income to live shows2, on concert pricing3 and on streaming's effect on music sales4. Bad Bunny is the series' artist who sings in Spanish, and the most streamed artist on Spotify in several recent years. That makes two questions sharper than for the others: whether a hit in Spanish lasts as long outside the Spanish-speaking world as inside it, and what his tickets cost in the Latin American countries that make up much of his audience and where incomes are lower than in the United States or Europe.

Data

The data are the series' common collection, made once on {{kworb_date}}: for the global chart and {{kworb_charts}} national Spotify charts, every song that has entered the Top 200 with its days, peak and total streams, and the artist's page with every song's streams6. By the series rule, the focal song is his song with the most global streams that has a full record, {{focal_title}}, the title song of his 2025 album Debí Tirar Más Fotos; it peaked at number one. The reference set is the {{q1_n}} global number ones since 2017 whose start can be seen.

For the tours, the Wikipedia articles on the X 100pre Tour (2019), the World's Hottest Tour (2022) and the Debí Tirar Más Fotos World Tour (2025–2026) list Billboard's Boxscore figures7: {{q3_entries}} entries covering {{q3_shows}} shows. His 2025 residency in San Juan, No Me Quiero Ir de Aquí, lists 31 shows but no tickets or gross, and by the series rule nothing is taken from other sources, so it is not in the tour results. Two other tours on the list, of 2022 and 2024, have no figures at all. Income is the World Bank's GDP per capita in current dollars for the country and year8; Puerto Rico has its own series there.

How this was done

The methods are those of the series, fixed before any data of this part were parsed. How long a song lasted is its total days in the global Top 200; songs still in the chart are treated as unfinished by the Kaplan–Meier estimator5, and a song's place is the share of number ones estimated to last longer, with a 95 % bootstrap interval. For countries, the focal song's days in each national chart are divided by the median days of that country's own number ones; where the song is still in the chart the ratio is a lower bound. Runs are compared with the share of tickets sold by Spearman's correlation, under the rule set in advance that if nine entries in ten sold out, the data show demand at or above capacity and nothing more. Ticket prices are gross divided by tickets sold, then divided by a day of the host country's GDP per capita. All results are descriptive.

How long {{focal_title}} lasted

By {{kworb_date}}, {{focal_title}} had spent {{focal_days}} days in the global Top 200 and was {{focal_still}}. {{focal_longer}} of number ones since 2017 are estimated to last longer than it has so far (95 % CI {{focal_lo}} to {{focal_hi}}), and because it has not left, that share can only fall. The median number one lasted {{q1_median}} days. He has more global number ones than any other artist in the series: {{other_ones}}. Dakiti and DÁKITI appear to be the same 2020 song under two track entries, one short-lived and one that kept charting. Most of his number ones lasted about as long as a typical one, and the two that lasted longest are the ones that became part of what people play every week.

The curve behind these numbers is steep at first and long at the end: half of all number ones had left the global chart by {{q1_median}} days, {{s730}} were still in after two years and {{s1000}} after a thousand days. {{focal_title}} is already well into the long end, and it will go on moving along it while it stays in the chart. What makes it unusual is not only how long it has lasted globally, but how it has lasted in the countries where he is strongest.

Where it lasted

{{focal_title}} entered {{q2_countries}} national charts. In most of Latin America and in Spain it has stayed in the Top 200 every day since it came out: {{co_days}} days in Colombia, {{mx_days}} in Mexico, {{es_days}} in Spain, the most a song released in January 2025 could have by October 2026. Because it is still in those charts, its ratio to the local median number one is a lower bound there, and it is held down by how long Latin American number ones last anyway, {{mx_median}} days in Mexico and {{co_median}} in Colombia. The countries where it stood out most against local hits were therefore elsewhere: the Dominican Republic ({{do_ratio}}×), Switzerland ({{ch_ratio}}×), the United States ({{us_ratio}}×) and Italy ({{it_ratio}}×). Luxembourg tops the registered ranking at {{lu_ratio}}× for the reason given in part 1: hundreds of songs there have been number one for a single day, so its median number one lasted {{lu_median}} days.

It lasted longer than the local median number one in {{q2_above}} of the {{q2_countries}} countries. Where it did not, the gap is wide. In the United Kingdom it spent {{gb_days}} days in the chart, {{gb_ratio}}× the local median; in Brazil, where Portuguese-language and local genres lead, {{br_ratio}}×; in Czechia {{cz_days}} days against a median number one of {{cz_median}}, {{cz_ratio}} times as long (95 % CI {{cz_lo}}–{{cz_hi}}), {{cz_rank}}th of {{q2_ranked}}. Slovakia, at {{sk_ratio}}×, and Poland, at {{pl_ratio}}×, kept it longer than Czechia did. It did least well in South Korea, Turkey and Pakistan, where it barely entered. The pattern is linguistic and regional more than it is about reach: a Spanish-language album held the charts of the Spanish-speaking world and of the United States, with its large Latin audience, and passed more quickly through markets where neither the language nor the genre is local.

One song or a catalogue

Kworb lists {{q4_songs}} songs on his Spotify page. The biggest, {{q4_top}}, holds only {{q4_top_share}} of his streams; {{q4_second}} and {{q4_third}} follow, and the top three together hold {{q4_top3}}. Half of all his streams come from {{q4_half}} songs, and {{q4_over1}} songs each hold one percent or more. For Harry Styles in part 1, six songs made up half; for BTS in part 3, 41. The Gini coefficient over songs is {{q4_gini}}. Bad Bunny's listening is spread over a large catalogue, much of it collaborations and album tracks that people keep playing, rather than concentrated on singles. Some of those songs are other artists' with him featured, which the page counts as his; that is how much of reggaeton is released, and it inflates the count of songs but not the shape of the spread.

How many nights a city can fill

His three tours with figures, {{q3_tours}}, played {{q3_shows}} shows in {{q3_entries}} Boxscore entries. The longest run was {{long_nights}} nights at the {{long_venue}} in {{long_city}} in 2026, ten sold-out nights in one stadium. {{q3_sold_out}} of entries sold 99.5 % of their tickets or more, which crosses the threshold set in advance: demand was at or above capacity nearly everywhere, and the data cannot say how far above. The correlation between nights and the share sold is {{q3_rho}} (95 % CI {{q3_lo}} to {{q3_hi}}), reported and not read further. The lowest share, {{q3_min}}, was in {{least_city}} on the {{least_tour}} of 2019, before his first number-one album. The median entry grossed {{q3_rev_night}} million dollars a night. The 31 nights in San Juan would have been the clearest test of how many nights one city can fill; without their figures, the question stops at ten.

What a ticket cost, in days of income

Across {{q5_countries}} countries, the average ticket cost {{q5_top_days}} days of the local GDP per capita in {{q5_top}} ({{q5_top_price}} dollars) and {{q5_second_days}} in {{q5_second}}; Peru, Colombia, Ecuador and El Salvador were each near six days, Mexico {{t_mex_days}} and Brazil {{t_bra_days}}. In {{q5_over2}} countries a ticket cost more than two days of income, all of them in Latin America. At home in Puerto Rico the average ticket was {{t_pri_price}} dollars, {{t_pri_days}} of a day of Puerto Rican income; in the United States {{t_usa_price}} dollars, {{t_usa_days}} day. The cheapest ticket in dollars was in {{cheap}}, {{cheap_price}} dollars, and the dearest {{dear_price}}: a ratio of {{q5_price_x}}, against about {{q5_days_x}}-fold in days of income.

Prices also rose across his tours: gross divided by tickets sold gives {{tp_x_100pre_tour}} dollars a ticket on the X 100pre Tour in 2019, {{tp_world_s_hottest_tour}} on the World's Hottest Tour in 2022 and {{tp_deb_tirar_m_s_fotos_world_tour}} on the Debí Tirar Más Fotos World Tour, which played more of Latin America and Europe. The result that matters most for his audience is the first: every country where a ticket cost more than two days of income was in Latin America, the region where his listeners are most concentrated. GDP per capita is not a fan's income, and the people at a stadium show earn more than the average; the figure shows what the price was set against rather than what each fan felt.

What changed after registration

  • 2 October 2026: two data definitions were added to the series design after the results of parts 2–5 were first computed, one for attendance figures that cover several venues and one for entries that count online viewers. Neither applies to any Bad Bunny entry.
  • Countries are also ranked by days alone, as in part 1, because small markets with many one-day number ones inflate the ratio.

What this means

Measured: {{focal_title}} has outlasted all but {{focal_longer}} of global number ones and is still in the chart, held in place by the Spanish-speaking world, where it has not left any chart since it came out. Bad Bunny has the most number ones in the series and one of its most spread catalogues, with half his streams in {{q4_half}} songs. Outside Latin America, Spain and the United States his hit lasted less than local hits, in Czechia about a quarter as long. His tours sold out, including ten nights in one stadium, and in every country where a ticket cost more than two days of income, the country was in Latin America. Read with parts 1 and 3, the series now shows three different shapes of success: a broad hit outlasting local songs almost everywhere, a fan base spreading its listening over a catalogue, and a language region holding one album in place.

What this does not show

Spotify is one service, strong in Latin America but not alone there, and YouTube matters more for reggaeton than for most genres. Days in the chart are not days in a row, and the countries' charts started at different dates. Where the song is still in the chart, its days and ratios are lower bounds. Nothing here says why a song lasted. The San Juan residency is missing from the tour results for lack of figures, and a sold-out show hides how many more wanted tickets. GDP per capita is a rough stand-in for what fans earn, and in countries where incomes are spread widely, the gap between countries overstates what fans at the shows felt.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from assets/pop/bad-bunny/results.json by a script. From the repository root: tools/pop/collect.py and collect_extra.py collect the raw files; parse.py bad-bunny, analyse.py bad-bunny, figures.py bad-bunny and article.py bad-bunny build the tables, estimates, figures and this page.
