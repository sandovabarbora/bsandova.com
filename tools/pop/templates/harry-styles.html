Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • His biggest hit is among the most durable songs of the streaming era, and it is still in the chart.
  • It lasted longer than the local typical number one almost everywhere; Czech listeners were exactly as loyal as the world's middle.
  • He is not a one-song artist: the hit is a minority of his streams, and two other songs come close to it.
  • His shows sold out nearly everywhere, so the data cannot say how many more would have bought; the same ticket weighed very differently on fans in poorer countries.
  • All of it is Spotify and Boxscore only, and none of it says why.

How long a hit lasts has been measured since the charts were printed on paper. Bradlow and Fader modelled the life of songs on Billboard's Hot 100 as a rise and a decay1, and Connolly and Krueger showed how much of a musician's income moved from records to concerts, and how unevenly it is spread among artists2. Streaming changed both halves of that story. A song now stays in the chart for as long as people keep playing it, so the chart measures listening rather than sales, and streaming displaced much of what used to be bought4. Concert prices, meanwhile, are set by a few promoters in one currency for very different audiences, and Courty and Pagliero describe how much of the pricing happens in ways a fan never sees3. This series, Pop, measured, asks the same five questions of five artists, so that the parts can be read side by side. Part 1 is Harry Styles: how long his biggest song lasted against every other global number one, in which countries it lasted longest, whether his songs are one hit or a catalogue, whether longer runs in one venue sold less of their tickets, and what an average ticket cost in days of the local income.

Data

Spotify publishes a daily chart of the 200 most-streamed songs for the world and for each country. Kworb.net keeps a running total for every chart: each song that has ever entered, how many days it spent in the Top 200, its peak position and its total streams6. These totals were collected once, on {{kworb_date}}, for the global chart and {{kworb_charts}} national charts, together with the charts of that day, which show which songs are still in. The global chart has had {{q1_n}} number ones since 2017 that can be followed from their start; {{q1_excluded}} more were already in the chart when it began, so their start is not seen and they are left out.

For his tours, the Wikipedia articles on Harry Styles: Live on Tour (2018) and Love On Tour (2021–2023) list Billboard's Boxscore figures: for each run of shows in one venue, the tickets sold, the tickets available and the gross in US dollars78. A run of several nights is reported as one entry; the {{q3_entries}} entries cover {{q3_shows}} shows. Income is the World Bank's GDP per capita in current US dollars, for the country and year of the show9. Every raw file is listed with its SHA-256 hash.

How this was done

The questions, measures and labels were written down and committed before any chart day or ticket count was collected, and the series design for the other four artists followed before any data were parsed. How long a song lasted is its total number of days in the global Top 200, not its days in a row: a song that left and came back counts both spells. Songs still in the chart on the collection day have not finished, so their days are a lower bound; the Kaplan–Meier estimator handles that, treating them as censored rather than as ended5. {{q1_censored}} of the {{q1_n}} number ones were still charting. Where a song stands is the share of number ones estimated to last longer than it did, with a 95 % interval from 2 000 bootstrap resamples of songs.

For countries, the same song's days in each national chart are divided by the median days of that country's own number ones, so that a market where every hit lingers is not mistaken for one that loves this artist in particular. The interval comes from resampling the country's number ones. For the tours, the measure is the share of tickets sold, compared with the number of nights in the run by Spearman's correlation. The rule for reading it was fixed in advance: if nine entries in ten or more sold out, the data show demand at or above capacity and cannot say how far above. For tickets, the average price of a country's entries is divided by a day of its GDP per capita. Nothing here is causal, and nothing is tested across artists.

How long {{focal_title}} lasted

{{focal_title}} had spent {{focal_days}} days in the global Top 200 by {{kworb_date}}, and it was still there. The typical number one since 2017 lasted {{q1_median}} days. Only {{focal_longer}} of number ones are estimated to last longer than {{focal_title}} has so far (95 % CI {{focal_lo}} to {{focal_hi}}): about one in {{focal_one_in}}. Because it has not left, that share can only fall. His other number ones did not last: {{other_ones}}. The interval is wide at the top because few songs last this long, so a handful of them decide where the line falls.

The curve behind these numbers is steep at first and long at the end. Half of all number ones had left the global chart by {{q1_median}} days, and the share still in falls to {{s730}} at two years and {{s1000}} at a thousand days. Most number ones are a season; a few become part of what people play every week for years, and the chart cannot tell, on the day a song reaches the top, which kind it will be. A number one that is still in the chart after a thousand days has stopped behaving like a new release.

Where it lasted

{{focal_title}} entered the national charts of {{q2_countries}} countries. In {{q2_above}} of them it lasted longer than the country's median number one, and the middle country kept it about {{q2_median_ratio}} times as long. By days alone it lasted longest in {{q2_top_days}}. Relative to their own number ones, it outlasted the local hits most in {{q2_top_ratio}}, once a small-market effect is set aside: in Luxembourg {{lu_ones}} songs have been number one, many for a single day, so its median number one lasted {{lu_median}} days and the ratio reaches {{lu_ratio}}×. That number is in the registered ranking and in the chart, but it says more about Luxembourg's chart than about its listeners. The song did least well against local hits in {{q2_low}}, two markets where domestic music fills the top of the chart.

The pattern in the middle of the table is regional. The three Baltic states rank {{lv_rank}}th to {{ee_rank}}th, with Latvia at {{lv_ratio}}×, Lithuania at {{lt_ratio}}× and Estonia at {{ee_ratio}}×: the song stayed about as long there as elsewhere, around {{lv_days}} days in Latvia, but local number ones are short-lived, with medians near {{lv_median}} days. Mexico shows the opposite case. {{focal_title}} spent {{mx_days}} days in the Mexican chart, more than in most countries, yet Mexican number ones themselves last a median of {{mx_median}} days, so the ratio is only {{mx_ratio}}×. Japan's number ones last longest of all, a median of {{jp_median}} days, which is why a song that spent {{jp_days}} days there ranks last. The ratio is a way of asking how much a song stood out against what that country's listeners usually keep playing, not how much it was played.

In Czechia {{focal_title}} spent {{cz_days}} days in the chart against {{cz_median}} for the median Czech number one: {{cz_ratio}} times as long (95 % CI {{cz_lo}}–{{cz_hi}}), {{cz_rank}}th of {{q2_ranked}} countries, almost exactly the world's middle. Slovakia, which shares much of its music with Czechia, ranks {{sk_rank}}th at {{sk_ratio}}×. By days alone, added after the results because of the Luxembourg effect, Czechia is {{cz_rank_days}}th.

One song or a catalogue

Kworb lists {{q4_songs}} songs by Harry Styles on Spotify. {{q4_top}} is the biggest, with {{q4_top_share}} of all their streams; {{q4_second}} follows with {{q4_second_share}} and {{q4_third}} is third. The top three together hold {{q4_top3}}. The Gini coefficient over songs is {{q4_gini}}, where 0 would mean every song streamed equally and 1 that one song took everything. The list counts every song on his page, including the few where he is a featured artist, and it counts only Spotify. What it shows is that his biggest hit is large but not the whole of him: two other songs each have more than half its streams.

How many nights a city can fill

{{q3_tours}} played {{q3_shows}} shows in {{q3_entries}} Boxscore entries. The longest run was {{long_nights}} nights at {{long_venue}} in {{long_city}}, which sold all {{long_sold}} tickets it offered. {{q3_sold_out}} of all entries sold 99.5 % of their tickets or more, which crosses the threshold set in advance: the data show demand at or above capacity nearly everywhere and cannot say how far above. The correlation between nights and the share sold is {{q3_rho}} (95 % CI {{q3_lo}} to {{q3_hi}}), reported as registered and not read further, because when almost everything sells out there is nothing left for it to measure. The lowest share, {{q3_min}}, was in {{least_city}} on {{least_tour}}, before his second album. The median entry grossed {{q3_rev_night}} million dollars per night.

What a ticket cost, in days of income

Dividing each entry's gross by its tickets sold gives the average price paid, every seat and fee together. Across {{q5_countries}} countries, measured in days of the local GDP per capita, the same tour cost {{q5_top_days}} days per ticket in the {{q5_top}} (an average of {{q5_top_price}} dollars) and {{q5_second_days}} in {{q5_second}}, against {{q5_last_days}} in {{q5_last}} ({{q5_last_price}} dollars). In dollars the dearest tickets were in {{dear}}, at {{dear_price}} dollars on average; in the United States they averaged {{us_price}} dollars, yet that was {{us_days}} of a day of American income. In {{q5_over2}} countries a ticket cost more than two days of income, most of them in Latin America and South-East Asia; in {{q5_under1}} it cost less than one. Poland paid {{pl_price}} dollars, {{pl_days}} days.

The prices themselves were close together. The cheapest average ticket, in {{cheap}}, was {{cheap_price}} dollars and the dearest {{dear_price}}: a ratio of {{q5_price_x}} from one end to the other. Measured against income, the same span is about {{q5_days_x}}-fold. Most of the difference between what a fan paid in the {{q5_top}} and in {{q5_last}} is not in the price but in what the price was worth where it was paid.

Prague had one show, at the {{prague_venue}} on {{prague_date}}. It sold all {{prague_sold}} tickets and grossed {{prague_rev}} dollars, an average of {{cz_price}} dollars, {{cz_tdays}} of a day of Czech income. GDP per capita is not a fan's income, and in countries where incomes are spread widely the people at a stadium show earn more than the average, so the gap between countries overstates what the fans themselves felt. It does show who the price was set for: one tour, priced in dollars, landed on very different wallets.

What changed after registration

  • 1 October 2026, before any data were parsed: the tours were widened from Love On Tour to every tour since 2017 with Boxscore figures, to match the series (e890c27), and the catalogue and ticket questions were added for all five parts (12417ae).
  • 1 October 2026, after the results: countries are also ranked by days alone, because small markets with many one-day number ones inflate the registered ratio. The registered ratio and its ranking are reported unchanged (ebf819f).
  • 2 October 2026, before publication: a parsing error was corrected. The 2017–2018 tour table gives the year in each date rather than in its caption, so its 74 entries had been joined to the latest GDP per capita instead of their own year's. Only the ticket results changed; the first results commit keeps the error for comparison.

What this means

Measured: {{focal_title}} is among the few most durable number ones of the streaming era, with {{focal_longer}} of them estimated to last longer, and the figure can only fall while it stays in the chart. It outlasted the local typical hit in {{q2_above}} of {{q2_countries}} countries, and Czech listeners sat at the world's middle. His streams are spread over several songs rather than one. His shows sold out almost everywhere, so how many nights a city could fill is above what was offered. The clearest difference the data show is in price: the same tour cost a ticket-buyer in the {{q5_top}} {{q5_top_days}} days of the average income and one in {{q5_last}} a fraction of a day. What this part cannot say is whether any of it is unusual; that is what the next four parts are for.

What this does not show

Spotify is one service among several, strongest in Europe and the Americas, so the charts describe its listeners and not a country's music market. Days in the chart are not days in a row, and a song that returned each Christmas would look as durable as one that never left. The countries' charts started at different dates, and a country that joined late has fewer and younger number ones in its median. Nothing here says why a song lasted: release timing, playlists, radio and short videos are not measured. Boxscore reports runs together, so a single night's sales are not known, and a sold-out show hides how many more people wanted tickets. GDP per capita is a rough stand-in for what fans earn, and the gross mixes every seat price and fee at the exchange rate used when it was reported.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from assets/pop/harry-styles/results.json by a script, so none is typed by hand. Run the scripts from the repository root in this order; raw files go to tools/data/pop/, which is not committed, and their hashes are in docs/research/pop-measured-files.sha256.

  1. tools/pop/collect.py and collect_extra.py: the chart totals, tour articles, World Bank income and artist song lists, slowly.
  2. parse.py harry-styles: the published tables in docs/research/harry-styles-*.csv.
  3. analyse.py harry-styles: the registered estimates.
  4. figures.py harry-styles and article.py harry-styles: the figures and this page.

The designs are docs/research/harry-styles-design.md and, for the series, pop-measured-design.md.
