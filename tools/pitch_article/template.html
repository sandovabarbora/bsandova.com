Pitch Perfect (2012) and Pitch Perfect 2 (2015) are comedies about a university a cappella group[[pp1]][[pp2]]. This study asked three questions of their jokes: whether the second film aims a larger share of its jokes at America (Q1); whether jokes about a protected trait, such as gender or race, laugh at the person who tells them or at the group (Q2); and whether the second film has more jokes that would not pass today (Q3). “Would not pass today” is this study's name for a joke structure, a joke at a protected trait whose laugh falls on the group; it is not a measure of today's taste. A language model found and coded the jokes in the films' English subtitles, as recent studies have used such models to annotate text in place of human coders[[gilardi]][[ziems]], and its codes were checked against a blind sample coded by the author, by Cohen's κ[[cohen]]. The design fixed the questions, the codebook, the readings and a gate for that agreement before any subtitle file was read.

The check failed. On the {{code_cues}} sampled cues that both the model and the author called part of a joke, their agreement on the target was κ {{k_t}} (95 % CI {{k_t_ci}}, bootstrap by cue) and on the butt κ {{k_b}} ({{k_b_ci}}), against a registered gate of {{gate}} on both. The model agreed with itself far better: in a second, independent run over both films, κ on the target was {{sa_t}} and on the butt {{sa_b}}, on {{sa_cues}} cues. The model is consistent, but consistent with a reading of the jokes that the one human coder did not share. The study is therefore labelled exploratory, and every result below describes what this model found; none is a validated measurement of the films.

Data

The author supplied English subtitle files, of the kind written for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, from her own copies of both films; their hashes were committed before any model run. The subtitles are the films' scripts, so they are not published and this page quotes none of them: the repository holds counts, cue numbers and codes only. After the registered cleaning, Pitch Perfect kept {{cues1}} of {{raw1}} cues and Pitch Perfect 2 {{cues2}} of {{raw2}}; the {{sound1}} and {{sound2}} cues dropped were left empty once sound descriptions and formatting were removed. Neither file marks song lyrics with ♪ or #, and neither has a run of three or more wholly italic cues, the registered fallback, so no line was removed as a lyric: whatever sung text the files carry stayed in and could be coded as a joke.

Cues were grouped into scenes wherever a gap of more than {{gap}} seconds separated them: {{scenes1}} scenes and {{hours1}} hours of dialogue in the first film, {{scenes2_all}} scenes in the second. One scene of the second film, with {{failed_cues2}} cues, failed twice in the model's first pass and was dropped by the registered rule, leaving {{scenes2}} scenes and {{hours2}} hours of dialogue. The full runtimes are {{rt1}} and {{rt2}} hours.

How this was done

The model worked in two passes. The first gave it one scene at a time and asked for every joke, as a span of cues, and whether it was a joke or an allusion to something outside the film. The second gave it one joke at a time, inside its scene, and asked for three codes from the registered codebook: the target (a group of people, America, the genre of musicals and showbusiness, or a character or situation); for a group, its type; and the butt, whether the laugh falls on the speaker, on the target, or cannot be told from the text. Seven group types count as protected traits: gender, race, weight, sexuality, nationality, religion and disability. Every example in the codebook is invented. One of them, a man saying he is naturally excellent at directions while walking into a lake, is a gender joke with the butt on the speaker (an invented example, not from either film). The prompts name neither film.

The model was claude-sonnet-5-5 at effort high. It ran through the Claude Code command-line client, version {{cli}}, under the author's account, not through the API as registered, because no API key was available; each call was a fresh process with the study's prompt and no tools, settings or memory, and the client's own default thinking. This is a dated deviation in the change log, with the one that let a run stopped by the account's usage limit resume. Both full runs used identical settings. Run 1 is the run of record and found {{jokes1}} jokes in Pitch Perfect ({{allus1}} of them allusions) and {{jokes2}} in Pitch Perfect 2 ({{allus2}}); run 2, which measures how far the model agrees with itself, found {{r2_jokes1}} and {{r2_jokes2}}.

Every interval is a 95 % percentile interval from {{reps}} bootstrap replicates that resample scenes within each film, because the jokes of one scene share a set-up. The effective sample is {{scenes1}} and {{scenes2}} scenes, and for the questions on protected traits {{prot1}} and {{prot2}} jokes. A difference whose interval includes zero is read inconclusive.

Does the model code jokes as a person would?

Before any model run, the code drew {{val_cues}} kept cues, half from each film, and the author coded each highlighted cue without seeing any model output: part of a joke or not, and if so its target and butt. She coded the first 50 in the form's random order and stopped there, and 3 of those she could not code; so {{skipped}} cues are skipped and the validation rests on {{coded}} coded cues, not {{val_cues}}. The shortening was recorded before any model run, and the gate was not changed.

On whether a cue was part of a joke, the two agreed on {{det_pa}} of the {{coded}} cues, κ {{det_k}}. The author marked {{a_jokes}} joke cues and the model {{m_jokes}}; the model found {{found}} of hers, and she marked {{marked}} of the model's. Target and butt can be compared only on the {{code_cues}} cues that both called a joke. There, agreement on the target was barely above chance and, even at the top of its interval, far below the gate. The interval for the butt reaches above the gate, so on {{code_cues}} cues the butt agreement is too imprecise to settle either way. The results file does not break these cues down by target, so agreement on protected-trait jokes, the cases Q2 and Q3 rest on, is not measured on its own.

The model's high agreement with itself shows that it applies the codebook the same way twice. It does not show that this way is right, and one coder's codes do not show that it is wrong: the author is a single coder, who had seen both films several times and stated her expectations before the data. The subtitles also leave out the delivery, the look or the cut that often decides on whom a laugh falls.

Q1: jokes at America

In run 1 the model coded {{am1_n}} of the {{jokes1}} jokes of Pitch Perfect as aimed at America, {{am1}} (95 % CI {{am1_ci}}, bootstrap by scene), and {{am2_n}} of the {{jokes2}} of Pitch Perfect 2, {{am2}} ({{am2_ci}}). The registered reading is inconclusive: the two films aim a similar share of jokes at America, or differ by less than this sample can tell, {{d1}} (95 % CI {{d1_ci}}, bootstrap by scene). A difference of about {{mdd1}} would have been detected four times in five. Run 2 gives the same reading, {{r2_d1}} ({{r2_d1_ci}}). Exploratory.

Q2: on whom the laugh falls in jokes on protected traits

The model coded {{prot1}} jokes of Pitch Perfect and {{prot2}} of Pitch Perfect 2 as aimed at a protected trait, most often gender ({{gender1}} and {{gender2}}). In run 1 the laugh fell on the speaker in {{pb1_s}} of the first film's {{prot1}}, on the group in {{pb1_g}} and was unclear in {{pb1_u}}; in the second film in {{pb2_s}}, {{pb2_g}} and {{pb2_u}} of {{prot2}}. D, the share on the speaker minus the share on the group, was {{D1}} in Pitch Perfect (95 % CI {{D1_ci}}, bootstrap by scene) and {{D2}} in Pitch Perfect 2 ({{D2_ci}}). Both intervals lie above zero, which by the registered reading means that in each film jokes on protected traits more often laugh at the speaker than at the group. For Pitch Perfect this does not hold across runs: in run 2 the model put the laugh on the speaker in {{r2_pb1_s}} and on the group in {{r2_pb1_g}} of {{r2_prot1}} such jokes, D {{r2_D1}} (95 % CI {{r2_D1_ci}}), an interval that includes zero and would read inconclusive. For Pitch Perfect 2 run 2 gives {{r2_D2}} ({{r2_D2_ci}}). Both readings rest on about twenty jokes per film and on butt codes that agreed with the author at κ {{k_b}}, so they are exploratory. The difference of D between the films, {{Ddiff}} (95 % CI {{Ddiff_ci}}), carries no reading by design.

Q3: jokes that would not pass today

In run 1 the model coded {{wnpt1}} jokes in Pitch Perfect and {{wnpt2}} in Pitch Perfect 2 as aimed at a protected trait with the laugh on the group, {{rate1}} and {{rate2}} per hour of dialogue (95 % CI {{rate1_ci}} and {{rate2_ci}}). The registered reading is inconclusive: the two films have a similar rate of jokes that would not pass today, or differ by less than this sample can tell, {{d3}} per hour (95 % CI {{d3_ci}}). A difference of about {{mdd3}} per hour would have been detected four times in five. In run 2 the model counted {{r2_wnpt1}} and {{r2_wnpt2}} such jokes, and the difference was {{r2_d3}} per hour ({{r2_d3_ci}}), larger but still inconclusive: at counts this small, a few jokes coded differently move a rate by several per hour. Exploratory.

Checks

The design registered checks that repeat the questions with one thing changed (§7); the results hold {{n_checks}} of them. Only one changes a reading: run 2 in place of run 1, for D in Pitch Perfect, as above. Counting every unclear butt as on the group, or every one as on the speaker, leaves D above zero in both films; so do a scene gap of 5 or 12 seconds, leaving out allusions, and widening the protected traits to every group type. Q1 and Q3 stay inconclusive in every check, including with the full runtime as the denominator of Q3. Counted per hour of dialogue instead of as a share, jokes at America were {{amr1}} and {{amr2}} per hour, a difference of {{amr_d}} per hour (95 % CI {{amr_d_ci}}). A check does not relabel a result.

The author's expectation

On 27 October 2026, before any subtitle was read, the author wrote down what she expected: yes on Q1, a larger share of jokes at America in the second film; mostly satire on Q2, the laugh on the speaker; and yes on Q3, more jokes that would not pass today in the second film. Q1 and Q3 came out inconclusive. Q2 came out as she expected in run 1, but not stably in the first film, and on codes that did not pass the agreement gate. She was also the blind coder, so her expectations could shape her own codes, though not the model's.

What this means

For anyone who wants to use a language model to code humour, the decision-relevant result is the agreement: a model run twice agreed with itself on the target of a joke at κ {{sa_t}}, and with one human coder at κ {{k_t}} on {{code_cues}} cues. Self-agreement is a check on noise, not on validity; a model can be reliably different from a person. On the films, the measured quantities are shares and rates of the model's codes: about one joke in thirteen at America in both, {{wnpt1}} jokes that would not pass today in each, and, in run 1, more laughs on the speaker than on the group among about twenty protected-trait jokes per film, a reading that the second run did not repeat for the first film. None of this supports a claim that one film is more or less offensive than the other, or than any other comedy.

What this does not show

It does not show what the writers meant, how audiences took the jokes, or any effect of the films; a difference between the films would be an association with the film, not caused by its year or director. It does not measure today's standards: “would not pass today” is a label for a joke structure. Subtitles are not the performance: tone, timing, visual gags and songs carry much of the humour, and jokes with no line are not in the data. Joke segmentation shapes every denominator, and the two runs differed by {{seg_diff1}} and {{seg_diff2}} jokes. The coding model may know the films from its training and code a line by what it knows of the scene. Pitch Perfect 3 (2017) and a comparison with comedies of today are left to a planned second part, with its own design.

Method, details and reproduction

What was seen before registration, and the deviations

The design discloses it (§0). The author had seen both films several times and stated her expectations before any data; she had not counted or coded their jokes, and no subtitle file had been supplied when the design was tagged. The drafting assistant and the coding model are AI models that have very likely seen quotes from and commentary on both films; every codebook example was written for the design and none paraphrases a scene. The deviations, each dated in the change log: temperature 0 could not be sent to the model and was replaced by explicit settings, then by the command-line client's defaults; the blind sample was cut to {{coded}} coded cues; the runs went through the Claude Code client, not the API; and after run 1 stopped at the account's usage limit, calls that got no answer were repeated, while failures in the model's reply kept their single retry. No code, count or estimate had been looked at when any of these was decided, and the gate, the codebook and the readings were not changed.

Reproduction

The stages, from hashing the files to the analysis, are in tools/pitch/README.md; they need the subtitle files, which are not distributed, and the model runs cannot be repeated exactly.
