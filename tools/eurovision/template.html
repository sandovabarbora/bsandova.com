Eurovision, not Brussels? Diaspora, juries and the EU in Eurovision votes

A pre-specified analysis of how Europe votes for its songs. Where a performer's diaspora is large, the public's points relative to the jury's are about {{R}} times what they are where there is none. Neighbours and a shared language show no clear difference between public and jury. Joining the European Union earns a country no extra points; the registered estimate is a small fall, and inconclusive.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Since 2016 every country gives Eurovision points twice in the final (and, until 2022, in the semi-finals): once from five professional jurors and once from its public, for the same songs on the same night.
  • Where many people from the performing country live, the public favours that song over the jury about twice as much as where none do. Where almost nobody does, the public gives less than the jury.
  • Neighbours and countries sharing a language favour each other too, and we found no clear difference between how the public and the jury do so, though that check is imprecise. Of the things tested, diaspora is the one the public clearly brings and the jury does not.
  • Two countries both being in the EU does not make them give each other more points. The registered estimate is a small fall, inconclusive under the registered rule, and most of it comes from the 2004 enlargement.
  • Based on a pre-specified analysis of 9 contests (2016–2025) and {{n_voters}} countries, comparing each country's jury and public on the same song. It is an association: migrant numbers may partly stand for how familiar the wider public is with a country, not only for migrants voting.

Every May, someone explains that Eurovision is political: the Nordics vote for each other, the Balkans vote for each other, Greece and Cyprus exchange twelves, and the song barely matters. Economists have checked. Ginsburgh and Noury found that most of the apparent bloc voting disappears once you account for language and culture: countries that share them also share taste1. Spierdijk and Vellekoop found that the bias in the votes follows geography and culture, and in part migration2. What those studies could not do well is separate two things that move together: cultural closeness, which makes people like the same songs, and diaspora, which gives a country a crowd of voters from somewhere else.

Since 2016, the contest has made that separation possible by accident. Each country now gives points twice: once from a jury of five music professionals and once from a public televote, for the same songs, performed the same night. A shared taste should show up in both. A diaspora, people who moved from one country to another and still feel for their old one, can only show up in the public vote: Poles in Ireland can call; the Irish jury has no reason to. So the difference between a country's televote and its jury, for the same song, measures what a public brings that a panel does not. This study measures how much of it follows diaspora.

A second question came out of a joke: is Eurovision a stronger European project than the European Union? That cannot be measured. A narrower version can: when two countries are both in the EU, do they give each other more points than before? The study asks that of fifty years of votes.

Data

The votes come from three sources. Janne Spijkervet's dataset has every country-to-country vote from 1957 to 2023, with jury and televote points separated since 20163. The Mirovision data by Burgoyne, Spijkervet and Baker cover 2016 to 2022 and include each juror's ranking4; they agree with Spijkervet's points on every pair they share. The 2024 and 2025 contests were read from the detailed voting tables of their Wikipedia articles at fixed revisions5; the same parser, run on 2023, reproduces Spijkervet's 740 non-zero 2023 votes exactly. The "rest of the world" online vote, which belongs to no country, is left out.

Diaspora is measured from the United Nations' international migrant stock6: the number of people born in the performing country who live in the voting country, per 1 000 of its inhabitants (population from the World Bank7), for the nearest of 2015, 2020 and 2024. The UN does not list every origin for every destination: for some countries, such as the United Kingdom, small origins are pooled into "other". Pairs without a figure are dropped, as the design said, and the result is reported again with those pairs counted as zero. Ukrainian refugees come from the UNHCR8, and shared borders and official languages from the CEPII distance database9.

The raw numbers already point one way (fig. 1). Where the performer's diaspora is tiny, under 0.01 per 1 000, the public gives it fewer points than the jury: {{bin_lo_tele}} against {{bin_lo_jury}} on average, a difference of {{bin_lo_gap2}}. As the diaspora grows, the gap closes and turns. Where it is over five per 1 000, the public gives {{bin_hi_tele}} points and the jury {{bin_hi_jury}}, a difference of {{bin_hi_gap2}}. The jury's average rises a little with diaspora, from {{bin_lo_jury}} to {{bin_hi_jury}} points; the public's rises {{tele_mult}}-fold.

The pairs with the largest gaps read like a map of European migration since 2004. {{top1}}. Poland, Lithuania, Romania, Albania and Serbia collect public points in the countries where their workers moved: Ireland, the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, Switzerland, Slovenia. At the other end, juries sometimes reward what the public ignores: from {{neg1}}, the televote gave {{neg1_tele}} points on average and the jury {{neg1_jury}}.

How this was done

The design, the hypotheses and the rule for reading each result were committed at f304d8c on 3 October 2026, before this analysis was run; the author had worked with these data since April 2025. The power was simulated from the contest's structure alone. The registration is self-timestamped: commits are recorded by the author's machine and pushed to a public repository, so they show the order of steps, not an independent seal. This study needed more changes after registration than the earlier ones; each is dated and listed at the end, and none was made after its result was seen.

The model for diaspora compares the two audiences of the same voting country for the same song. Each row is a voting country, a performer, a contest round and an audience, with the points given (zero for most). A Poisson regression, estimated by pseudo-maximum likelihood, which handles counts with many zeros10, includes three sets of fixed effects: separate baselines, so that only differences within each one count. One for every voting country, round and audience absorbs how generous each audience is. One for every performer, round and audience absorbs how much each audience liked each song. One for every pair of countries in every round absorbs everything about the pair in that contest that both audiences share: neighbours, language, history, a song sung in the voter's language. What remains is how the televote's points to a performer differ from the jury's, and the estimate is how that difference grows with the diaspora. Standard errors are clustered by pair of countries, which allows the votes within a pair to be correlated.

The rule was set in advance. Let the ratio be the televote's points relative to the jury's for a pair at the 90th percentile of diaspora, {{per90}} per 1 000, against the same ratio for a pair with none: a ratio of two ratios, saying how much more the public favours a performer over the jury where its diaspora is large. The hypothesis is "supported" if the ratio's 95 % confidence interval (the range the true value plausibly lies in) lies wholly above 1, "not supported" if the ratio's interval lies within 0.90 to 1.10, and "inconclusive" otherwise.

Where diaspora is large

At {{per90}} migrants per 1 000 inhabitants, the public's points to a performer, relative to the jury's, are {{R}} times what they are for a performer with no diaspora in the voting country (95 % CI {{R_lo}} to {{R_hi}}). The interval lies far above 1, so the hypothesis is {{label}}. The estimate rests on {{n_cells}} rows, one per audience, from the {{n_pr}} pair-rounds in which either audience gave a point. It is an association: the migrant count may also stand for how familiar the wider public is with the performing country, through media, travel or minorities not born there.

It holds however the data are cut (fig. 2). Without 2022, the year almost every public voted for Ukraine, the ratio is {{r_no22}}. In finals only it is {{r_fin}}, in semi-finals only {{r_sf}}. Compared with each of the five jurors separately rather than with the jury's combined points (2016–2022, the years with juror rankings, converted to points), it is {{r_jur}}. Counting the pairs the UN does not list as zero migrants, it is {{r_zero}}.

The most telling check, not itself a registered hypothesis, adds shared borders and shared official languages, each allowed to matter differently for the public and the jury. The diaspora ratio stays at {{r_cl}}. Neighbours' public votes are {{ct}} times their juries' (95 % CI {{ct_lo}} to {{ct_hi}}), and a shared language's {{lt}} ({{lt_lo}} to {{lt_hi}}): no clear difference, though both intervals allow differences of up to about 20 %. Neighbours and countries that share a language do favour each other, which earlier studies found1, and which the pair-level effects here absorb, but we find no sign that the public favours them more than the jury does. That fits cultural taste, which reaches professionals as well as viewers. Pairs missing from the CEPII file, among them Montenegro's, were counted as neither neighbours nor sharing a language, rather than left out as the design note said. Diaspora is different. It shows up only in the public vote.

Seen from two countries (fig. 3), the pattern is concrete. Czechia's public gave more than its jury above all to {{cz_top}} (average points, televote minus jury): countries with communities in Czechia, by far the largest of them Ukrainian. Germany's public did so for {{de_top}}. Where a diaspora is small, the public often gives less than the jury, which is the other half of the same fact: the jury spreads its points by craft, while the public concentrates them on the songs it has a reason to call for.

Ukraine

The registered second test asked whether, from 2022, countries hosting more Ukrainian refugees per inhabitant gave Ukraine more public points relative to their juries than before. The estimate is {{h2}} televote-minus-jury points to Ukraine for each unit of log(1 + Ukrainian refugees per 1 000 inhabitants at the end of 2022) (95 % CI {{h2_lo}} to {{h2_hi}}), across {{h2_voters}} voting countries. The registered rule needed the interval to lie within ±0.5 points to call it a null, so it is {{h2_label}}. One possible reason, not tested here: in 2022 most publics in Europe gave Ukraine their top points, leaving little room for differences between countries. With only {{h2_voters}} voting countries, the interval is also simply wide.

Does the EU earn points?

The third test follows pairs of countries through fifty years of contests and asks what happens to the share of points they give each other once both are in the European Union12. Countries joined at different times, in 1981, 1986, 1995, 2004, 2007 and 2013, so pairs that became joint members can be compared with pairs that were not yet, or never, both members. The estimator is Gardner's two-stage difference in differences13, which compares the change in joining pairs with the change in pairs not joining at the time and is built for countries joining at different times, as implemented in pyfixest14. The outcome is the share of a voter's points that went to the other country, so the change in 2016 from one set of points to two does not matter. {{h3_pairs}} directed pairs (voter to performer) became joint members with at least three contests observed before; {{h3_dropped}} had too few and were dropped.

In the first six contests after both were in the EU, their mutual share of points changed by {{h3_pp}} percentage points (95 % CI {{h3_lo}} to {{h3_hi}}), against an average of {{h3_base}} % before: about {{h3_pct}} %. The registered rule expected a rise or nothing, so a fall is labelled {{h3_label}}. It should not be read as "the EU makes Europeans like each other less". The contests before accession are not flat (fig. 4, an event study, which estimates each contest year separately against the last contest before joining): two contests before, pairs gave each other {{ev_m2}} percentage points more than expected (95 % CI {{ev_m2_lo}} to {{ev_m2_hi}}), and five before, {{ev_m5}} ({{ev_m5_lo}} to {{ev_m5_hi}}). Both are about as large as the fall itself, so the comparison pairs do not track joining pairs well. Much of the fall, about {{h3_no04_share}} %, goes when the 2004 cohort, the countries that joined that year, is left out; that enlargement coincided with the introduction of semi-finals and with the arrival of new countries from the east that never joined the EU. Without it, the estimate is {{h3_no04}} percentage points ({{h3_no04_lo}} to {{h3_no04_hi}}): no longer clearly negative, but still inconclusive under the registered rule, whose margin is ±{{h3_margin}} points. That check and the one for finals only are registered checks, missing from the first code and added after the first estimates were seen; they use a stricter filter for usable rows than the main estimate. In finals only it is {{h3_fin}}.

Brexit was the reverse case, and it is exploratory: the United Kingdom's mutual share with EU countries, 2021–2025 against 2016–2019, relative to its pairs outside the EU, changed by {{bx}} percentage points ({{bx_lo}} to {{bx_hi}}). The interval is wide: this comparison cannot tell whether leaving cost or gained anything.

What this means

Measured: of what we tested, the clearest thing a public adds to a jury's verdict follows its migrants. Where a performer's diaspora is large, the televote favours it over the jury about twice as much as where there is none; where the diaspora is tiny, the televote gives less than the jury. Neighbours and shared languages matter too, and we found no clear sign that they move viewers more than professionals, which fits taste rather than loyalty. Two countries both being in the European Union did not raise what they give each other; the registered estimate is a change of about {{h3_pct}} %, concentrated in the 2004 enlargement, and the study cannot tell whether that fall is real or a by-product of what else changed in 2004.

So is Eurovision a stronger European project than the EU? On this narrow measure, the answer is that they work on different things. The EU did not make anyone vote for a fellow member. What the votes do show is that migration, including the migration EU enlargement made easier, from Poland, Lithuania and Romania to Ireland, Britain and Italy, shows up on the scoreboard every May. This study does not separate EU free movement from other migration: some of the largest gaps, such as Latvia to Russia or Switzerland to Serbia, follow migration from outside the EU.

What this does not show

Points are not friendship. They are a public's and a jury's taste for one song on one night, and a country that sends a weak song loses points from friends too. The diaspora estimate cannot say whether migrants themselves cast the extra votes, or why they would: identity, organisation, or simply that a community that cares calls more than once. It uses migrants by birthplace, so second generations and refugees not yet counted as migrants are missing. A third of the rows have no UN figure; counting them as zero gives the same answer, but the true figures could differ. The EU test bundles accession with everything that came with it, and the years before accession are not flat, so it can rule out a large gain from membership but not every small one. And all of this is televotes and juries from 2016 on: the decades before, when each country published a single set of points, are seen only in the EU test.

Changes after registration

All are dated in the design file. Before the data step: the Mirovision files end in 2022, so split votes for 2016–2023 come from Spijkervet, with Mirovision as a check, and the juror check covers 2016–2022. During the data step: the Wikipedia parser's header detection was made robust; country names in one source were mapped to codes; the rest-of-the-world vote was dropped from the EU test; pairs without a UN figure were dropped as registered, and a check counting them as zero was added. Before any estimate was seen: the registered pair fixed effects did not converge, so pair × contest effects, which contain them, were used. After the first estimates: a software problem in the two-stage estimator was worked around by dropping rows it cannot use, and the registered EU checks and the Brexit comparison, missing from the first code, were added, the EU checks with a stricter filter for usable rows than the main estimate; the check by audience for the EU test cannot be run, because no country joined between 2016 and 2025.

Every number on this page is filled in from assets/eurovision/results.json and the published tables by a script. From the repository root:

  1. tools/eurovision/collect.py: every source and its hash.
  2. prepare.py: the three tables and the validation.
  3. estimate.py: the registered estimates and checks.
  4. article.py: the figures, the table and this page.
