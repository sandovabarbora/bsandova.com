Where is delay born on Prague's trams? Segment by segment, 2025

A pre-specified analysis of every stop-to-stop segment of Prague's tram network finds that a tenth of the segments make half of all delay and that they are the same ones week after week; many of them are the segments leaving the large stops.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Every time a tram goes from one stop to the next, its delay changes. Over {{passes_m}} million such passes in 2025, the {{top_k}} worst of Prague's {{segments}} tram segments made {{S}} % of all the delay ({{S_lo}} to {{S_hi}} %), just over the half set in advance, so the concentration is {{S_label}}, by a narrow margin.
  • They are the same segments every week: ranked separately in odd and even weeks, the order agrees almost perfectly ({{rho}}). Delay is made in fixed places, not at random.
  • The largest are {{p1}}, {{p2}} and {{p3}}. At the large stops the pattern is regular: leaving each of them costs time, and at the busiest, Anděl, Hlavní nádraží and Václavské náměstí, trams make time up on the way in. Slack in the timetable before the stop and time spent boarding at it both fit; this pattern was described after the results, not registered.

A tram that reaches the end of its line five minutes late did not lose those five minutes in one place. It lost a few seconds here, made some up there, and the sum is what the passengers at the far end see. The author's thesis showed that this delay persists: once a tram is late, it tends to stay late for the rest of the hour1. This part asks the question before that one. Along the way, where does the delay appear?

Every pass of a tram from one stop to the next changes its delay by some number of seconds, positive if it falls further behind, negative if it catches up. Add those changes up for every pass over the season and each of Prague's tram segments has a total: the delay the network took on there, or made up.

The question

Two questions were written down before any segment's delay was read. First, is the delay concentrated: do the tenth of segments with the largest totals make more than half of all the delay made anywhere? Segments where trams catch up are left out of that sum, so the share is of the delay made, not of the net. Second, are those places stable: if the segments are ranked by the delay per pass separately in odd and in even weeks, is the rank correlation at least 0.70? Both are read from a bootstrap that resamples whole days, which also redraws which segments count as the top tenth. A question is "supported" if its 95 % interval lies wholly above its line, "not supported" if wholly below, and "inconclusive" otherwise.

A segment here is one direction between two named stops, so the trip from Anděl to Arbesovo náměstí and the one back are two segments. The delay change on a pass is the delay at the next stop minus the delay at this stop, so it includes the time the tram stands at this stop as well as the run to the next.

Data

The passes are those of the author's thesis: Prague Integrated Transport's stop-time history from Golemio2, 15 March to 8 September 20251, with the screening of the earlier studies for planned closures and feed gaps, which removes {{cl_lo}}–{{cl_hi}} % of tram passes a month. Passes whose delay changed by more than ten minutes on one segment are left out as diversions or faults, as are {{rare}} segments with fewer than 1 000 passes ({{rare_p}} passes in all), which are one-off routings. That leaves {{passes_m}} million tram passes over {{segments}} segments on {{dates}} days.

One thing in the data was not as its name said. The thesis's column for the delay gained on a segment turned out, on a sample of {{sampled}} passes, to be the running time against the timetable, which leaves out the time at the stop; it differed from the change in delay in {{def_off}} % of them. The design had a rule for this, fixed in advance: the change in delay is recomputed as the delay at the next stop minus the delay at this one. Running time alone is reported as a check.

A tenth of the network makes half of the delay

Over the season, Prague's trams made {{made_h}} hours of delay on {{producers}} segments and made up {{made_up_h}} hours on {{recoverers}} others. The {{top_k}} segments with the largest totals made {{S}} % of the delay made (95 % CI {{S_lo}} to {{S_hi}} %). The whole interval lies above one half, so the concentration is {{S_label}}, but only just: the line of one half was a round number fixed in advance, and {{S}} % clears it by less than a percentage point. A tenth of the network makes half of the delay, not most of it. The largest single producer is {{p1}}, with {{p1_h}} hours over the season, {{p1_s}} seconds on an average pass, followed by {{p2}} ({{p2_h}} h), {{p3}} ({{p3_h}} h) and {{p4}} ({{p4_h}} h) (figs. 1 and 2).

Part of any concentration is traffic: a busy segment carries more trams and so more of the total. A comparison computed after the results, and not registered, shows how much. The tenth of segments with the most passes carry {{vol}} % of all tram passes, so if every segment added the same delay per pass, the top tenth would make about {{vol}} % of the delay. They make {{S}} %. The largest producers are not simply the busiest segments either: they carry {{byd}} % of the passes, and only {{ovl}} % of them are also among the busiest tenth.

The same places every week

Ranked by the delay per pass in odd weeks and again in even weeks, the segments come out in nearly the same order: the rank correlation is {{rho}}, and its 95 % interval, {{rho_lo}} to {{rho_hi}}, lies above 0.70, so stability is {{rho_label}}. That threshold was easy to pass. With tens of thousands of passes on each segment, a segment's mean barely moves from week to week, so a near-perfect rank correlation is close to guaranteed by construction. What it shows is that the pattern is structural, set by the timetable, the fixed stops and the junctions, not that the hotspots are real in some stronger sense. The point estimate sits just above its own bootstrap interval; resampling whole days draws some days twice and spreads them unevenly between odd and even weeks, which pulls the correlation down a little, and the label does not depend on it. Weekday peaks and weekday middays share {{jac}} of their worst tenth (Jaccard index), so the rush hour makes delay in largely the same places as midday, only more of it. Where delay appears on Prague's trams is a fixed feature of the network and its timetable, not the accident of a given day.

In on time, out late

Look at where the largest producers are, and a pattern appears that the design did not ask about, and that is reported here as a description. At each of six large stops, trams lose time on the segments leaving, and at the busiest of them they make it up on the segments arriving; at Kobylisy and Karlovo náměstí they arrive about even. On an average pass, weighted over the segments, a tram's delay changes by {{and_in}} seconds arriving at Anděl and by {{and_out}} leaving it, and by {{hn_in}} and {{hn_out}} at Hlavní nádraží. On Václavské náměstí the segment from Vodičkova changes it by {{vod}} seconds and the next one, on to Jindřišská, by {{vac}} (fig. 3).

Three explanations fit, and the data cannot fully separate them. The first is the timetable. Schedules give the run into a large stop some slack, so a late tram can arrive closer to time, and the stop itself a dwell that the crowd at Anděl or on Václavské náměstí does not keep to. The second is how the delay is measured: because the delay on a segment includes the time spent at its first stop, a tram that stands long at Anděl or on Václavské náměstí while people board shows it on the segment leaving them, so the busiest stops carry their boarding time into the next segment. The third is traffic, which the comparison above shows explains only part of the concentration. Running time alone, without the stop, tells the same story from the other side: of the {{run_seg}} segments with a running time, trams run faster than the timetable on {{run_neg}} %, and only {{run_pos}} lose time while moving, so on running time the top tenth make practically all of the delay made ({{run}} %). How timetables spread such slack, and how late trams pass delay on, is an old question for railways45 and buses6; the dwell at busy stops is its usual source on the street7.

Checks

The share made by the top segments holds across the checks registered in advance (fig. 4). It is {{t5}} % for the top 5 % of segments and {{t20}} % for the top 20 %. Ranked by delay per pass instead of by total, the top tenth make {{pp}} %, because the busiest segments count for more in a total. Leaving out the first and last segment of each trip gives {{noterm}} %, and keeping only passes within five minutes gives {{g300}} %. Month by month the share runs from {{m_lo}} to {{m_hi}} %.

City buses, analysed alongside with their own labels, show the same: on routes 100 to 299, the top tenth of {{bseg}} segments make {{bS}} % of their delay ({{bS_lo}} to {{bS_hi}} %, {{bS_label}}), and the ranking is as stable as for trams ({{brho}}, {{brho_label}}). Their peaks and middays share less of their worst tenth ({{bjac}}), as buses meet the rush hour on the roads.

What this means

Prague's tram delay is made in fixed places, a tenth of the network makes half of it, and the same places stand out every week. The places are mostly the large stops and the segments leaving them. That points less to the street than to the timetable: the time it allows at the busiest stops and the slack it puts before them. A city that wanted fewer late trams would look first at how long trams stand at Anděl, Václavské náměstí and Hlavní nádraží, and at whether the timetable gives them that time.

What this does not show

A segment where delay appears is not always where it is caused: a tram held at a junction or a stop shows the delay on the segment where it is recorded, and the timetable decides where a late tram can catch up. The pattern at the large stops is a description found after the registered questions were answered, not a test. The delay is per vehicle, not per passenger, so a minute lost on a full tram counts the same as on an empty one. The season is one spring and summer; winter, the metro and other years are not covered. The map places {{drawn}} of {{segments}} segments, those whose stops could be found in the timetable data.
