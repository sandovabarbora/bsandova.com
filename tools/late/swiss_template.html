Is Prague's delay map a law of trams? Zurich, Basel and Bern

In Prague, a tenth of the tram segments make half of the delay. Basel and Bern look much the same; Zurich does not. Measured against what chance alone would give, all three Swiss networks concentrate their delay less than Prague.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Part 1 found that on Prague's trams, the tenth of segments (stretches from one stop to the next) that make the most delay make {{pr_s}} % of it. This part asks the same of Zurich, Basel and Bern over the same 2025 season, with the same code.

Part 1 of this series measured where Prague's trams gain delay1. Over the 2025 season, the 62 of 618 segments with the largest totals made {{pr_s}} % of all the delay gained, and they were the same segments week after week. Is that a property of Prague, with its old street network and trams sharing junctions with cars, or of tram networks in general? This part runs the same measure, with the same code and over the same 178 days, on the three Swiss tram networks that publish every stop time: Zurich, Basel and Bern. Geneva publishes its times too, but labels none of them as measured, so by the registered rule it enters only a check.

Data

Switzerland publishes the actual times of every public-transport vehicle at every stop, one file per day for the whole country2. From 15 March to 8 September 2025, the trams of Zurich's VBZ, of Basel's BVB and BLT (one track network, pooled) and of Bern's Bernmobil were kept. A pass is a tram's run from one stop to the next; its gain is the delay at the next stop minus the delay at the first, as in Prague. A pass is kept only if the times at both stops are marked as measured ({{zurich_kept_real}} % of Zurich's passes are on segments that qualify, with similar shares elsewhere), its gain is within ten minutes, and its segment has at least 1 000 kept passes. That leaves {{zurich_segs}} segments and {{zurich_passes}} million passes in Zurich, {{basel_segs}} and {{basel_passes}} million in Basel, and {{bern_segs}} and {{bern_passes}} million in Bern.

Both Prague's and Switzerland's timetables are to the minute, so the delay is measured against the same kind of schedule in both. The Swiss files give no order of stops within a trip; stops are ordered by their scheduled times, and {{ties}} stops scheduled in the same minute as the one before were dropped rather than ordered by their actual times.

How this was done

For each network, S is the share of all delay gained that the tenth of segments with the largest totals make, with a 95 % interval from resampling whole days. Its difference from Prague, D, is labelled by a rule fixed in advance: like Prague if its 90 % interval lies within ±5 points, unlike Prague if its 95 % interval lies wholly outside them. Five points is the smallest difference that would change part 1's sentence, "about half of the delay from a tenth of the segments".

A tenth of a small network is a handful of segments, and a handful of segments can make a large share of the delay by luck alone. The chance level S₀ is the share the top tenth would make if delay were not tied to place: the segment labels are shuffled among the passes of each day and hour, 199 times, and S recomputed. The excess over chance, S − S₀, is compared between cities in the same way.

What this means

Measured: on the same season and by the same code, Basel's and Bern's trams make about as large a share of their delay on a tenth of their segments as Prague's, and Zurich's a clearly smaller one. Against what chance alone would give a network of their size, all three concentrate less than Prague. In all four cities the places that make the delay are the same week after week.

The author expected the Swiss networks to be less concentrated than Prague, because they have more reserved track and priority at junctions. Zurich fits that on the registered share, and all three fit it on the excess over chance, which was registered alongside; Basel and Bern do not fit it on the raw share. Track, junctions, stop spacing and timetables were not measured, so these data cannot say which, if any, explains the difference.

What this does not show

Why the cities differ: network shape, stop spacing, street running against reserved track, signal priority, timetable padding and traffic all differ, and with four networks none can be separated from the others. As in part 1, where delay is recorded is not always where it is caused. Other cities, winter and other years are not covered; Stockholm, Helsinki, the Netherlands and Vienna publish no archive of actual times for the season. Seconds per tram, not per passenger.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from results.json and the screening file by a script. From the repository root:

  1. tools/late/swiss_fetch.py: the monthly archives, tram rows only.
  2. swiss_screen.py: the screening and the passes.
  3. swiss_estimate.py: the registered estimates and checks, with tests in test_swiss.py; it stops unless it reproduces part 1's published result.
  4. swiss_article.py: the figures, the maps and this page.

The design is docs/research/swiss-trams-design.md.
