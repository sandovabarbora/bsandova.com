Do Swiss trams pile up delay like Prague's?

Basel and Bern pile their delay onto a few stretches much as Prague does and Zurich does not, so the pattern holds roughly, not as a law of trams.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Part 1 found that on Prague's trams, the tenth of segments (stretches from one stop to the next) that make the most delay make {{pr_s}} % of it. This part asks the same of Zurich, Basel and Bern over the same 2025 season, with the same code.
  • Basel ({{basel_s}} %) and Bern ({{bern_s}} %) are within five points of Prague, the margin set in advance (within, not equal); Zurich ({{zurich_s}} %) is clearly lower. In every city the worst segments are the same from week to week.
  • Part of any concentration is chance; the share chance alone would give is higher in Basel and Bern than in Prague. Above it, all three Swiss networks concentrate less than Prague ({{zurich_de}}, {{basel_de}} and {{bern_de}} points). But ranked by delay per tram instead of in total, all three concentrate more than Prague: the result depends on the measure.
  • The author expected the Swiss networks to be less concentrated, because of reserved track and priority at junctions. Zurich fits that on the registered share; Basel and Bern do not. Why the cities differ is not measured.

Part 1 of this series measured where Prague's trams gain delay1. Over the 2025 season, the 62 of 618 segments with the largest totals made {{pr_s}} % of all the delay gained, and they were the same segments week after week. This part asks whether that belongs to Prague, with its old street network and trams sharing junctions with cars, or to tram networks in general. It runs the same estimator, with the same code for the estimates, over the same season (178 days in Switzerland, 170 in Prague after part 1's screening) on the three Swiss tram networks that publish every stop time, in Zurich, Basel and Bern. Geneva publishes its times too, but marks none of them as measured, so under the registered rule it enters only a check.

Data

Switzerland publishes the actual times of every public-transport vehicle at every stop, one file per day for the whole country2. From 15 March to 8 September 2025, the trams of Zurich's VBZ, Basel's BVB and BLT (one track network, pooled) and Bern's Bernmobil were kept. A pass is one tram's run from a stop to the next. Its gain is the delay at the next stop minus the delay at the first, as in Prague, so it includes the time standing at the first stop. A pass is kept only if the times at both stops are marked as measured, its gain is within ten minutes and its segment has at least 1 000 kept passes. That keeps {{zurich_kept_formed}} % of the passes formed in Zurich, {{basel_kept_formed}} % in Basel and {{bern_kept_formed}} % in Bern. The archive version used here has no stops outside Switzerland, so the ends of Basel's lines into France and Germany fall outside the network. That leaves {{zurich_segs}} segments and {{zurich_passes}} million passes in Zurich, {{basel_segs}} and {{basel_passes}} million in Basel, and {{bern_segs}} and {{bern_passes}} million in Bern.

Prague and Switzerland both publish timetables to the minute, so in both the delay is measured against the same kind of schedule. The Swiss files do not give the order of stops within a trip, so stops are ordered by their scheduled times. A stop scheduled in the same minute as the one before was dropped rather than ordered by its actual time, which removed {{ties}} stops (in all four networks). Otherwise screening followed the Swiss data, not part 1's Prague-specific rules, so the two sides differ in more than the estimator.

How this was done

For each network, S is the share of all delay gained that the tenth of segments with the largest totals make, with a 95 % interval from resampling whole days. Its difference from Prague, D, is labelled by a rule fixed in advance: like Prague if its 90 % interval lies within ±5 points, unlike Prague if its 95 % interval lies wholly outside them. Five points is the smallest difference that would change part 1's sentence, "about half of the delay from a tenth of the segments". It is also about the spread of Prague's monthly shares, which was known when the margin was chosen. Before registration, one Swiss sample day was read for its counts, not its delays.

Some concentration arises even if delay has nothing to do with place, since segments carry different numbers of trams and gains have long tails. The chance level S₀ is the share the top tenth would make if delay were not tied to place. To get it, the segment labels are shuffled among the passes of each day and hour, 199 times, and S is recomputed. The excess over chance, S − S₀, is compared between cities in the same way, with S₀ held fixed, so its intervals leave out S₀'s own small uncertainty.

Basel and Bern like Prague, Zurich not

In Zurich, the top tenth of segments make {{zurich_s}} % of the delay (95 % CI {{zurich_lo}} to {{zurich_hi}} %), {{zurich_d}} points from Prague ({{zurich_d_lo}} to {{zurich_d_hi}}), labelled {{zurich_d_label}}. In Basel the share is {{basel_s}} % ({{basel_lo}} to {{basel_hi}} %), {{basel_d}} points from Prague, labelled {{basel_d_label}}. In Bern it is {{bern_s}} % ({{bern_lo}} to {{bern_hi}} %), {{bern_d}} points, labelled {{bern_d_label}}. Like Prague means within five points, and both Basel's and Bern's intervals exclude zero, with Bern lower by {{bern_d_abs}} points. By the rule part 1 used, half of the delay on a tenth of the segments is {{basel_label}} in Basel and {{zurich_label}} in Zurich and Bern, where the share falls below one half (fig. 1).

In every city the worst segments stay the same from week to week. The rank correlation of each segment's mean gain between odd and even weeks is {{zurich_rho}} in Zurich, {{basel_rho}} in Basel and {{bern_rho}} in Bern, as in Prague. With thousands of passes per segment that is close to guaranteed, so it shows the pattern belongs to places without showing why. Near one, the resampling intervals of this correlation sit slightly below the estimate, as in part 1.

Above chance, less than Prague

Chance alone would put {{pr_s0}} % of Prague's delay on its top tenth, {{zurich_s0}} % of Zurich's, {{basel_s0}} % of Basel's and {{bern_s0}} % of Bern's. Basel has nearly as many segments as Zurich, so network size alone does not account for it. The chance level also depends on how unevenly passes are spread over the segments and on the tails of the gains, and these were not separated here. Above that level, Prague makes {{pr_e}} points more, Zurich {{zurich_e}}, Basel {{basel_e}} and Bern {{bern_e}}. Compared with Prague, the excess is {{zurich_de}} points in Zurich, {{basel_de}} in Basel and {{bern_de}} in Bern, and by the same rule all three are unlike Prague, less concentrated. Basel is the closest call, with an interval ending at {{basel_de_hi}} points and a chance level that alone ranges from {{basel_s0_lo}} to {{basel_s0_hi}} %. Fig. 2 follows the whole curves. Over the largest third of segments, all three Swiss curves sit closer to their chance curves than Prague's does. Further down the order is no longer fixed. From 49 % of segments on, Zurich's gap is the larger, and in places so is Bern's.

Zurich is the least concentrated of the three Swiss networks in every row that measures concentration, while against Prague the picture is mixed. Ranked by delay per tram instead of in total, all three Swiss networks concentrate more than Prague ({{zurich_pp}}, {{basel_pp}} and {{bern_pp}} % against {{pr_pp}} %). By length and in the top 20 %, Basel and Bern reach or pass Prague. On these point estimates, Basel ranked per tram falls just below one half and Bern without first and last segments rises above it, so the "half on a tenth" reading is not robust for either city. The worst segments in peaks and in middays overlap far less in Switzerland than in Prague ({{pr_jac}}). The Gini here counts producing segments only, while part 1 reported one over all segments. In Basel, BVB's passes alone give {{basel_bvb}} %, and within the pooled network the top tenth make {{basel_bvb_p}} % of the delay of BVB's passes and {{basel_blt_p}} % of BLT's. Keeping times not marked as measured barely moves the shares, and Geneva, which enters only here, gives {{geneva_s}} % on {{geneva_segs}} segments.

What this means

As measured on the same season and by the same estimator, Basel's and Bern's trams make a share of their delay on a tenth of their segments within five points of Prague's, and Zurich's a clearly smaller one. Above what chance alone would give, all three concentrate less than Prague, and ranked by delay per tram all three concentrate more. In all four cities the places where delay builds up are the same week after week. Every network gathers its delay on a few places, but how far, and how that compares with Prague, depends on the measure.

The author expected the Swiss networks to be less concentrated than Prague, because they have more reserved track and priority at junctions. Zurich fits it on the registered share. Basel and Bern do not, with Bern lower than Prague by {{bern_d_abs}} points, within the margin. The excess over chance, registered alongside, fits it in all three, and delay per tram goes the other way. Track, junctions, stop spacing and timetables were not measured, so this study cannot tell which, if any, explains the difference.

What this does not show

This study does not show why the cities differ. Network shape, stop spacing, street running against reserved track, signal priority, timetable padding and traffic all vary between them, and with four networks none can be separated from the others. As in part 1, the place where delay is recorded is not always the place where it is caused. Other cities, winter and other years are not covered. Stockholm's archive needs a key and a reconstruction from snapshots, and Helsinki, the Netherlands and Vienna have no open archive of actual times for the season. Delay is in seconds per tram, not per passenger.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from results.json and the screening file by a script. From the repository root:

  1. tools/late/swiss_fetch.py: the monthly archives, tram rows only.
  2. swiss_screen.py: the screening and the passes.
  3. swiss_estimate.py: the registered estimates and checks, with tests in test_swiss.py; it stops unless it reproduces part 1's published result.
  4. swiss_article.py: the figures, the maps and this page.
  5. swiss_animation.py: the data of the animated comparison (fig. 2), drawn by assets/late-swiss/cities.js with d3; the chance curves are a description, not part of the registered analysis.

The design is docs/research/swiss-trams-design.md.
