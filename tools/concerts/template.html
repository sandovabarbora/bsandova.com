Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • In the week of his first show in a country, his share of its Spotify Top 200 rose by {{w0}} percentage points (band {{w0_lo}} to {{w0_hi}}), about {{w0_rel}} % of what it was before. From the week after, no week is clearly above zero.
  • The registered result, the average over five weeks, is +{{sum}} points (95 % CI {{sum_lo}} to {{sum_hi}}) and {{label}}: the study could not rule out an average effect of about ±{{mde}} % of his share.
  • It rests on comparing visited countries with ones not yet visited, assuming both would otherwise have moved alike. One of the eleven weeks before the shows (ten weeks out) breaks that pattern, and the weeks just before them drift slightly down.
  • The jump came mainly from later, smaller markets such as New Zealand, Australia and Belgium; the first and largest markets show none in the raw data, and without Australia the five-week average falls by about {{loo_min_drop}} %. Only the Top 200 is seen, and the study covers one tour.

For most artists, concerts now earn more than recordings, and the two are often treated as complements: a show sells the songs, and the songs sell the show. Connolly and Krueger traced how musicians' income moved towards live performance1, and Papies and van Heerde found, for German artists before streaming, that concerts and record sales moved together in ways that depended on the artist2. Streaming makes the question sharper, because listening is now counted every day in every country; such country-level streaming data have already been used to measure streaming's effect on record sales3, and they can follow a concert within days. A tour that reaches countries at different dates allows a comparison with the countries not yet visited, which stand in for what would have happened to the others without a show. It is not a random experiment, because the order of the tour was chosen. Recent methods for such staggered timing45 avoid the comparisons that make a simple regression misleading, but they still rest on that stand-in being fair. This study asks it of Harry Styles's first solo tour, which visited {{n_treated}} countries with a complete Spotify chart between September 2017 and June 2018: did his share of a country's chart rise after he played there, and for how long? It is related to Pop, measured, whose first part describes the same artist's charts and tours.

Data

Spotify publishes a daily Top 200 for each country, with every song's streams that day; a copy of every chart from 2017 to 2021 is on Kaggle6. From it the study takes the charts of countries from March 2017 to December 2018 and sums them by week. In each country and week, the outcome is Harry Styles's share of the streams in the Top 200: his songs' streams divided by all the chart's streams. The share removes the size of each market and the growth of Spotify over the period. {{n_songs}} of his songs appear in the window, almost all from his first album; the three most streamed are {{top3}}.

The tour article on Wikipedia lists every show7: {{n_shows}} dated shows, the first in San Francisco on 19 September 2017 and the last of a new country in Mexico in June 2018. A country is treated from the week of its first show. Of the {{n_countries}} countries with a chart in every week of the window, {{n_treated}} had a show and {{n_never}} did not, among them Czechia, Poland and Austria. {{n_dropped}} countries whose charts start or stop during the window are left out: {{dropped}}. Thailand had a show but no full chart, so it is among them.

The tour came in waves. It opened in North America in September and October 2017, crossed to western Europe in late October and November, went on to Singapore, Australia, New Zealand and Japan in November and December, returned to Europe in March and April 2018, playing again in several countries and for the first time in Switzerland, Belgium, Denmark, Norway, Spain and Ireland, went back to Australia and on to the Philippines, Hong Kong and Thailand in April and May, and to Latin America in May and June, before a last North American leg in June and July that added no new country. Only the first show in each country is used, and a country counts as visited from then on. Where a country was played again, the second visit came at least 18 weeks after the first, outside the twelve weeks after the first show that the estimates cover, so the estimate is the effect of a first visit. The {{n_treated}} visited countries therefore fall into {{cohorts}} groups by the week of their first show, most of them of one to three countries, spread over nine months. That spread is what the method needs: in almost every week of the tour some countries had been visited and others had not yet.

The raw numbers already tell most of the story, though they cannot be read as an effect. In the visited countries, his average share of the chart's streams fell from {{raw_m12}} % twelve weeks before the show to {{raw_m2}} % two weeks before, as his first album aged; it jumped to {{raw_0}} % in the show week, then fell again, to {{raw_p4}} % four weeks after and {{raw_p12}} % at twelve. The fall before the show would make any simple before-and-after comparison misleading, because the share was dropping everywhere, visited or not; that is why the comparison has to be with countries not yet visited, which were ageing out of his first album at the same time.

How this was done

The design, the outcomes and the rule for reading the result were committed before the chart data were downloaded; the code and the weekly panel were committed before the first estimate. The estimator is Callaway and Sant'Anna's for staggered adoption4. For each group of countries first visited in the same week, it compares their change in share with the change, over the same weeks, in countries not yet visited or never visited. These comparisons are averaged by weeks since the first show, from 12 weeks before to 12 after. Following the software's default, each estimate before the show is a one-week change, that week against the week before, while each estimate from the show week on compares that week with the week before the show. Inference uses a multiplier bootstrap clustered by country (999 draws), and the bands are uniform: they hold for all weeks at once, which is stricter than a separate interval for each week.

The registered summary is the average effect over weeks 0 to +4. It was labelled in advance: "supported" if its interval excludes zero and the weeks before the show show no trend, "not supported" if its interval includes zero and stays within ten percent of the share before the show, and "inconclusive" otherwise. The share before the show was {{base_pp}} % of a country's Top-200 streams, so ten percent of it is a small number, and the rule asks a lot of the data. The method rests on two assumptions. The first is parallel trends: without the show, a visited country's share would have moved like that of the countries not yet visited. The second is no anticipation: the share does not move in the weeks before a show just because a show is coming. Neither can be proven, but both leave traces in the weeks before the show, which is why those weeks are reported. A simpler regression with country and week effects would mix up the comparisons, using countries already visited as controls for those visited later; when effects change over time, as a spike that fades does, that can bias the estimate and even flip its sign8. The estimator used here avoids those comparisons. The weeks before the show test whether visited and unvisited countries were already drifting apart; a placebo moves every first show 26 weeks earlier; and the summary is re-estimated leaving out each visited country in turn.

What a show did

In the week of the first show, Harry Styles's share of the country's Top-200 streams rose by {{w0}} percentage points (95 % uniform band {{w0_lo}} to {{w0_hi}}), about {{w0_rel}} % of his share before the show. The band excludes zero even though it covers all 25 weeks at once. In the week after, the estimate is {{w1}} points ({{w1_lo}} to {{w1_hi}}), still positive but no longer clearly above zero; it is {{w2}} points in the second week and {{w3}} in the third. From the fifth week to the twelfth the estimates average {{late}} points against the week before the show, and none of their bands excludes zero, so the data cannot say whether the share went back exactly to where it was. The change from the second week before the show to the week before is {{wm1}} points: there is no sign that the anticipation alone moved the chart.

The registered summary over weeks 0 to +4 is {{sum}} points (95 % CI {{sum_lo}} to {{sum_hi}}), about {{sum_rel}} % of the share before the show. Its interval includes zero and is too wide to rule out an effect of ten percent, so by the rule set in advance it is {{label}}. The five-week window was chosen before the data were seen, on the expectation that an effect would last a few weeks. Only the show week is clearly above zero, and averaging it with four weeks that are not diluted it. Both the single week and the registered summary are reported here; the headline does not swap one for the other.

In streams, the effect is modest. The median visited country's Top 200 held {{w0_total}} million streams in the show week, so {{w0}} percentage points is roughly {{extra}} extra streams of his songs in such a country that week. This is a back-of-the-envelope figure, the share effect times the median chart, and it counts only streams of songs that were in the chart. The estimate is an average over countries, each counting the same, not over streams, and the largest raw jumps were in small markets (see below), so it would be wrong to multiply it by the charts of all {{n_treated}} countries; no total is given. Where the tour article gives Boxscore figures7, a single-night first show in a country sold between {{hall_min}} tickets (Sydney's Enmore Theatre) and {{hall_max}} (Dublin's 3Arena), and the 2017 first shows were mostly in theatres. The extra streams in a week are therefore about {{per_head_lo}} to {{per_head_hi}} per person in the hall, or a little from many others; the data cannot say which.

The same pattern holds for the number of his songs in the chart. In the show week the chart held {{y2_w0}} more of his songs on an average day (band {{y2_w0_lo}} to {{y2_w0_hi}}), against {{y2_base}} before the show, so some of his songs entered or re-entered the chart; over five weeks this outcome too is {{y2_label}}. Restricted to his three biggest songs, an outcome registered as exploratory, the summary is {{y3_sum}} points and {{y3_label}}, nearly the same as the main estimate. That says little, because these three songs already made up {{y3_pre_frac}} % of his chart share before the show: the outcome is almost the main one under another name, and the data cannot tell whether the show also helped his other songs.

Where the jump was largest

The following is descriptive and was not registered: for each visited country, the change in his raw share from the four weeks before the show to the show week, without any comparison group. The largest jumps, in percentage points, were in {{jump_top}}: all visited after the opening legs in North America and western Europe. In {{jump_top_held}} his album was still in the chart before the show; in {{jump_top_back}} he had left the chart, and the show week brought him back into it. In {{jump_neg}} the raw share fell in the show week; these were among the first countries visited, in the autumn of 2017, when his album was leaving the charts quickly from a high level, and the fall before the show simply went on. In {{n_zero}} countries, {{jump_zero}}, he was not in the Top 200 in those weeks at all, so any effect was below what the chart shows. These raw changes are not effects: the estimate in fig. 2 compares each country with others at the same time, which is what turns a falling line into a measured rise.

Checks

Comparing with countries never visited only, rather than also with those visited later, gives a summary of {{never}} points ({{never_lo}} to {{never_hi}}), close to the main estimate. Leaving out each visited country in turn keeps the summary positive, between {{loo_min}} (without {{loo_min_c}}) and {{loo_max}} points (without {{loo_max_c}}); without the United States it is {{loo_us}}, without the United Kingdom {{loo_uk}}. Without {{loo_min_c}} it falls by about {{loo_min_drop}} %, so one country carries a good part of it, and a summary whose interval already includes zero keeping its sign is weak evidence. Leaving countries out was registered for the five-week summary only and was not run for the show-week estimate. The placebo, which pretends every show happened 26 weeks earlier, gives {{placebo}} points ({{placebo_lo}} to {{placebo_hi}}). Its interval includes zero, but it is too imprecise to rule out a false effect larger than the one reported, so it does not test the design; moving early shows back also pushes them out of the window.

Of the eleven weeks from −12 to −2, {{n_bad_pre}} has a band that excludes zero: {{bad_pre}} ({{bad_pre_band}} points). Because the bands are uniform, even one week outside them is more than chance alone would usually produce; the registered rule counts it, and it is one reason the label is not "supported". Read as one-week changes, the six weeks from −7 to −2 are all negative, from {{pre_down_min}} to {{pre_down_max}} points, and add up to {{pre_down}}, after weeks −12 to −8 that add up to {{pre_up}}. No one of the six excludes zero, but together they suggest a mild decline in the visited countries, relative to the others, in the weeks before their shows, about as large as the show-week effect. The slightly negative estimates from the fifth week on may continue that decline rather than show a fall below where the share would have been. Re-estimating against a single base week, which would show this directly, was not registered and was not run.

How large an effect could the design have caught? The interval of the five-week summary is about ±{{mde}} % of his share before the show. An average effect over five weeks smaller than that could not be told apart from none, which is why the rule's ten-percent threshold for "not supported" was out of reach. A single strong week clears that bar easily; a lasting effect of a few percent would not have. With {{n_treated}} visited countries and one tour, that is the limit of what these data can say, and more tours, later in an artist's career, would be needed to narrow it.

What this means

Measured: in the week of a first concert in a country, Harry Styles's share of its Spotify chart rose by about {{w0_rel}} % against countries not yet visited; from the week after, no week is clearly above zero. Over the registered five weeks the average effect is positive but {{label}}. For someone planning a tour, what the data support is narrow: a show week brought a spike in local listening, carried mostly by later and smaller markets, and there is no sign, in this tour, of a lasting gain in the chart, though the study could not have detected a small one. That fits a picture in which most of the people at a show were already listeners and played the songs before and after it, while the rest of the country did not catch on. Whether a larger tour, a later career stage or a country's first visit by a star would differ is open; the same design could be run on other artists' tours.

What this does not show

Only the Top 200 is published, so listening below it is invisible; in a country where he was outside the chart before and after the show, a rise that did not reach the chart counts as nothing. Spotify is one service, and radio, video and the show itself are not measured. The estimate bundles everything that came with a show: the performance, the press and social media around it, and fans preparing for it. The tour started months after his first album, so the effect is for an artist on his first record and may not hold for one with many. His second and larger tour, Love On Tour (2021–2023), would be the natural test, but the public daily charts end in 2021, when that tour had played only in the United States; he has not toured since July 2023. Countries were not visited at random: promoters chose large and nearby markets first, and the method relies on the countries visited later being a fair comparison for those visited earlier, which the weeks before the shows only partly support: one of them has a band that excludes zero, and the weeks just before the shows drift slightly down. The estimate is for a first visit only. Nothing here says anything about revenue.
