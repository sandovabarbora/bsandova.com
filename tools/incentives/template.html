Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • In the three years after a firm was promised a state incentive for at least 300 jobs, the district's registered unemployment share moved by {{q1}} relative to districts without such a promise. That is the opposite sign from the fall the author expected, but the 90 % interval, {{q1_ci90}}, includes small falls as well as small rises. The registered label is underpowered: the interval is {{width}} points wide, just over the limit of {{width_max}}.
  • Even the largest promise, {{ex_jobs}} jobs for Foxconn in Kutná Hora, about {{ex_per100}} for every 100 residents of working age, came with a raw change of {{ex_change}} against comparison districts, less than a tenth of what the jobs would mean if each had gone to a registered job seeker of the district. Across all promises, the data are consistent with a fall of at most about a {{q3_lo_frac}} of that bound, or none.
  • The sign is fragile. Without the {{n_span}} promises whose years straddle January 2013, when the ministry changed the definition of its published unemployment share, the estimate is {{k2013}} (90 % {{k2013_ci90}}). Neither sign is supported.
  • This is association around a decision date in districts that firms chose, not a measured effect of incentives; no public record says how many promised jobs were created or who took them.

Czech governments have long offered tax relief and grants to firms that build or expand a plant and promise to hire. The question here is whether registered unemployment in a district fell after a large promise was made there, relative to districts that got no large promise at the time. Between {{year_first}} and {{year_last}}, {{events}} incentive decisions promised at least 300 jobs in a single district outside Prague with no other promise of that size there in the two years before or the four years after; they fell in {{districts}} districts. The study compares each with {{comp_min}} to {{comp_max}} districts without such a promise, using the CzechInvest list of projects[[czechinvest]] and the monthly district figures of the Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs (MPSV) from July 2004 to December 2024[[mpsv]]. The questions and the rules for reading the result were fixed under a signed tag before any unemployment figure for a district was read.

In years 1 to 3 after the decision (year 0, the first twelve months, is left out), the unemployment share in these districts moved by {{q1}} relative to the comparison districts, measured against the two years before. A rise is the opposite sign from a fall, and it is small: the 90 % interval runs from {{q1_ci90}}, so it includes a fall of a tenth of a point as well as a rise of three tenths. When each promise is handed instead to a district drawn at random from its own comparison pool, more than half the draws give an estimate at least this far from zero (p = {{p}}). Year by year, the coefficients stay within about a tenth of a point of the year before the decision (fig. 1).

The registered label is underpowered: the 90 % interval is {{width}} points wide, just over the {{width_max}} the rule allows. With {{events}} promises, only a change of about {{sdc}} points either way could have been told from zero. The design named this label as likely before any figure was read.

How far to trust it

Three things limit what these numbers can say. Firms chose where to invest, and until September 2019 the law halved the minimum investment for a plant in a district whose unemployment was at least half as high again as the national rate[[nku]], so the districts with a promise are not a random draw. The list records promises, not plants: {{cancelled}} of the {{events}} promises were later cancelled and {{unverified}} is marked for verification, and the Supreme Audit Office found that the ministry did not assess how far the jobs created matched those promised, nor track how many went to people registered at labour offices[[nku]]. And the study changed before its outcome was read. The population field the registered outcome needed turned out to be missing from every MPSV file, and the registered rules left 12 events, under the minimum of {{min_events}}. Before any unemployment figure was read, the author approved MPSV's own published unemployment share as the outcome, a clean window starting two years rather than four before the decision, and {{block_jobs}} rather than 200 jobs for a project that keeps a district out of the comparison or the events. Everything added after the results, the per-promise figures, the map and the example district, is marked as such and changes no label.

The {{districts}} districts

The promises are spread across the country, from Cheb in the west to Ostrava in the east (fig. 2). Most were for 300 to 600 jobs, typically about {{q3_med}} jobs for every 100 residents aged 15 to 64. Two promises far enough apart to count twice fell in each of {{n_twice}} districts: {{twice}}. The largest were {{largest}}. The map shows each district's promises and, as one of its views, the raw change in its gap with its comparison districts; a single district's change is not an estimate, as one district's share moves with much besides a promise.

One district: {{ex_name}}

The largest promise in the study went to {{ex_company}}: {{ex_jobs}} jobs at {{ex_name}}, decided on {{ex_date}}, in a district of about {{ex_pop}} residents aged 15 to 64, so {{ex_per100}} jobs for every 100 of them. Registered check 1 sets this promise aside, and it is shown here for that reason; it was chosen after the per-promise figures had been computed. In the two years before the decision, {{ex_name}}'s unemployment share was on average {{ex_g_pre_abs}} points above the mean of its {{ex_comp}} comparison districts. In years 1 to 3, which run from spring 2008 through the 2009 recession to early 2011, it was {{ex_g_post_abs}} points below it, a raw change of {{ex_change}}. By year 3 the share was {{ex_t_3}} % in {{ex_name}} and {{ex_c_3}} % on average in the comparison districts.

Had every promised job taken one registered job seeker of the district off the register, the share would have fallen by about {{ex_per100}} points. The raw change is {{ex_share}} of that, less than a tenth. One district is not evidence of anything: the recession reached districts differently, the list does not say how many people the plant hired or from where, and {{ex_name}} is here because of its size, not its result.

How large a fall was possible

The third registered question puts the change on the scale of the promises. On average a promise was {{q3_den}} jobs per 100 residents aged 15 to 64, pulled up by {{ex_name}}: without it {{q3_den_wo}}, and for the median promise {{q3_med}}. Dividing Q1 by that average gives Q3, {{q3}} (90 % {{q3_ci90}}). On this scale −1 is an arithmetic bound: every promised job going to a registered unemployed resident of the district, which lowers MPSV's share, the unemployed per 100 residents aged 15 to 64 in its definition since 2013, by the promise's jobs per 100. The point estimate has the other sign, and the interval's lower end, {{q3_lo}}, is consistent with falls of up to about a {{q3_lo_frac}} of that bound, not more. Q3 is descriptive and carries no label.

The bound is not a forecast. Jobs go to commuters, to people leaving other jobs and to workers from abroad; the audit office notes extra state spending on more police and on checks of the employment and housing of foreign workers around the Solnice–Kvasiny industrial zone, next to Škoda's plant at Kvasiny, one of the promises here[[nku]]. Promises are also cancelled or scaled down. Fig. 3, drawn after the results, shows the promises one by one. Their raw changes scatter from {{chg_min}} to {{chg_max}} whatever the size of the promise, {{neg}} below zero and {{pos}} above. Of those below zero, {{n_below}} fell by more than their promise could account for even under the bound, {{below}}; the movement from district to district is larger than any change the promises could plausibly bring, which is why {{events}} of them cannot pin the average down.

The check that flipped the sign

Twelve registered checks repeat the analysis with one thing changed, fourteen estimates in all, and most land between zero and a sixth of a point above it (fig. 4). Two do not: the {{k1000_n}} promises of at least 1 000 jobs, too few to read, and check 12. In January 2013 MPSV changed how its published unemployment share is defined, and the outcome here is that published share in every month. A step at that date need not be the same in every district, so before the outcome was read the change log added check 12: the estimate without the {{n_span}} promises whose years −2 to 3 contain January 2013. With the {{k2013_n}} that remain, in {{k2013_d}} districts, it is {{k2013}} (90 % {{k2013_ci90}}). Of the {{n_span}} left out, {{span_pos}} had a raw change above zero, with a mean of {{span_mean}} (fig. 3, coral). That could come from the definition change, from the years {{span_from}} to {{span_to}} those windows cover, or from chance among ten districts; the data cannot tell these apart. Its interval and Q1's both include zero, so the flip shows how fragile the sign is, not that promises lowered unemployment.

Leaving out the {{cancelled}} cancelled and {{unverified}} unverified promises gives {{kcanc}}, practically zero. The other checks stay on the positive side of zero with intervals that include it: without {{ex_name}} {{klarge}}, statutory incentives only {{kstat}}, years 1 to 5 {{k15}}, comparison districts from the same region {{kregion}} or the same third of unemployment before the promise {{kterc}}. The {{k1000_n}} promises of at least 1 000 jobs give {{k1000}} with an interval from {{k1000_ci90}}. A placebo that moves each decision two years earlier, so that every year it compares lies before the real decision, gives {{kplac}} (90 % {{kplac_ci90}}), half the size of Q1 with an interval as wide. Between years −2 and −1 the promised districts' share rose by {{pre}} relative to the comparisons (95 % {{pre_ci95}}); the interval includes zero, so the label needs no qualifier, but two years before a promise say little about earlier paths.

The author's expectation

On 16 October 2026, before reading anything about incentives or their evaluation, the author wrote that she expected a large promise to lower unemployment in the district a little, barely visible in district-wide figures, with no timing stated. The point estimate has the other sign, and the interval includes small falls of the kind she expected, down to {{q1_lo90}} points. The data neither support the expectation nor rule it out.

What this means

For someone weighing an incentive, the measured quantity is the change in a district's registered unemployment share in the three years after a promise of at least 300 jobs, relative to districts without one: {{q1}}, with a 90 % interval from {{q1_ci90}}. Read against the size of the promises, any fall consistent with the data is small, at most about a {{q3_lo_frac}} of what the promised jobs would mean if each went to a registered job seeker of the district, and the sign of the estimate turns on the {{n_span}} promises made from {{span_y0}} to {{span_y1}}. Whether incentives create jobs, and for whom, is a different question that the public data cannot answer, since no record links promised jobs to the people who took them[[nku]].

What this does not show

It does not show an effect of incentives in either direction. Firms chose the districts, the rules favoured districts with high unemployment until 2019, and a district with one large promise and no other for six years may differ from districts that attract many. The decision date is not the day hiring began: projects are announced earlier and plants open later, so a change can fall in the reference years or after year 3. Jobs taken by commuters show in neighbouring districts, some of them comparison districts. Registered unemployment also moves with the rules for registering and with benefits, and the published share changed its definition in 2013, which check 12 shows matters for the sign.

Method, details and reproduction

Method: data, events, model and labels

Data and events

The projects are the CzechInvest dashboard table of {{rows}} projects, {{rows_ipo}} of them with an incentive decision[[czechinvest]]. A project's place, a free-text field, is matched to a municipality and so to its district as of 2024. An event is a decision with at least {{event_jobs}} promised jobs in one district outside Prague, decided between {{first_decision}} and {{last_decision}}, so that years −2 to 3 lie inside the data. Of {{cand}} such decisions, {{drop_window}} fall outside those dates, {{drop_prague}} are in Prague, {{drop_multi}} name several places and {{drop_clean}} fail the clean window: another decision of at least {{block_jobs}} jobs in the same district from {{win_before}} months before to {{win_after}} months after. Cancelled and unverified promises stay in, so the estimate is of a promise. The comparison districts of an event are those outside Prague with no such decision in the same window, {{comp_min}} to {{comp_max}} per event.

The outcome is MPSV's published share of unemployed persons, podilNezamestnanychOsobPNO, in percentage points, for each district and month[[mpsv]], averaged over each twelve-month event year; year 0 starts in the month of the decision. The population aged 15 to 64 in Q3's denominator comes from ČSÚ's year-end stocks[[csu]], interpolated to months and averaged over year −1; it enters nothing else.

How this was done

Each event has its own panel of the treated district and its comparison districts over years −2 to 3. Stacked over events, a weighted least-squares regression of the outcome on treated × event-year indicators, with district-by-event and year-by-event fixed effects, gives a coefficient per year relative to year −1, the stacked design of Cengiz and co-authors[[cengiz]]. The treated district weighs 1 and each of the n comparison districts 1/n, so every event counts once on each side; because every event has all six years, Q1 equals the mean over events of each district's own gap change, which fig. 3 uses. Q1 is the mean of the year 1–3 coefficients minus the mean of years −2 and −1. Intervals come from {{draws}} bootstrap draws of whole districts (seed {{seed_boot}}; about {{events_per_draw}} events per draw, none redrawn and none failed) and the p-value from {{draws}} random reassignments of each event's treated district within its pool (seed {{seed_perm}}). Computed in Python with NumPy and pandas[[software]].

Q1 takes the first registered label that applies: lower or higher unemployment after the promise if the 95 % interval excludes zero, the estimate is at least {{effect_min}} points from it and the randomisation p-value is at most 0.05; no change larger than {{band}} points if the 90 % interval lies within ±{{band}}; underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than {{width_max}} points; inconclusive otherwise. A 95 % interval of year −1 minus year −2 that excluded zero would have added “with differing paths in the two years before the promise”. With fewer than {{min_events}} events or {{min_districts}} districts no label would be given.

Before registration and before the outcome

The design discloses what was seen before registration (§0). The author had read nothing about Czech incentives. AI agents, told not to read any unemployment figure, downloaded the project list and counted projects by year, size and cancellation, read the field names of the MPSV files, and read the audit office's report; on counts of the list alone the threshold of 300 jobs was set. On 26 October 2026, from field presence and event counts only, the data step found the population field missing and 12 events left, and the author approved the changes described above; a second change, the same day and still before any outcome, took Q3's denominator from ČSÚ and added check 12. Both are in the change log with the counts as amended: {{events}} events in {{districts}} districts, {{boundary_treated}} of them in a district whose boundary changed in 2007 or 2016.

Reproduction and change log

Every number on this page is filled in by a script from results.json, the screening file and districts.json. From the repository root, with the raw files in tools/data/incentives/:

  1. tools/incentives/fetch.py, czechinvest.py and screen.py: the project list, the MPSV and ČSÚ files and the screening, with place matching in projects.py.
  2. estimate.py --unblind: the registered estimates and checks, with the Q3 population in population.py and tests on made-up data in test_incentives.py.
  3. districts.py: the per-promise figures, the map and the example district, after the results.
  4. article.py: the figures and this page.

The design is docs/research/incentives-design.md. Every change after registration, dated, including both deviations and the figures added after the results, is in the change log.
