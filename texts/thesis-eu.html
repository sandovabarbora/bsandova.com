Franco-German voting after the Treaty of Aachen (bachelor's thesis, 2021)

A note on a bachelor's thesis that scraped votes in the EU Council, the European Parliament and two national parliaments to ask whether voting changed after the 2019 Treaty of Aachen.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • The 2019 treaty promised closer French and German cooperation inside the EU; the thesis looked for it in votes.
  • In the EU Council their votes did not move closer; in the European Parliament agreement on several topics rose, possibly an artefact of coding and of the new Parliament elected in May 2019.
  • Both national parliaments approved more EU items after 2019, but that measures each chamber on its own, on few items, and depends on what was tabled.
  • It rests on 1 588 Council and 2 788 Parliament vote records and 129 Bundestag and 499 Assemblée nationale items. The comparisons are before and after only, with no comparison country, so the treaty cannot be credited with the changes; comparing other large-country pairs, or holding the agenda fixed, could erase them.

The question

In January 2019 France and Germany signed the Treaty of Aachen, which renewed the Élysée Treaty of 1963 and committed the two countries to coordinate more closely, including inside the European Union's institutions. Two years later, this thesis asked whether that showed in how they voted: in the Council, where governments vote, in the European Parliament, where their members sit in party groups, and in the two national parliaments when they dealt with European matters1. It drew on the literature on coalitions in the Council, which explains the Council's high rate of consensus as a by-product of governments trying to form blocking coalitions3. The university later named it the faculty's best bachelor's thesis in its ESOP 2021 competition2.

What was done

Votes from 2014 to April 2021 were scraped with Python from VoteWatch Europe, the Assemblée nationale and the Bundestag, cleaned in SQL, and split into the period before the treaty was signed in January 2019 and after it. The treaty entered into force only in January 2020, so the first year of the "after" period, 2019, comes before it applied. For the two EU institutions the thesis compared how often the two countries voted the same way by topic, computed correlations between countries' voting records, and used what the thesis called Shapley values (in fact a machine-learning attribution, see below) to ask which partners would most help each country's coalitions. For the national parliaments it counted how often European items were approved. A short text analysis compared news headlines from 2014–2015 and 2020–2021, found in 2021 through Google News searches that included the phrase "Franco-German cooperation".

What it found

In the Council the two countries already agreed on foreign and security policy in every recorded vote, before and after the treaty; agreement on agriculture fell from 86 % to 82 %. The correlation between their voting records stayed near zero, −0.088 before and −0.084 after. The two do not conflict: each vote was coded 1 for yes and 0 for anything else (in the published code absences too, although the text says they were dropped); a body that mostly votes yes leaves the correlation little to work with, so near zero does not mean disagreement. In the European Parliament, agreement rose on several topics and no topic showed a fall; on foreign and security policy it went from about 1 % to about 64 %. The thesis does not say how a country's many members were counted as agreeing, and a jump that size is hard to read as behaviour: it may come from coding, and the split falls just before the May 2019 election, which elected a new Parliament. The correlation between French and German members went from 0.063 to −0.024, near zero either way. The largest changes were national: the Bundestag approved 29 % of European items before the treaty and 75 % after, the Assemblée nationale 25 % and 45 %. These are each chamber's own approval rates on few items (45 in the Bundestag after), not a measure of the two voting together, and they move with what was tabled, as the thesis notes. In the headlines, the phrase "Franco-German" went from 25 % in 2014 to 67 % in 2020, base not stated; because the searches used the phrase, the levels partly reflect the query, though both periods used the same queries. The thesis concluded that the treaty was being carried out "partly", more in the parliaments than in the governments' votes; that is the thesis's reading, not one this note endorses, since a before-and-after comparison without a comparison country cannot credit the treaty with any of these changes.

What I would do differently now

Every comparison is before and after, with no comparison pair of countries, so a change in what was put to a vote, an election or the pandemic in 2020 could explain as much as the treaty; a difference-in-differences (the France–Germany change set against the change for other pairs of large member states) would be the minimum. Agreement by topic depends on which items came up in each period, and the thesis does not hold the agenda fixed. The "Shapley values" were computed with a machine-learning library that attributes a model's predictions to its inputs, not the cooperative-game Shapley value of a coalition. In the code they come from a random-forest model plotted for a single vote, and its inputs appear to include the country being predicted, so they say little about prediction and nothing about coalitions. And the European Parliament is better read through roll-call scaling4, which places each member on a political axis from all their votes, than through country correlations. The data work was real and is where this site's habits began; the inference is what I would now design before collecting anything.
