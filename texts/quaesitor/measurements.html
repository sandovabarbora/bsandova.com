Same score, opposite characters

A silent failure is an answer that runs without error, looks plausible and is wrong, with nothing downstream to say it should not be trusted. Every text-to-SQL benchmark reports accuracy. Accuracy folds two very different things, declining and being confidently wrong, into one "not correct", and for anyone actually deploying one of these assistants that is the one distinction that matters1.

So I built warehouses with the traps in known places (a null inside an aggregate here, a batch loaded twice there; the full list is the second text), wrote a verified query for every question by hand, and let the models answer. Fifteen languages, three repeats each, one verdict per cell. The numbers on this page are the run's, not mine; the pages they were first published on carry the fingerprints12.

Two models, one score, two personalities

Read the "correct" column first. The two models land within a few answers of each other on both packs, level on one. Now read the last column.

One declines 55 times, the other once. They do not differ in what they know. They differ in what they do when they do not know it1. A declined answer is friction: somebody notices, somebody asks. A confident wrong one is a number that looks exactly right and walks straight into a slide. Picking the "better" model does not fix this. It relocates it.

The cleaner and more designed the data, the better the models look. On a live taxi meter both models were silently wrong four times in five, and the cautious one that declined 59 times on my retail pack declined zero times here. My synthetic warehouse was not unfairly hard. It was easier than reality.

The one worth sitting with, from TPC-DS. Asked how many distinct products exist in the catalogue:

COUNT(DISTINCT i_item_sk), the surrogate key: one row per version of an item, so twice too many. Reaching for DISTINCT is what a careful person does. It was applied to the column that makes it wrong.

On the taxi data one model answered a question that has no answer at all: the average tip across 439 191 cash journeys, where the meter never records a tip3.

The query ran. The answer was zero. Nobody had any customers that month.

Ten questions that reach past the edge of what the warehouse holds. Every column they need exists, so every query runs clean and returns a number, and the number answers "how many rows match", not the question6. The clearest: how many customers registered in July 2026? Orders run through August 2026, so the period looks safe. One model wrote this:

SELECT COUNT(*) AS new_customers
FROM dim_customer
WHERE registered_date >= DATE '2026-07-01'
  AND registered_date <  DATE '2026-08-01'
  AND is_test_account = FALSE

That is a careful query; it even excludes test accounts. It returns zero. What it does not know is that the customer dimension stopped being written to in February 2025, eighteen months earlier. Zero means the table is not being filled, not that nobody signed up, and nothing in the result tells the two apart. A dimension that quietly stops loading while the facts keep arriving is one of the most ordinary things that goes wrong in a warehouse. Both models answered both questions in that category with a number6.

Declining is the right answer to all ten. In Czech, model A declined five of ten; model B, none. Across fifteen languages A declined two to five per language, B zero or one6. An earlier version of this pack asked for things with no column behind them (a gross margin where nothing records cost) and both models scored ten out of ten: noticing a missing column is a lookup, not a judgement. The hard version keeps every column and removes only the data. That is the version that separates them.

Documentation made the errors smaller, not rarer

The obvious fix is context: hand the model documentation of the schema and the metrics. So I did, on the retail pack, 26 questions, three repeats, Czech, one input changed and the digests of both runs recorded so that the "one input" claim is checkable rather than asserted2.

Correct answers: 2 of 26 before, 2 of 26 after. Exact McNemar, p = 1.00. What moved was the size of the error: across the 22 questions measurable in both runs the median absolute error fell from 72 % to 23 %; 15 answers moved closer, 2 further; exact sign test p = 0.0023. It replicated three times, at p = 0.00008, 0.00073 and 0.00232.

Read that again from the reader's side. Every answer that had been obviously wrong came back plausible, and none went the other way. A revenue figure off by six hundred per cent returned off by seventy. The error did not shrink to nothing. It shrank to a size nobody queries. The prediction going in was that documentation would suppress refusals; it did not. The effect is real and it was not the predicted one.

Limits, before anyone else states them: 26 questions, one warehouse, one model, one language, two runs. This is not evidence about documentation in general, and it does not show that documentation raised the silent-failure rate (77 % → 85 %, p = 0.73, noise at this size). It shows where the risk goes when the answers get better: out of the column a reader checks, into the one nobody does2.
