Trust grows faster than accuracy

A model that is right nine times out of ten is more dangerous than one that is right four. At four in ten you check everything. At nine you stop, because checking is expensive and it has been right all week. The failure does not go away as the model improves. It goes quiet.

Ask a data assistant how many customers made a second purchase within thirty days of their first. The right query counts thirty days from the first purchase and returns 621. A model that anchors the window to the registration date returns 684, which it did in ninety-five attempts across my runs1. Both are valid SQL. Both run. The answer is ten per cent too high, and nobody notices, because ten per cent is a perfectly ordinary-looking number. An answer off by eighty-five per cent gets queried. This one gets used.

That is a silent failure, and it is not an edge case. It is the normal case, the one accuracy averages away. Quaesitor was a one-person service built to measure it: put the assistant in front of a warehouse, ask questions with a hand-verified answer, count how often it is wrong without saying so, sign the result. The service is closed. This text is what I would want to keep from it.

Why the gap was open

Three kinds of tool sit next to this problem and none is in it. Semantic layers define what a metric means, which is the best single thing you can do, and still do not verify that the definition matches the data or that the model used it. Eval and observability platforms measure consistency, latency, cost and text-level hallucination; they cannot decide whether a number is factually right, because that needs the metric's definition, the grain and the data model. Governance platforms watch who asked what and whether data left the building; they tell you the assistant stayed within the rules, not that the answer was true. The hole between them needs data engineering, metric definitions and security at once, and almost nobody stands in all three2.

The more honest reason: compute is the cheapest part. A full run of the measurement costs a couple of dollars. What takes weeks is the verified query behind every question, written by hand against a real schema, reconciled against a number somebody already trusts, read clause by clause with the person who owns the definition. On your warehouse nobody knows the right answer in advance; if they did there would be nothing to check. That work cannot be automated, and it is exactly the work everyone skips.

How a question became a verified one

Five steps, in the order they happen3. The owner of a metric states its definition in writing. A query is written to implement it and read against the definition clause by clause. The query is reconciled against a figure the business already trusts, a certified report or a finance close. The question is then asked of the assistant, three times, in the languages the business uses, and every answer is classified mechanically: correct, silently wrong, implausible, declined, with the rule that fired printed next to it, because a service arguing that confident output needs a signal should not rest its own number on a model's judgement. And the whole set, questions, both queries, builders and runs with their fingerprints, is handed over rather than asserted. A verified query can be wrong; that is this method's own silent failure, and one such correction was stated in a client's report before they could find it4.

Two rules the site enforced on itself, which I have kept for everything since: no figure is typed into a page, every one is injected from the run at build time, and the build refuses when the runs stop supporting a sentence. It has happened. That was the point.

None of this was my claim to make

Eight things the work depends on were established by people with no stake in it. A service that implied otherwise would be worth less, not more5.

A benchmark number does not transfer to your warehouse. The same class of model that scores in the eighties on public benchmarks reaches 10.8 % on 9 128 questions taken from real private warehouses, and 30.1 % even with oracle hints67. Whatever accuracy a vendor quotes was not measured on your schema.

The failure arrives looking plausible. Across 4 602 incorrect queries, 81.2 % are schema-level errors: the wrong column, the wrong sense of a term, returning a correctly formatted number in the expected range8. Worked through independently on a 35-table schema: row duplication returns 271 049 where 154 050 is correct, and in the author's words nothing flags it9.

It is the majority of what goes wrong. A taxonomy validated against fifty real incidents from court documents and regulatory filings finds 53 % of verifiable public failures were silent10.

Declining is correct behaviour, not a shortfall. In dataset retrieval on the open web, a broader agent answered 40 % more questions and was 65.7 % less precise for it11; the same shape of decision, trust the model, trust the context, or abstain, is scored across roughly 69 000 instances elsewhere12. My abstention finding separates two models on exactly this axis, further than anything else I ran.

A model agreeing with itself is not evidence it is right. Under dense retrieval, 59 % of errors showed zero dispersion across five samples: the system agreed with itself every time and was wrong every time13. In my runs, keeping only answers where all three attempts agreed left the error rate at 84.9 % against 85.1 %, and cost twenty points of coverage. Agreement is free to measure and worth almost nothing.

The language of the question is a variable. An 8.79 % accuracy gap outside English is reported and reproducible14, which is why the suite ran fifteen languages rather than assuming. My own result there is a null one, and published as such.

The bill lands on the organisation, not the pipeline. A wrong number is an engineering event for a day; afterwards it is a decision somebody made and cannot source15.

And the best objection: a semantic layer fixes it. Give the model your metric definitions and accuracy rises by seventeen to twenty-three points across three frontier models, 50.5 → 67.7 %, 46.5 → 68.7 %, 45.5 → 68.7 %16. It is the strongest measured case against the service, and it installs the argument rather than refuting it: sixty-eight per cent is not the accuracy at which people check. It is the accuracy at which they stop. And the benchmark reports accuracy without classifying the errors, so it cannot say whether the remaining third got easier or harder to notice. My replication says that is exactly where the risk moves: documentation left correctness unchanged and made the surviving errors more plausible.

What is left

The name is older than the software. A quaesitor was the Roman examining magistrate who presided over a court of inquiry: the one who investigated the facts and signed the finding. The office is two thousand years older than the problem and the job is the same one. I liked that, and I still do.

The practice is closed. Three things stay. The runs: 5 790 measured answers as of August 2026, fifteen languages, five warehouses, published with fingerprints4. The harness that produced them, open17. And quaesitor-zero: a tiny check that generates questions your schema cannot answer and scores whether your assistant admits it. Apache-2.0, on PyPI, no network code, no telemetry, no model access, so it cannot report to anyone what it found on your data. You run it, you keep the result18. Replies are classified mechanically and every classification prints the rule that fired.

Not an accredited certification, not a conformity assessment, not a statutory audit; a finding, signed and dated, with the queries attached so that somebody who disagrees can show where. That sentence was on every page of the service. It is the one I am proudest of.
