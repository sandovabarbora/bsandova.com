Research · transit · Prague, 4 July 2025

The day Prague's trams stopped

When the Czech grid split at noon on 4 July 2025, 56 % of Prague's tram service stood still, half of it was moving again by 13:30 and nine tenths by 15:00.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • At 11:59 on Friday 4 July 2025 28 % of the Czech grid's load was cut off, including part of Prague. On 259 of 445 frequent tram segments (a segment is the stretch from one stop to the next), no tram passed for twenty minutes; buses ran on.
  • Service came back in about three hours: half of the usual trams by 13:30, nine tenths by 15:00. Measured against 13:01, when the second of the two substations feeding Prague was back, a quarter of the stopped segments saw a tram again before it and the median 28 minutes after it.
  • The registered delay measure, the median delay, was back in its ordinary range at 14:10, before service was. It cannot be read as recovery: after the restart, half of the trams were recorded as running early, some by hours, against the trips the data matched them to, and early and late cancel in a median. Described after the results: at 14:10, 80 % of tram passes were more than five minutes from their matched trip, and at 18:10 still 22 %. Whether the trams ran off the timetable or the data matched them to the wrong trips, the data cannot tell.
  • One day in one city: a description of how this network came back, not a rule for others. Every comparison is with the same rule run on ordinary Fridays (a placebo). The metro check missed by up to 32 minutes, so every restart time here is uncertain by about that much.

It was a Friday in early July, just before lunch. People stood at tram stops across Prague, checking the board for the next tram. On much of the network it did not come.

The trouble started far from the city. At 11:51 on Friday 4 July 2025, the 400 kV line between Hradec and Výškov in north-west Bohemia tripped. Eight minutes later, just before noon, the affected part of the Czech grid separated and collapsed. About 2 300 MW went off, 28 % of the country's load at that moment, in four regions and part of Prague1.

Getting the power back took the rest of the day. Dispatchers gave priority to the two substations that feed Prague, the big stations that take power off the national grid for the city. Malešice was back at 12:30 and Chodov at 13:01. By 14:09 every 400 and 220 kV substation was switched back on (energised), and all the load was back at 17:351. The energy regulator's working group later named Prague as the main region where a power cut quickly leads to a collapse of transport2.

A transport news site followed the afternoon live. Trams on the right bank of the Vltava stopped, while those on the left bank kept going. The metro ran again from 12:15 and 12:35, and at 15:16 "tram service returned to normal"3.

This study goes back over that afternoon using the record of every tram passing every stop. It asks how much of the tram service stopped and how fast it came back, and then how long the timetable took to catch up. The questions and their rules were registered before that day's records were read on their own.

Data

The stop-time history of Prague Integrated Transport, collected from Golemio for the author's master's thesis, has one row for each trip from one stop to the next, with the scheduled and the observed time to the second45. Here a pass is a row with an observed arrival at the next stop, in plain words one tram getting from a stop to the next. A segment is an ordered pair of stops, the stretch of track between them, so each direction counts as its own segment. Each pass is matched to a trip in the timetable, and its delay is measured against that trip. Earlier studies on this site used these data with 4 July as one of about 170 days in season-wide sums. None of them showed that day on its own, and the author had not looked at it.

The day is compared with the three Fridays that followed, 11, 18, 25 July, which ran the same summer timetable. Every number is set against a placebo, the same rule applied to each of those Fridays against the other two. For the headline rules the placebo gives 12:00 on every ordinary Friday, and a value of 4 July inside the placebo range is not read as an effect of the outage. Buses do not run on traction power, so they show whether the data feed itself was alive. It was, with bus passes above four fifths of an ordinary Friday in every ten minutes of the day.

How this was done

For every ten minutes from 10:00 to 22:00, the number of passes of each mode on 4 July is divided by the mean of the ordinary Fridays in the same ten minutes. Half back is the first ten minutes from noon that is itself at or above 0.5 and opens an hour in which at most one ten-minute bin dips below it. Nine tenths back is the same at 0.9. A segment stopped if no tram passed it between 12:10 and 12:30, among segments with at least three trams in those twenty minutes on ordinary Fridays. Its estimated restart is its first tram after 12:10, less half its usual interval between trams. Delay was registered as the median delay of passes in each ten minutes, back to normal when it stays inside the range of the ordinary Fridays, widened by 30 seconds or a quarter of their mean, whichever is larger.

Rules in detail, checks, and what changed

All questions are descriptive. One event cannot show what makes a network recover faster, and no result carries a label. Screening had a rule that a ten-minute bin is suspect if buses or trains fall below half of their ordinary level. Trains were the wrong reference, since most Czech lines are electric and the outage stopped them too; with them, 29 bins of 4 July were flagged, and that output was seen. The rule was then applied to buses alone, as meant, and flagged none. That change was made after seeing those flags, so it depends on the data, but before any ratio, pass count or delay of the day was summarised. Tests on made-up data showed that "the first ten minutes from which five of the next six pass" lets a run open on a bin that fails; the opening bin must now pass itself, a change made before any value of 4 July was computed. Both are dated in the change log.

The metro was registered as a positive control: the median over each line's segments of the first pass after noon, against the reported restarts. Lines A and C came out at 12:07 and 12:14 (reported 12:15), line B at 12:02 (reported 12:35). Line B misses by more than ten minutes, so by the rule set in advance every restart time on this page is uncertain by about 32 minutes. Why line B shows passes so soon after noon is not known. The metro also ran at only 0.76 of an ordinary Friday's passes at 11:40, before the outage, so its curves are drawn but its times are not read. Buses came out at 12:00 on every rule, as on an ordinary day. Trolleybuses, registered as curves only, are drawn in fig. 1.

Checks of the tram times: with a threshold of 0.85, nine tenths back is 14:50; counted from departures, 15:00; requiring all six bins in the hour, 15:00 (placebo up to 12:40); with bins of 5 and 15 minutes, 15:00 and 15:00. Over 12:10–12:40 the stopped share is 52 %. The mean delay, instead of the median, is back in range at 19:10 (placebo 12:00). Two checks could not be made as registered: the archived PID disruption pages hold no page about the outage, and the two sections reported still without power at 14:383 disagree with the data, which record trams near Starý Hloubětín by 12:54 and around Nádraží Hostivař by 13:29. Either the report was out of date or those passes belong to trips the data matched wrongly, and there is no way to tell which from these records. The June Fridays, registered as a second reference for the curves, are in the results file.

What stopped

Soon after the collapse, much of the tram network stood still. Between 12:10 and 12:30, no tram passed 259 of the 445 segments that ordinarily see at least three in that time.

Counting busy segments for more than quiet ones, by how many trams they carry on ordinary Fridays, that is 56 % of the usual tram service at that time. On those Fridays the same count gives under 1 %. Another 160 segments with fewer trams were left out, because twenty minutes without a tram is ordinary there.

Buses kept running at about their usual number (fig. 1). The stopped segments lie mostly east of the river (fig. 2), which matches the live reports.

How fast trams came back

The trams came back section by section. Tram passes stayed above half of an ordinary Friday from 13:30, and above nine tenths from 15:00. On the ordinary Fridays both rules give 12:00, and fig. 3 plays the afternoon back.

Nine tenths at 15:00 is close to the "back to normal" reported at 15:163. The rule allows one dip, and one came later, when tram passes fell to 0.68 of an ordinary Friday for ten minutes at 16:50.

Of the 259 segments that stopped, 258 saw a tram again before 22:00. The other 1 did not.

The restarts are measured from 13:01, when Chodov, the second of the two substations dispatchers restored first for Prague, was back. Of the stopped segments, 65 (25 %) had an estimated restart before it. Another 61 % restarted within the hour after it, and 14 % later. The median was 28 minutes after it. The range ran from −30 to 150 minutes (fig. 4).

The 13:01 mark is a reference point and is not read as a cause, since which substation fed which tram section is not known.

How long the timetable took

On paper, delays looked normal again early. The registered delay measure, the median delay of tram passes, was inside the ordinary range from 14:10, before nine tenths of the service was back. It should not be read as recovery.

How the data are built offers a clue. Each pass is tied to a trip in the timetable, and its delay is measured against that trip. Between 13:30 and 14:10, 51 % of 3 587 tram passes were more than two minutes early on the trip the data matched them to. A quarter were more than about 41 minutes early, and the earliest by about 7 hours.

A tram does not normally run hours ahead of its timetable. This is consistent with trams back in service being matched to timetable trips other than the ones they ran, and if so, early and late passes cancel out in the median (fig. 5, first panel). The mean delay, a registered check, came back to the ordinary range only at 19:10. On 4 July, 3 910 tram passes were more than an hour off their matched trip, early or late.

A second measure, added after the results and not registered, does not cancel out like that. It is the share of passes more than five minutes from their matched trip, early or late (fig. 5, second panel). It was 80 % at 13:10 and 80 % at 14:10. On ordinary Fridays it is at most 4 % at those times. By 16:10 it was 37 %, and at 18:10 22 %. At 19:10 it was 8 %, inside the ordinary range.

This share mixes trams running off the timetable with trams matched to the wrong trip, and these records cannot separate the two. A single "back to normal" time from this share, by the same rule, failed its own placebo, a run of that rule on the ordinary Fridays, with one of them coming out at 19:00. So none is given.

What this means

Measured directly, 56 % of Prague's usual tram service stopped when the grid failed at noon, and buses did not. Trams came back to half of their usual number by 13:30, and to nine tenths by 15:00.

For most of the afternoon after the restart, a large share of tram passes were far from the trips the data matched them to. Only around 19:00 did that share fall to an ordinary level.

The author expected trams to start running soon after the power came back, and delays to last hours longer. The registered measure did not support this, since the median delay was back in range at 14:10, before the service was. The mean delay and the share far from the matched trip are consistent with that expectation. But the first is a check and the second was chosen after seeing the results, and both may count wrongly matched trips as delay.

A possible reading, not shown here, is that a tram network needs more than power at the substation to restart. Overhead lines, signals and stranded vehicles all have to come back too. Elsewhere, in Britain in 2019, 22 trains locked out by a dip in the grid's frequency could not be restarted without a technician on site6. Studies of metro disruptions trace degradation and recovery over time and place, as figs. 1, 3 and 4 do here for one day7.

What this does not show

The study does not show why segments restarted in the order they did. Which substation fed which section, how dispatchers sent trams out and where vehicles were stranded are not in the data, and the order is tied to geography.

A tram standing between stops, a trip cancelled midway and a short turn that matches no trip in the timetable all leave no pass. So "stopped" here means that no tram was recorded.

After the restart, delay depends on which trip a tram was matched to, so how late passengers were is not known. The study says nothing about passengers, how many were stranded or for how long. It is one event, in one city, in summer, at noon.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from results.json, the screening file and posthoc.json by a script. From the repository root, with the transit database of the thesis:

  1. tools/blackout/screen.py: the baseline days and the feed flags.
  2. estimate.py: the registered estimates and checks, with tests in test_blackout.py.
  3. posthoc.py: the descriptions after the results.
  4. animation.py: the data of the animated map (fig. 3), drawn by assets/animap.js with d3.
  5. article.py: the figures, the map and this page.

The design is docs/research/blackout-design.md.
