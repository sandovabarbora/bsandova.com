Prague, measured · Part 4, the long version · pre-registered · 208 European metropolitan regions, Czech permits 2007–2024, Prague 2012–2021

Two homes for Vienna's three, measured again

Part 4 counted new homes in Prague, Vienna and Warsaw and found Prague last. Three cities cannot say why. This version puts Prague among Europe's metropolitan regions, follows Czech homes from permit to house number, and asks where in Prague the new flats go. It registered its questions, tests and decision rules before the data were joined, and reports every one whichever way it fell.

Part 4 found that Prague completes about 4.5 homes a year for every 1 000 residents, Vienna 6.6 and Warsaw 10.4. A city can build little for different reasons, and housing economics has names for them:

  • Little demand.
  • Slow procedures. Permits take long, or building takes long once permitted.
  • A supply that does not respond when prices rise. Where regulation binds, prices rather than quantities take up the demand123.

Three cities, each counted by its own office under its own definition, cannot tell these apart. This version therefore makes Prague one member of larger groups:

  • 208 European metropolitan regions counted by one census;
  • the Czech regions, month by month;
  • the 1 km squares of Prague itself.

It also borrows a Polish panel of 380 districts for the one question the Czech data cannot answer.

How this was done

The design was written and sent to three referees: a housing economist, a statistician and a specialist in official statistics. It was revised and committed to the site's public repository before any outcome was joined4. The review changed its shape:

  • "Started" dwellings are permitted ones. The Czech Statistical Office's "zahájené byty", literally dwellings started, are counted from building permits. This text therefore says "permitted dwellings", and the pipeline it measures runs from permit to house number.
  • Only three hypotheses were kept as confirmatory: H2, H4 and H5. They share a 5 % family-wise error rate through Holm's procedure5.
  • Two were demoted to estimates, each with a sentence fixed in advance for what would be said:
    • The European comparison (H1): Prague's census numerator was effectively known from Part 4.
    • The price response (H3): with one Prague and 27 quarters, no valid test exists at those thresholds.
  • Each question has a relevance check. It tests whether the model sees the expected relationship where it should. If the check fails, the result is labelled uninformative, whatever its p-value.

Power was simulated, with sealed scripts, before any outcome in the confirmatory windows was seen. The simulations gave these expectations:

  • The pipeline test would be decisive at a six-month difference unless monthly noise was as large as the signal. In Prague the noise turned out to be 3.3 times the signal. That triggered the registered rule that a failed test must be written "underpowered"; the rule never applied, because the test passed.
  • The metro test would be decisive at a 22 % density difference.
  • The builders' test would be weak.

Everything that changed after registration is listed below.

Against Europe

The 2021 census round recorded when each dwelling's building was built. Dwellings in buildings from 2011 or later, per resident and per year, measure the last decade's new supply on nearly the same definition across the 22 countries; Czechia, Spain and Croatia also count reconstructions6.

The unit is the metropolitan region, because housing markets do not stop at city limits. Prague's is Prague plus Středočeský kraj. Of Eurostat's 297 metropolitan regions, 208 in 22 countries have the census answer and the covariates.

Per resident, Prague's metropolitan region built 5.28 dwellings a year by the census count. That is 163rd of 208 from the bottom, in Europe's upper quarter; the median region built 3.55.

Counting only completions of new houses, from the Czech Statistical Office, gives 3.88. That is mid-table: 116th of 208 on raw rates, against a median of 3.50.

Vienna's region built 6.2 a year and Warsaw's 10.0: Part 4 compared Prague with two of the region's faster builders.

The registered estimate goes further. It compares each region with a prediction from what was known in 2011:

  • population growth to 2011;
  • size;
  • output per head;
  • dwellings per head;
  • whether the region holds a capital.

The prediction for each country's regions comes from a model fitted on all other countries, and the gap is scaled by how well such regions are usually predicted78.

Against that model, Prague's region ranks 119th from the bottom, Vienna 155th and Warsaw 205th:

  • Czechia. Its mean score is 0.18, and it is 8th of 22 countries from the bottom. Prague lies 0.12 above the other Czech metro regions.
  • The city alone. Prague's own region ranks 107th.
  • Rebuilt numerator. With the count rebuilt from the Czech Statistical Office's completions of new houses, Prague ranks 93rd as a metro region and 76th as a city.

The rule written in advance would call this "within the range of European peers". But the model's relevance check failed. Regions that grew faster before 2011 did not build more afterwards: the coefficient is negative. By the same rule, the ranks against the model are labelled uninformative.

A separate descriptive comparison, the raw Czech Statistical Office rate with no model, is not part of the registration. By it, Prague built the second most of the 14 Czech regions per resident: 3.7 a year, against a median of 2.1 elsewhere.

From permit to house number

Every month the Czech Statistical Office counts dwellings permitted and dwellings completed in each region. If Prague's flats take longer to finish, completions follow permits with a longer delay. The model estimates that delay as a distribution of lags between the two series, for flats in new apartment buildings, Prague against the other twelve regions. Středočeský kraj is left out of the comparison, because it absorbs Prague's overflow.

The best-fitting mean lag is 16 months in Prague and 5 months elsewhere: a difference of 11.1 months (90 % interval 8.7 to 12.6, p < 0.001). H2 passes its threshold.

The sign holds in every variant run:

  • +7.6 months against Jihomoravský kraj, home of Brno, alone;
  • +10.3 with Středočeský kraj counted as Prague;
  • +5.0 when family houses are added (p = 0.055).

The caveats come with it, several visible in fig. 2:

  • Prague's lag is weakly identified. Anything from about 14 to 38 months fits almost as well. Prague's fitted lag distribution is degenerate: all its weight falls on one lag, 16 months.
  • The estimates are stable in two narrower ways. No weight falls beyond the six-year window, and in the bootstrap the completion yield and the lag are almost uncorrelated (−0.05).
  • The contrast is clearer than Prague's own number.
  • Five months is too short to build a block of flats. Outside Prague, the fit probably also tracks common reporting timing.
  • Project size. Prague's projects may be larger, so the lag may reflect project size rather than procedure.

What the result supports is modest: once permitted, Prague's new flats reach completion later than the rest of Czechia's, by months, not weeks. It says nothing about how long the permit itself took, which is the part of the process Czech reform has targeted9.

Prices and permits

Where supply responds, more dwellings are permitted after prices rise1011. The registered estimate is the cumulative response over twelve quarters: the summed coefficients of permitted dwellings, on a log scale, on quarterly log price changes of flats from the cadastre's index, 2018–2024.

A value of 16 means that a price rise of 1 % in each of the twelve preceding quarters goes with about 16 log points more permitted dwellings; −11 means 11 fewer.

The rest of Czechia's own response is the relevance check, and it cannot be told from zero: 16 (90 % interval −22 to +54). So the comparison is uninformative. The numbers behind that label:

  • Prague's response relative to the rest is −11 (−86 to +63, with a fixed-b interval of −101 to +78). Prague is 5th of 13 regions.
  • The pre-written sentence would have read "cannot be told apart".
  • A placebo with family-house prices gives +8 (−25 to +42).
  • A shorter four-quarter version over 35 quarters gives +4 (−2 to +10).

Twenty-seven quarters of one city cannot answer this question.

Who builds, in Poland

Part 4 ended on Vienna's limited-profit associations, which built a quarter of Vienna's new homes in 2024. No yearly Austrian series by builder was found. Poland has one: in each of its 380 districts, the dwellings that municipalities, social building societies (TBS) and developers begin each year.

The registered question was whether non-market builders respond less to local price growth than developers. The question is about Poland only.

The point estimate goes the expected way: −0.56 (90 % interval −1.52 to +0.39, p = 0.17). The 242 districts act like about 63 independent clusters.

But the relevance check fails. Developers' own starts do not visibly respond to local price growth either (0.09, p = 0.32), so there is no response for the non-market builders to be smaller than. The result is uninformative, and the pre-written sentence ("non-market builders respond less") is not used.

The design-stage power check had warned of this: 19 % power at the smallest difference of interest at α = 0.0167, and 26 % at 0.05. One variant, a negative-binomial model, gives −1.52 with p = 0.046. It is the only interval that excludes zero, and it is a variant, not a finding.

Nothing here says that a Vienna-style sector would add to Prague's output. Subsidised construction can partly replace what the market would have built1213, and more homes are not the same thing as better housing. Warsaw built the most of the three cities and still had fast price growth.

Where Prague builds

Land near a metro station is worth more, so an unconstrained market would build more densely there. Near-metro squares are also largely built-out estates, where there is little land left to build on. The test counts flats completed between 2012 and census day in March 2021 in each of Prague's 524 one-kilometre squares14, holding equal two things:

  • the distance from the centre;
  • how many people already lived in the square in 201115.

The estimate is γ = −0.227 (90 % interval −0.349 to −0.105): each doubling of the distance to a station goes with about 20 % fewer new flats per square kilometre. The registered test is indeterminate.

The decision takes the larger of two p-values:

  • 0.0011 from the spatial (Conley) standard error;
  • 0.0252 from the wild bootstrap over Prague's 22 administrative districts, which act like fewer than 14 independent clusters.

Holm's second threshold is 0.025, so H5 is not supported, and the reverse test does not come close. The margin, 0.0002, is within the bootstrap's own simulation error (about 0.0016). The test was not re-run.

Every variant points the same way:

  • half-kilometre squares;
  • 2012–2024;
  • without construction types implausible for new building;
  • with distance to rail and tram stops;
  • with clustering by the 57 city districts;
  • dropping any one district, which gives −0.27 to −0.18.

In 2000–2011 the gradient was about a quarter as steep and uncertain (−0.06). Registered rules are there to stop a writer from counting a miss like this as a hit, so it stays a miss.

A supported result would not have explained itself either. Squares near stations differ from others in the land left to build on and in what the plan allows there. Those layers could not be obtained in time and were not run.

How far it holds

Registered rule: robust if at least 75 % of specifications share the sign and at least 50 % reach p < 0.05. Data: housing_extended_robust.json.

Against the demand model, which failed its relevance check, Prague stays out of both tails in every variant: from 45th of 116 to 138th of 208.

  • without GDP: 123rd of 216;
  • counting only 2016 and later: 120th of 208;
  • with the stricter 5 % threshold for unknown construction dates: 106th of 199;
  • without Spain and the other Czech metro regions, whose censuses count reconstructions, and with Prague on the rebuilt numerator: 50th of 183;
  • with a shift-share employment shock in place of population growth: 45th of 116;
  • weighted by population: 137th of 208;
  • with population growth over 2011–2021, which is endogenous to the construction it predicts: 138th of 208.

Two pipeline variants, an exponential lag and one without the COVID months, have bootstraps in which a few draws run off to unbounded lags; their tests carry no information and are counted as failures.

The Warsaw replication of the price question, with notified starts of works rather than permits, gives Warsaw a cumulative relative response of −5.7 (Warsaw is 4th of 376 districts given the same treatment). It has no test, because there is one Warsaw.

What changed after registration

Not run, because the inputs could not be obtained before the outcomes were joined:

  • the land and planning layers for the metro question: Urban Atlas, heritage zones, flood plains, the 1999 plan and line D;
  • the metro analysis by basic settlement unit;
  • Polish municipal construction times: the statistics API refused connections;
  • the stock of dwellings under construction: the Prague series has not been published since 2009;
  • one price-index variant, and the annual version of the price question;
  • an OECD source to stand beside the Doing Business figure, which was not verified in time.

Run late. Two registered checks were omitted from the first runs and added after the fact-check on 29 September 2026. Neither is in the family:

  • the metro test clustered by the 57 city districts;
  • the Warsaw replication.

Choices the design left open:

  • census reference dates come from national documentation, and several are marked for verification;
  • a metro region's core is its densest member;
  • the Czech rebuilt numerator uses monthly regional completions;
  • the within-Czechia comparison is the raw rate, with no model;
  • lags are the best of 10 starting values;
  • twelve quarterly lags leave 27 quarters, not 35;
  • the fixed-b critical value is simulated;
  • the Polish bootstrap is the unstudentised score version, and its effective number of clusters uses an independence working covariance;
  • the Polish common support is 242 districts;
  • the rail and tram distances use today's network;
  • half-kilometre squares take their parent square's density;
  • robustness bootstraps use 999 draws.

After estimation:

  • H5 was decided at p = 0.0252 against 0.025 and not re-run.
  • H2's noise was 3.3 times the signal, which triggered the registered "underpowered" wording rule. It did not apply, because H2 passed.
  • The rest of Czechia's 5-month lag and Prague's flat likelihood are flagged.
  • Two pipeline variants had diverging bootstraps.
  • Three relevance checks failed, and their results are labelled uninformative.
  • Robustness variants for H4 drop district-builder cells with no dwellings at all. The main frame had none.

What this does not show

  • No cause. The European comparison is a description, not a cause. Its demand model failed its relevance check.
  • Not the time to get a permit. The pipeline result starts at the permit.
  • Not an elasticity. Prices respond to supply as well, so the price questions measure responsiveness.
  • Not Vienna. The builders' test is Polish, and it says nothing about what limited-profit associations would do in Prague.
  • Buildings, not flats. The cadastre keeps only standing buildings, and it dates the building rather than the flats added to it.
  • Definitions and resolution. The census dates buildings too, and in Czechia, Spain and Croatia it counts their reconstruction. One-kilometre squares and fourteen regions are coarse units16.
