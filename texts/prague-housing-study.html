Prague, measured · Part 4, the long version · pre-registered · 208 European metropolitan regions, Czech permits 2007–2024, Prague 2012–2021

Two homes for Vienna's three, measured again

Part 4 counted new homes in Prague, Vienna and Warsaw and found Prague last. Three cities cannot say why. This version puts Prague among Europe's metropolitan regions, follows Czech homes from permit to house number, and asks where in Prague the new flats go. It registered its questions, tests and decision rules before the data were joined, and reports every one whichever way it fell.

Part 4 found that Prague completes about 4.5 homes a year for every 1 000 residents, Vienna 6.6 and Warsaw 10.4. A city can build little for different reasons, and housing economics has names for them:

  • Little demand.
  • Slow procedures. Permits take long, or building takes long once permitted.
  • A supply that does not respond when prices rise. Where regulation binds, prices rather than quantities take up the demand123.

Three cities, each counted by its own office under its own definition, cannot tell these apart. This version therefore makes Prague one member of larger groups:

  • 208 European metropolitan regions counted by one census;
  • the Czech regions, month by month;
  • the 1 km squares of Prague itself.

It also borrows a Polish panel of 380 districts for the one question the Czech data cannot answer.

How this was done

The design was written and sent to three referees: a housing economist, a statistician and a specialist in official statistics. It was revised and committed to the site's public repository before any outcome was joined4. The review changed its shape:

  • "Started" dwellings are permitted ones. The Czech Statistical Office's "zahájené byty", literally dwellings started, are counted from building permits. This text therefore says "permitted dwellings", and the pipeline it measures runs from permit to house number.
  • Only three hypotheses were kept as confirmatory: H2, H4 and H5. They share a 5 % family-wise error rate through Holm's procedure5.
  • Two were demoted to estimates, each with a sentence fixed in advance for what would be said:
    • The European comparison (H1): Prague's census numerator was effectively known from Part 4.
    • The price response (H3): with one Prague and 27 quarters, no valid test exists at those thresholds.
  • Each question has a relevance check. It tests whether the model sees the expected relationship where it should. If the check fails, the result is labelled uninformative, whatever its p-value.

Power was simulated, with sealed scripts, before any outcome was seen. The simulations predicted three things:

  • the pipeline test would be decisive at a six-month difference;
  • the metro test would be decisive at a 22 % density difference;
  • the builders' test would be weak.

Against Europe

The 2021 census round asked every European country the same question of every dwelling: when was its building built? Dwellings in buildings from 2011 or later, per resident and per year, measure the last decade's new supply on one definition in every country6.

The unit is the metropolitan region, because housing markets do not stop at city limits. Prague's is Prague plus Středočeský kraj. Of Eurostat's 297 metropolitan regions, 208 in 22 countries have the census answer and the covariates.

Each region's number is compared with a prediction from what was known in 2011:

  • population growth to 2011;
  • size;
  • output per head;
  • dwellings per head;
  • whether the region holds a capital.

The prediction for each country's regions comes from a model fitted on all other countries, and the gap is scaled by how well such regions are usually predicted78.

Prague's metropolitan region built 5.3 dwellings per 1 000 residents a year and ranks 119th from the bottom: a little above prediction, in the middle of the distribution. Vienna ranks 155th and Warsaw 205th.

The city alone tells the same story. Prague's own region ranks 107th of 208. Rebuilt from the Czech Statistical Office's completions of new houses, so that reconstructions the Czech census counts are left out, Prague falls to 93rd as a metro region and 76th as a city. It is still in the middle.

Within Czechia, Prague built the second most of the 14 regions per resident: 3.7 a year, against a median of 2.1 elsewhere.

The rule written in advance would call this "within the range of European peers". But the model's relevance check failed. Regions that grew faster before 2011 did not build more afterwards: the coefficient is negative. By the same rule, the result is labelled uninformative.

What remains is descriptive and still worth stating. On a common definition, Prague's decade of construction is ordinary for Europe. Part 4's comparison was with two of the region's faster builders.

From permit to house number

Every month the Czech Statistical Office counts dwellings permitted and dwellings completed in each region. If Prague's flats take longer to finish, completions follow permits with a longer delay. The model estimates that delay as a distribution of lags between the two series, for flats in new apartment buildings, Prague against the other twelve regions. Středočeský kraj is left out of the comparison, because it absorbs Prague's overflow.

The best-fitting mean lag is 16 months in Prague and 5 months elsewhere: a difference of 11.1 months (90 % interval 8.7 to 12.6, p < 0.001). H2 passes its threshold.

The sign holds in every variant run:

  • +7.6 months against Jihomoravský kraj, home of Brno, alone;
  • +10.3 with Středočeský kraj counted as Prague;
  • +5.0 when family houses are added (p = 0.055).

Three caveats come with it, all visible in fig. 2:

  • Prague's lag is weakly identified. Anything from about 14 to 38 months fits almost as well. The contrast with the rest is clearer than Prague's own number.
  • Five months is too short to build a block of flats. Outside Prague, the model partly tracks months in which permits and completions are both reported, not only construction.
  • Prague's projects are larger, and large projects take longer anywhere.

What the result supports is modest: once permitted, Prague's new flats reach completion later than the rest of Czechia's, by months, not weeks. It says nothing about how long the permit itself took, which is the part of the process Czech reform has targeted9.

Prices and permits

Where supply responds, more dwellings are permitted after prices rise1011. The registered estimate compares Prague's cumulative response over twelve quarters with the other regions', using the cadastre's quarterly price index of flats, 2018–2024.

The rest of Czechia's own response, the relevance check, cannot be told from zero: 16 (90 % interval −22 to +54). So the comparison is uninformative. Prague's relative response is −11 (−86 to +63). A placebo with family-house prices gives +8 (−25 to +42), and a shorter four-quarter version over 35 quarters gives +4 (−2 to +10). Twenty-seven quarters of one city cannot answer this question.

Who builds, in Poland

Part 4 ended on Vienna's limited-profit associations, which built a quarter of Vienna's new homes in 2024. Austria publishes no yearly series by builder that would allow a test. Poland does: in each of its 380 districts, the dwellings that municipalities, social building societies (TBS) and developers begin each year.

The registered question was whether non-market builders respond less to local price growth than developers. The question is about Poland only.

The point estimate goes the expected way: −0.56, with the interval from −1.52 to +0.39, p = 0.17. But the relevance check fails. Developers' own starts do not visibly respond to local price growth either (0.09, p = 0.32), so there is no response for the non-market builders to be smaller than. The result is uninformative, as the design-stage power check had warned (19 % power at the smallest difference of interest).

Nothing here says that a Vienna-style sector would add to Prague's output. Subsidised construction can partly replace what the market would have built1213, and more homes are not the same thing as better housing. Warsaw built the most of the three cities and still had fast price growth.

Where Prague builds

Land near a metro station is worth more, so an unconstrained market would build more densely there. The test counts flats completed between 2012 and census day in March 2021 in each of Prague's 524 one-kilometre squares14, holding equal two things:

  • the distance from the centre;
  • how many people already lived in the square in 201115.

New flats are denser near the metro. Each doubling of the distance to a station goes with 20 % fewer new flats per square kilometre (γ = −0.227, 90 % interval −0.349 to −0.105). The spatial test puts this far from chance (p = 0.001). The wild bootstrap over Prague's 22 administrative districts, which act like fewer than 14 independent clusters, gives p = 0.0252.

The registered decision takes the larger of the two p-values, and Holm's second threshold is 0.025. H5 misses it by 0.0002, less than the bootstrap's own simulation error. It was not re-run. The registered outcome is "not supported", and the registered reading is indeterminate.

Every variant points the same way:

  • half-kilometre squares;
  • 2012–2024;
  • without reconstructions;
  • with distance to rail and tram stops;
  • dropping any one district, which gives −0.27 to −0.18.

In 2000–2011 the gradient was about a quarter as steep and uncertain (−0.06). Registered rules are there to stop a writer from counting a miss like this as a hit, so it stays a miss.

A supported result would not have explained itself either. Squares near stations differ from others in the land left to build on and in what the plan allows there. Those layers could not be obtained in time and were not run.

The European ranks hardly move:

  • without Spain and Croatia, whose censuses also count reconstructions, and with Prague rebuilt: 50th of 183;
  • with a shift-share employment shock in place of population growth: 45th of 116;
  • weighting by population, or with growth over 2011–2021: 137th and 138th.

None comes near the tail. Two pipeline variants, an exponential lag and one without the COVID months, have bootstraps in which a few draws run off to unbounded lags; their tests carry no information and are counted as failures.

What changed after registration

  • Not run, because the inputs could not be obtained before the outcomes were joined:
    • the land and planning layers for the metro question: Urban Atlas, heritage zones, flood plains, the 1999 plan and line D;
    • Polish municipal construction times: the statistics API refused connections;
    • the stock of dwellings under construction: the Prague series stops being published after 2009;
    • two price-index variants.
  • Choices the design left open:
    • census reference dates are taken from national documentation, and several are marked for verification;
    • a metro region's core is its densest member;
    • the Czech rebuilt numerator uses monthly regional completions;
    • twelve quarterly lags leave 27 quarters, not 35;
    • robustness bootstraps use 999 draws.
  • After estimation:
    • H5 was decided at p = 0.0252 against 0.025 and not re-run;
    • the rest of Czechia's 5-month lag and Prague's flat likelihood are flagged;
    • three relevance checks failed, and their results are labelled uninformative;
    • robustness variants for H4 drop district-builder cells with no dwellings at all. The main frame had none.

The full, dated list is in the design file4. The numbers come from six scripts:

  • housing_data.py

What this does not show

  • No cause. The European comparison is a benchmark against peers, not a cause. Its relevance check failed, so even as a benchmark it is labelled uninformative.
  • Not the time to get a permit. The pipeline result starts at the permit.
  • Not an elasticity. Prices respond to supply as well, so the price questions measure responsiveness.
  • Not Vienna. The builders' test is Polish, and it says nothing about what limited-profit associations would do in Prague.
  • Buildings, not flats. The cadastre keeps only standing buildings, and it dates the building rather than the flats added to it.
  • Definitions and resolution. The census dates buildings too, and in Czechia, Spain and Croatia it counts their reconstruction. One-kilometre squares and fourteen regions are coarse units16.
