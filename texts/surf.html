Not worth the flight

I live in Prague and surf in Portugal, so every trip starts with a forecast five or six days out and a decision about a flight. The forecast comes from a model, and models are sometimes right; this page counts how often, by lead day, and turns the count into a rule for when to book.

What the page measures

A forecast can fail a surfer by promising a swell that never comes, so that you fly for nothing, or by missing one that does, so that you stay home, and both failures depend on the lead time, since the evening before the model is nearly reporting, while six days out it is guessing with confidence. The quantity to measure is therefore P(surfable on the day | what the forecast said d days before) for each d. With that probability in hand the flight becomes arithmetic, and you book when the probability, times what a surf day is worth to you, times the days you would get, exceeds what the trip costs1.

What is stored each morning

At 06:10 UTC a GitHub Actions job fetches the seven-day forecast for three spots from three wave models (swell height, period, direction; wave height) together with the wind, aggregates each target day to daylight maxima and means, and commits the file. A day later it fetches the analysis for the previous day, the model's final state, and commits that too. From then on every stored forecast can be paired with what its target day turned out to be, at lead 0 through 623. The whole infrastructure is a folder of small JSON files in a public repository, with no keys, no server and no database, and anyone can re-run the pairing.

The truth against which forecasts are checked is a proxy, and the page says so. The Portuguese buoys (Leixões, Nazaré, Sines) belong to the Instituto Hidrográfico and are released on request rather than through an API, and the European in-situ portal has no Portuguese wave platforms4. "What arrived" is therefore the analysis of the same model family that made the forecast. That is what a surfer actually checks the night before, and it makes the skill numbers read optimistically, so the spread between the three models and the rank correlation are the columns to trust more than the error in metres. If a buoy feed appears, it replaces the proxy without changing the code.

The surfable rule and the summer at Ericeira

In version one of the rule a day is surfable when the daily maximum swell height is between 1.0 and 2.5 m, the mean period is at least 8 s, and the wind is at most 25 km/h or offshore (east ± 60°). Applied to the last 92 days of analysis at Ericeira, the rule clears five days. From mid-June to mid-September the west coast gets wind swell and the nortada, with periods at 5–8 s and an afternoon wind often over 25 km/h, and only eleven of the 92 days had a mean period of 8 s or more. The ground swell that clears the rule easily arrives in autumn, and the page will show it arriving3.

The booking decision as expected value

Let p be the probability the day is surfable given the forecast at lead d, v the value of a surf day, n the number of days you would get if it is on, and c the cost of the trip. You book when the expected value of the trip is positive:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{trip}] = p\,v\,n - c > 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > p^{*} = \frac{c}{v\,n}$$

At 180 EUR for a flight and two nights, 120 EUR a day and two days, the break-even is 75 %. Until the pairs exist, the page uses the base rate from the last 92 days, which is 5 %, as p, so today every row says "not worth the flight". That base rate is the honest prior, and the page exists because the whole value of a forecast lies in how far it moves p away from 5 %1.

How forecast skill is measured

Every stored forecast is paired with the analysis of its target date, giving one pair per spot, model, lead day (0–6) and date, and four kinds of measure are computed on each set of pairs3. The continuous measures are the mean absolute error, bias and RMSE of the daily maximum swell height, the MAE of the mean period, and Spearman's rank correlation, which copes better than the error in metres with the fact that the truth and the forecast share a model family. The binary measures come from the "surfable" contingency table (hits, misses, false alarms, correct negatives): the probability of detection, the false-alarm ratio, and the two numbers the decision needs, P(surfable | forecast said yes) and P(surfable | forecast said no). For the probabilistic measure, the share of the three models that call the day surfable is treated as a forecast probability \(f_i\) and scored with the Brier score against the outcome \(o_i \in \{0,1\}\), and the Brier skill score gives how much better that is than a reference forecast, climatology or persistence. Those two references are also the baselines, with climatology forecasting the base rate and persistence taking the forecast for lead d to be the analysis d days before the target, and a forecast that does not beat persistence at lead 1 is not counted as a forecast5.

$$\mathrm{POD} = \frac{H}{H+M},\qquad \mathrm{FAR} = \frac{F}{H+F},\qquad P(\text{surfable}\mid\text{yes}) = \frac{H}{H+F} = 1-\mathrm{FAR}$$ $$\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f_i - o_i\right)^{2},\qquad \mathrm{BSS}_{\text{ref}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\text{ref}}},\quad \text{ref} \in \{\text{climatology},\ \text{persistence}\}$$

Here \(H\), \(M\) and \(F\) are the hits, misses and false alarms of the contingency table; climatology forecasts the base rate \(\bar o\) every day, and persistence forecasts the analysis of \(d\) days earlier. A BSS of 0 means no better than the reference, 1 is perfect, and a negative value is worse than not forecasting.

Consecutive days are not independent draws, so the intervals on MAE and on P(surfable | yes) come from a block bootstrap by ISO week (500 resamples). No number is shown for a lead with fewer than 14 pairs, and below 30 pairs a number is marked descriptive. For reliability, the page reports for each forecast probability (0, ⅓, ⅔, 1) how often the day was actually surfable, which is the reliability diagram in table form.

Sensitivity to the surfable rule

The binary results are recomputed under five alternative rules: period thresholds of 7 s and 9 s, wind caps of 20 and 30 km/h, and a wider height band of 0.8–3.0 m. On the 92-day climatology alone the sensitivity is already large, with 5 % of days surfable at 8 s, 2 % at 9 s and 19 % at 7 s, 4 % at a 20 km/h wind cap and 5 % at 30, and 11 % with the wider band3. The skill numbers will be shown under every one of these rules, and the conclusion has to hold across them or be reported as rule-dependent. Two further splits are built in. Self-verification (MFWAM against its own analysis) is flagged separately from cross-model verification (GWAM and EWAM against the MFWAM analysis), because the first is the optimistic case, and every metric is computed per spot, since Sagres faces south-west and Ericeira north-west.

The proxy truth remains a limitation that the splits cannot fix: if the MFWAM analysis is itself wrong on a day, forecast and truth are wrong together and the pair looks good. The direction of that bias is known, and only a buoy feed would remove it.

Data engineering

There are two data products, both append-only. One is a file per issue date with the seven-day forecast for three spots and three models, aggregated to daily records with a fixed schema (max and mean swell height, mean period, circular-mean direction, max wave height, mean wind and direction; daylight window 06–20 UTC; units metres, seconds, degrees, km/h). The other is one analysis file keyed by spot and date. Aggregation happens at ingest, so the repository stays small (8 KB a day) and the verifier only ever compares like with like. Raw hourly data is not kept, because the aggregation is deterministic and the API call is reproducible.

The collector overwrites the file for its own issue date and upserts analysis days by key, so a re-run is harmless and a missed run leaves a visible gap rather than a duplicate. A failed API call fails the job without writing anything partial, and the workflow commits only if something changed.

The verifier also reports data quality from what the stored files actually contain: issues stored against issues expected, missing issue dates, the share of null values per model, days a model dropped out, the share of target days on which the models disagree by more than a metre, and gaps in the analysis. On day one it found that EWAM returns no values beyond lead 3, so 43 % of its cells are null and it will only ever be scored at leads 0–3. The page reports this next to EWAM's scores3.

A pytest suite builds a synthetic world, with a fake analysis, 45 issues, error that grows with lead and a planted positive bias, and checks that the verifier reports what was planted: MAE monotone in lead, rank correlation falling, the bias recovered, bootstrap intervals bracketing the point estimate, the 14-pair gate holding, the skill scores beating both baselines, and the quality counts matching the files. The six tests take three seconds and run before the page trusts a number3.

One script builds data.json from the two data products and the rule constants, and the figure on this page reads the same constants, so the figure and the page cannot disagree. The infrastructure is a GitHub Actions cron, a public repository and a static page, and it costs nothing. If a buoy feed appears, it becomes one more collector writing into the analysis file under a new key, and the verifier gains a second truth column.

Expectations recorded on 14 September 2026

Three claims were written down on 14 September 2026, to be checked against the page when the pairs exist.

  1. At lead 1–2 the three models will agree on swell height to within 0.3 m on most days and the rank correlation with the analysis will be above 0.9; the forecast is useful for deciding whether to drive to Ericeira tomorrow.
  2. At lead 5–6 the mean absolute error of daily maximum swell height will exceed 0.5 m and the models will disagree by more than 0.5 m on at least a third of days; the forecast at flight-booking lead is a coin with a bias.
  3. At long leads the models will over-forecast swell height more often than under-forecast it (positive bias), because a forecast swell that fades is more common than one that appears from nothing.

If the third claim is wrong, the rule for booking flips from "wait" to "trust the early call", which would be worth knowing. Results will be added here with the date they were read.
