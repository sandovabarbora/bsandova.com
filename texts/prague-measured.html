Prague, measured

This series asks how one city is governed, how it votes, how it spends, how much it builds and how it prices its streets, and answers each question from the data the city and the state publish about themselves. Every part began as a descriptive article and became a study whose hypotheses, tests and decision rules were reviewed by three referees and committed to a public repository before the outcome data were joined. This page lists the parts with what each found, explains how they were registered, and points to the data and code.

The parts

  1. Part 1 · the City Assembly · roll calls 2010 to September 2026

    Prague's council rarely says no

    The city publishes how each of its 65 councillors voted on every resolution it adopts, and in the current term the opposition club Praha sobě voted on the same side as the coalition club SPOLU 88 % of the time. Across 10 565 roll calls since 2010 the same councillor votes against about half as often in each new term (× 0.49), and ANO voted yes 95 % of the time while in the coalition and 59 % while in opposition, so a list votes according to whether it governs more than according to the party behind it.

    1 of 3 registered tests passed

  2. Part 2 · Chamber elections of 2017, 2021 and 2025 · 1 119 precincts

    Prague votes in rings, not along lines

    ANO won 26 % in precincts made up mostly of panel flats and 16 % where there are almost none, and the distance from Můstek and the share of panel flats explain the pattern of the 2025 vote while the distance to the metro adds almost nothing (−0.17 points per doubling of distance, 90 %: −0.40 to +0.05). Education and citizenship from the 2021 census account for 43 % of ANO's estate gradient, and the gradient steepened from 0.55 to 0.80 points per 10 percentage points of panel flats between 2017 and 2025.

    1 of 6 registered tests passed

  3. Part 3 · city-district budgets 2022–2024 · the city's grants to its districts 2015–2023

    One city, 57 budgets

    The thirteen large districts spend between 8 346 and 17 604 crowns a year per resident, Praha 1 spends 35 320, and for the median district 76 % of income is passed on by the city and the state. The study found no sign that a district receives larger in-year grants when its mayor's party joins the city's coalition (−37 %, 95 %: −115 to +21 %), but the design could only have detected a premium of about 100 %, which is two to three times the premiums published for other countries.

    0 of 2 registered tests passed

  4. Part 4 · new homes in Prague, Vienna and Warsaw · 208 European metropolitan regions

    Prague builds two homes for Vienna's three

    Since 2015 Prague has completed 4.5 new homes a year for every 1 000 residents, against 6.6 in Vienna's new buildings and 10.4 in Warsaw, and it has not passed 5 in any year since 2012. Of the three registered tests only the pipeline test passed: a permitted flat in Prague takes about 11 months longer to receive a house number than one elsewhere in Czechia (90 %: 8.7 to 12.6). The test of new flats against the distance to the metro ended indeterminate, and the comparison with Europe's regions was uninformative because its relevance check failed.

    1 of 3 registered tests passed

  5. Part 6 · paid parking zones and every car in the Czech register · registered analysis plan

    Bigger cars, the same kerb

    On today's paid kerb, 2012's cars would fit about 1.6 % more (1.3–1.9 %), some 2 800 of 168 400 stalls. A new car in Czechia grew 2.3 cm between 2022 and 2025 (95 %: −0.3 to +5.2), less than the 6 cm Transport & Environment projected. A long car's advantage under the flat resident permit is worth about 120 Kč a year, while the implicit subsidy (forgone rent) to parking at all lies between 5 800 and 48 000 Kč a year, depending on the benchmark. The article replaces three shorter texts published in September 2026.

    1 of 2 registered tests passed

How each part was registered

Each part was first published as a short descriptive article. Its extension started from a written design listing the research questions, the hypotheses with their direction, the tests and the decision rule for each, and the sentence that would report each possible result. The design opens with a section on what had already been seen, because a hypothesis written after looking at the outcome is not a test of it, and the earlier article had usually published part of the outcome. Three referees then read each draft design independently, one for the subject, one for the statistics and one for the data, and the second version answers their reports point by point.

The second version was committed to a branch of this site's public repository and pushed before any outcome data were joined, so the commit history dates the design ahead of the results. The branches were then merged into the site in one commit each, which would erase the order of events, and they are kept on GitHub for that reason. Within each part the confirmatory tests share a 5 % family-wise error rate through Holm's procedure, and results are reported in the registered wording, such as supported, not supported, inconclusive, indeterminate or uninformative. Everything decided after registration, including corrections to parsers after the outcome had been seen, is listed with its date under "What changed after registration" at the end of each article.

Data and code

All the sources are public: the City Assembly's roll-call records, the Czech Statistical Office's election results, census grid and housing statistics, the Ministry of Finance's MONITOR budget database, the city's closing accounts, the national vehicle register and the European Environment Agency's CO2 monitoring data, together with Statistik Austria, Statistics Poland and Eurostat. Each design lists the files it read with their SHA-256 hashes. The scripts that download, parse and estimate are in the repository next to the designs, and the numbers behind the figures and tables are published as JSON beside the articles.
