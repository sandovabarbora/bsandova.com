Prague, measured

Five studies of how Prague's City Assembly votes, how its precincts voted in 2025, how the city funds its districts, how much housing it completes and what longer cars take from its paid kerb.

The parts

  1. Part 1 · the City Assembly · roll calls 2010 to September 2026

    Votes against in Prague's City Assembly, 2010–2026

    Across 10 565 roll calls since 2010 the same councillors' rate of voting against fell to 0.49 times its earlier level per term, pooled over 59 people and three transitions (H1 supported), with most of the fall between 2010–14 and 2014–18; the tests that votes split along coalition and opposition better than along left and right (H3) and that the opposition opposes planning and property more than grants (H4) were not supported. ANO, descriptively, voted yes 95 % of the time in the coalition and 59 % in opposition.

  2. Part 2 · Chamber elections of 2017, 2021 and 2025 · 1 119 precincts

    Housing estates and the 2025 vote in Prague's precincts

    ANO won 26 % in precincts made up mostly of panel flats and 16 % where there are almost none, and its gradient across the estates steepened from 0.55 to 0.80 pp per 10 pp of panel flats between 2017 and 2025 (H3 supported). Education and citizenship from the 2021 census account for 43 % of that gradient, short of the registered half (H1b not supported); the metro test was not supported: inconclusive (H2), and both turnout tests were uninformative.

  3. Part 3 · city-district budgets 2022–2024 · the city's grants to its districts 2015–2023

    City grants to Prague's 57 districts and party alignment

    The thirteen large districts spend between 8 346 and 17 604 crowns a year per resident, and for the median district 76 % of income is passed on by the city and the state. The test that a district receives larger in-year grants when its mayor's party joins the city's coalition was not supported and underpowered (−37 %, 95 % CI −115 to +21 %), nor was the test that any premium runs through investment grants, and the design could only have detected a premium of about 100 %.

  4. Part 4 · new homes in Prague, Vienna and Warsaw · 208 European metropolitan regions

    New housing in Prague: permits, completions and the metro

    Since 2015 Prague has completed 4.5 new homes a year for every 1 000 residents, against 6.6 in Vienna's new buildings and 10.4 in Warsaw, while by census count its metropolitan region sits in the upper quarter of 208 European regions for 2011–2021. A permitted Prague flat takes about 11 months longer to receive a house number than one elsewhere in Czechia (H2 supported; bootstrap 90 % interval 8.7 to 12.6), a gap that may partly reflect when completions are registered; the metro test (H5) was indeterminate, and the test on Polish builders and the European and price estimates were uninformative.

  5. Part 5 · paid parking zones and every car in the Czech register

    Longer cars and Prague's paid kerb

    On today's paid kerb, 2012's cars would fit about 1.6 % more (1.3–1.9 % under the registered inputs), some 2 800 of 168 400 administrative stalls, and the flat resident permit is worth about 120 Kč a year more to a long car than to a short one. New cars in Czechia grew 2.3 cm between 2022 and 2025 (95 % CI −0.3 to +5.2), less than the 6 cm Transport & Environment's series implies (H2b supported), and H2a, the check of the register against a wheelbase estimate, was inconclusive (underpowered).

How each part was registered

Each part began as a short descriptive article and was extended from a written design listing the hypotheses with their direction, the test and decision rule for each, and the sentence that would report each possible result. The design opens with what the author had already seen, since the earlier article had usually published part of the outcome. The series has one author, and there was no external peer review.

Parts 1, 3, 4 and 5 each have a registered analysis plan: the design was committed on the author's own branch before the results commit (table below); commit times are self-reported, and the branch was pushed after the analysis. In Part 2 the design and results first appear in the same commit, and its registration reads "design written and reviewed before modelling; not independently timestamped." Future studies will be registered on OSF before any outcome is observed.

Within each part the confirmatory tests share a 5 % family-wise error rate through Holm's procedure, and results are reported in the registered wording. Everything decided after registration, including parser corrections made after the outcome had been seen, is dated under "What changed after registration" in each article.

Data and code

All the sources are public: the City Assembly's roll-call records, the Czech Statistical Office's election results, census grid and housing statistics, the Ministry of Finance's MONITOR budget database, the city's closing accounts, the national vehicle register and the European Environment Agency's CO2 monitoring data, together with Statistik Austria, Statistics Poland and Eurostat. Each design lists the files it read with their SHA-256 hashes, and the numbers behind the figures and tables are published as JSON beside the articles.
