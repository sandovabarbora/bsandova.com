Delay persistence and the value of reliability

Transport appraisal values unreliability through the dispersion of travel times. The scheduling model of Noland and Small prices the cost of arriving early or late when travel time is uncertain2, Bates and co-authors review how that cost and the older mean–variance approach are valued for personal travel3, and Fosgerau and Karlström show that under scheduling preferences the expected cost of unreliability is proportional to the standard deviation of travel time4; Carrion and Levinson review the empirical values that follow5. These measures use the unconditional dispersion, which fits a traveller who knows nothing about the current state of the route, and they implicitly treat a route's delays as independent from one trip to the next. The thesis asks whether within-day delays on Prague's buses and trams are serially correlated and, if they are, how much of their dispersion a traveller who watches a live feed can forecast1. The thesis states two contributions, a correction of the appraisal measure through a smaller conditional variance, where a view of delays as compounding shocks would apply a multiplier, and a value of real-time information derived from the same variance decomposition, without a stated-preference survey. The passenger-benefit studies reviewed by Brakewood and Watkins measure that value mainly through waiting times, ridership and satisfaction6. The decomposition needs only vehicle-location records and a timetable, which any operator running automatic vehicle location already holds.

Data

The thesis uses the vehicle positions of Prague Integrated Transport published by Golemio, Prague's open-data platform, from 15 March to 8 September 2025, 121 197 794 vehicle-location records in all1. Inference rests on buses, with 691 routes, and trams, with 41. The metro has three route clusters, too few for inference, and appears in the thesis only descriptively. The cleaning rules, the number of routes and trips dropped and the access dates of the feed are not reproduced on this page. Five days of the same records, aggregated into five-minute windows, underlie the sonification Delayed All the Time7.

How this was done

Persistence is measured with a second-order autoregression of delay within a day, whose two coefficients are summed, and simulated impulse responses give the time a shock takes to halve. The equation below is schematic. What the time index counts, whether routes are pooled, which route and time-of-day terms enter, and how the bias of dynamic panel estimators with fixed effects is handled are specified in the thesis and not reproduced here, since this page draws only on the thesis's abstract, Chapter 6 (results by hypothesis) and Chapter 8 (conclusion)1.

$$d_t = \phi_1 d_{t-1} + \phi_2 d_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t,\qquad \hat\phi_1+\hat\phi_2 = 0.727\ (\text{bus}),\ 0.715\ (\text{tram})$$

The cost side rests on an exact decomposition of delay variance into a seasonal part, a forecastable state and unforecastable noise. The value of real-time information is defined as the gain from watching the state instead of trusting the timetable, expressed as a share of the commuter-tier reliability cost, and converted into crowns per passenger per year at a thirty-minute horizon. The calibration constants behind the crown figures (value of time, reliability ratio, trips per year) are not listed on this page. Out-of-sample figures come from a split of the sample by date1.

The hypotheses in the thesis proposal

The three hypotheses were stated in the thesis proposal before estimation. For the first hypothesis, the thesis reports that the gap in persistence between bus and tram is a quarter of the smallest effect the design could reliably detect, so the two modes cannot be ordered with this design; the minimum detectable effect itself is not given on this page. Within a route, the effect of incoming delay on persistence has the expected sign. The second hypothesis fails on its sign, as described in the section on real-time information below. For the third, a simulated positive control shows that the design had the power to detect a gradient, but a placebo test failed, which leaves open that education is entangled with the structure of the network, and the null therefore does not establish that no gradient exists1.

Persistence in bus and tram delays

Within-day delays are serially correlated on both modes that carry inference, with AR(2) coefficient sums of 0.727 on bus across 691 routes and 0.715 on tram across 41, and simulated impulse responses cross half their magnitude after about thirty-three minutes. Because the delay level is far more persistent than its increment, the thesis locates the memory in a slowly moving, observable state rather than in accumulating shocks. Interday correlation is far lower, so the persistence is a within-day property1.

Real-time information and the cost of unreliability

Persistence of this kind makes a fraction of the dispersion forecastable and does not compound it, so in the thesis the correction appraisal needs is a smaller conditional variance. A compounding multiplier describes a single decaying shock, whereas the bus and tram series behave as a level plus a short-memory autoregression, and the multiplier is reported only for metro, where three route clusters deny it inference. In the variance decomposition the state dominates on metro and the unforecastable residual on bus and tram1.

The value of real-time information is 5.6 % of the commuter-tier reliability cost on bus and 2.3 % on tram. Because the tiers share their calibration constants, the share does not depend on the value of time or on the reliability ratio, the two parameters that occupy much of the appraisal literature and that are not known for the Czech context. The levels in crowns do depend on them and are reported as a bracket: at a thirty-minute horizon about 204 CZK a year for a bus passenger and about 60 for a tram passenger, and 182 and 40 when held out of sample on the date split. These figures index the dispersion made forecastable; no passenger has been measured to gain them1.

What this does not show

The dispersion priced is schedule adherence, whereas for turn-up-and-go service the passenger cares about headway regularity, and this caveat binds hardest on metro. The persistence coefficients are within-day objects, so none of this describes a commuter accumulating risk across weeks. The sample runs from 15 March to 8 September 2025 and contains no Prague winter, the season a study of delay persistence would most want to see. This page carries no confidence intervals, no count of the clusters behind each interval and no robustness table, because the thesis text they would come from is not yet public; until it is, the figures here are point estimates without stated uncertainty. Whether they hold outside Prague is for replication to establish1.
