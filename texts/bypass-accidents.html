Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • After a bypass opened, the police recorded fewer injury accidents inside the town, with a larger relative fall on the old through road in a check, while the count outside the built-up area went up by about one a year per town and the municipality's total did not change detectably. Every one of these intervals is wide, and the registered label is underpowered: with 35 towns, only a change of about 28 % could have been told from zero.
  • One registered check came out backwards: with damage-only crashes counted too, accidents seemed to rise. The trail led to Prague, a comparison town that dominates the counts and whose recorded minor crashes fell by half. Without Prague, an analysis added after the results and not registered, that check is −2.1 % and the injury result −15.3 % (95 % −52.7 to +7.0 %), still underpowered.
  • The author expected accidents to move onto the bypass. The pattern is consistent with that without supporting it.
  • All of this is association around the opening date in a small, recent set of towns, not a measured effect of the bypass.

When a Czech town gets a bypass, the through traffic leaves its main street for a new road around the edge. The question here is whether the town's streets then see fewer recorded crashes, and whether crashes turn up instead out where the traffic went. This study looked at the 35 towns whose bypass opened between 2020 and 2024 and compared them with 50 towns still waiting for theirs, using every accident the Czech police recorded from 2016 to 20251. The questions, the list of bypasses and the rules for reading the result were fixed before any accident was counted for a place or a year.

In the three years after opening, injury accidents recorded inside the built-up area of the towns changed by −18.8 % relative to the waiting towns, about −1.37 a year per town. On the old through road, the road the bypass took over, a registered check gives −57 %, about −0.9 a year. Outside the built-up area, where the bypass runs, the count moved the other way, by +0.96 a year per town, and for the whole municipality the change is +2.3 %, with no detectable change either way (fig. 1). On the bypass roads themselves the police recorded 0.80 injury accidents per kilometre and year, 57 % of them at junctions. A Norwegian study of 20 bypasses found 19 % fewer injury accidents5, close to the Czech estimate.

Each of these numbers comes with a wide interval. The 95 % interval for the town runs from −53.4 to +3.4 %, so it includes both a fall of half and no change at all, and the registered label is underpowered: with 35 towns, the study could tell only a change of about 28 % from zero. The figures describe how recorded accidents changed around the opening date; they do not measure what the bypass did.

How far to trust it

Three things limit what these numbers can say. The towns are few and recent: the public police files start in 2016, and the author decided before any count was made not to request the older records that would have added bypasses opened since 2008, which by the registered rule made this recent set, stage A, the headline. Towns got their bypass when permits and money were ready, not at random, so the waiting towns are a comparison, not a control. And, as the next sections show, the comparison leans on one city. The registered run also stopped twice before producing an estimate, once for a missing software package and once for a fit outside its error guard; both were fixed, logged and the code frozen again before any estimate was read. Everything marked as added after the results, the Prague tabulation, the re-estimate without Prague, the map and the example town, is reported as such and changes no registered label.

The 35 towns

The towns are spread across the country, from Karviná in the east to Mariánské Lázně in the west (fig. 2). 13 sit on class I roads built by the state road agency and 22 on class II roads built by the regions; several bypasses serve two or three municipalities. Most are small: in half of them the police recorded 2.2 or fewer injury accidents a year inside the built-up area before works began. The map shows these raw counts before construction and after opening for each town. A single town's before and after is not an estimate: it has no comparison and moves with chance from year to year.

One town, picked by a rule: Kámen

One town was picked, to show the raw counts, by a rule set before its counts were read: among the towns on class I roads, the one with the most years after opening in the data, with ties broken by the earliest opening date. That is Kámen on road I/19, whose bypass (BYP-014) opened on 21 February 2020 after works began in 2018, the only class I bypass opened in 2020. Inside its built-up area the police recorded no injury accident in any year from 2016 to 2025, so the town adds nothing to Q1 and shows that some of these towns are villages. In the whole municipality, crossed by road I/19, the police recorded 7, 3, 5, 3 in 2016–2019, before and during construction, then 0, 1, 2, 2, 1 injury accidents in the 5 full years after opening; on the bypass road, which need not lie wholly inside the municipality, they recorded 7 in those years. One town is not evidence of anything, and Kámen is here because of the rule, not because of its result.

The check that came out backwards

Thirteen registered checks repeat the analysis with one thing changed, and most land near the headline or below it (fig. 4). Check 1 did not. Counting every recorded accident, damage-only crashes included, it gave +50 % inside the towns and +66 % for the municipalities, as if crashes had risen sharply where injury crashes fell. A registered check cannot change a label, but a reversal this large needed explaining, so the counts behind it were tabulated by place and year after the results, without estimating anything (tools/bypass/describe.py).

The police field descriptions showed no change in how severity is coded over the decade1, and the treated towns' own damage-only counts had changed by +13 %, close to the other comparison towns' +9 %. The difference lay in one comparison town. Prague enters the comparison group because the bypass of Úvaly on road I/12 (BYP-057), whose works began in 2025, reaches into its territory, and the design counts every municipality a bypass serves. Prague then holds 93 % of the comparison towns' damage-only accidents inside built-up areas and 83 % of their injury accidents, and in a Poisson model the yearly baseline follows the counts. Between 2016–2019 and 2023–2025 Prague's recorded damage-only accidents fell by half (−51 %) while its injury accidents rose (+7 %) (fig. 3). Measured against Prague's fall, any town whose minor crashes held steady looks like a rise.

Prague's fall sits mostly in the smallest crashes, those with recorded damage up to 100 000 CZK (−68 % from 2016 to 2025), while those above it held steady in nominal crowns. Below that limit the police need not be called to a crash without injury6, so whether such a crash is recorded depends on the drivers. That pattern fits fewer minor crashes being reported or recorded in Prague better than fewer crashes, though the records cannot tell the two apart and nothing read for this study names a change in Prague's practice.

Setting Prague aside tests this reading. At the author's request, after the results and not registered, Q1, Q2 and check 1 were estimated again by the frozen code with only Prague's row removed from the comparison towns; Úvaly stays (tools/bypass/without_prague.py). Check 1 fell from +50 % to −2.1 % inside the towns and from +66 % to +10.9 % for the municipalities. The injury result moved much less, from −18.8 % to −15.3 % (95 % −52.7 to +7.0 %), and the registered rule would still call it underpowered; Q2 became +6.5 %. The reversed sign of check 1 therefore depends on Prague being in the comparison group, while the injury result depends on it much less. None of this replaces a registered label.

The other checks

The remaining checks mostly point the same way as the headline, and some sit further from zero: killed or seriously injured −56 %, the old through road −57 %, class I roads −29 % against class II −3 %, and smaller towns −53 %. They are checks: none changes the registered label, all keep Prague, and the first rests on 411 of 2 000 draws because serious crashes are rare. No check has a 95 % interval wholly above zero.

All registered checks, with fig. 4 and the table

Fifteen checks were registered and thirteen apply to stage A (fig. 4). The check on killed or seriously injured gives −56 % (95 % −72 to −12 %), but in most draws a cell had no such accident, so the interval rests on 411 of 2 000 draws and is probably cut short at its negative end; for the municipality it gives −4 % on 720 draws. The old through road gives −57 % (95 % −85 to −21 %). The 13 class I towns give −29 % (95 % −74 to −10 %) and the 22 class II towns −3 % (95 % −49 to +36 %); both share Q1's sign, and the two lists differ in how complete they are.

Town-specific trends move the estimate to −36 %, −17 points from Q1, so the treated towns' fitted trends pointed upwards relative to the common year effects, though the lead −3 coefficient (+11 %) points the other way and each slope rests on two to seven years. Smaller towns give −53 % and larger ones −16 %; the smaller-town interval needed 1 989 redraws and the split invites regression to the mean. Dropping 2020 and 2021, the opening year as a dose, planned comparison towns only, class III added or post years 1–5 all stay between −14 % and −20 %. Callaway and Sant'Anna7, on counts as a share of the treated towns' mean before construction, gives −39 %, a scale on which a change below −100 % is possible.

The author's expectation

On 15 October 2026, before she had looked at any accident data and after she had been told of the Norwegian result, the author wrote that she expected the accidents to move onto the bypass (in Czech: „předpokládala bych, že se přesunou na obchvat“). The model gives −1.37 injury accidents a year inside the town and +0.96 outside it, per town, and the bypass roads carry 0.80 per kilometre and year. Neither the inside nor the outside change is clear of zero, Q2 as a whole is +2.3 % (95 % −14.4 to +28.1 %), the offset share was not reported by rule, and Q3 has no comparison, so the data are consistent with the expectation without supporting it.

What this means

For someone deciding about a bypass, the measured quantity is the change in police-recorded injury accidents inside a Czech town in the first three years after opening, relative to towns still waiting: −18.8 %, with a 95 % interval from −53.4 to +3.4 %. That range includes the Norwegian fifth and includes no change, and the comparison behind it leans on Prague; without Prague, in an unregistered re-estimate, the change is −15.3 % (95 % −52.7 to +7.0 %), so the conclusion does not turn on it. The study cannot say how much of any fall the bypass itself accounts for, and it says little about the bypass road, where the rate is descriptive. A larger set of towns, such as stage B with the older records, would be needed to tell a change of a fifth from none.

What this does not show

It does not show an effect of bypasses. Towns got their bypass when its permits and money were ready, not at random, and the opening came with other changes: new junctions, a downgraded old road, signs that move the edge of the built-up area. Police recording can change between places as well as over time, as Prague shows. Accidents in neighbouring municipalities are not counted in Q2, and 14 of the 31 bypasses have no mapped line for Q3.

Method, details and reproduction

Method: data, model, labels and what was seen before registration

Data

The accidents are the record-level files of the Police of the Czech Republic for 2016 to 2025, one row per accident, with date, severity, the built-up-area flag (p5a), road number and S-JTSK coordinates1. An injury accident is one in which at least one person was killed, seriously or slightly injured; damage-only accidents enter only check 1. Each accident is placed in a municipality by the RÚIAN polygons2. Inside the built-up area means the record's own flag, as recorded in the year of the accident.

The bypasses come from a list built by an AI agent from construction facts only, without any accident or traffic count: what was built, where, when works started and when the road opened4. Only full bypasses of class I and II roads count, that is roads that pass the built-up area entirely on one side and take the through traffic. A treated town needs two calendar years in the police files before its works started, so stage A holds the towns whose works started in 2018 or later. After the exclusions written into the design (missing dates, other road works in the town, a second bypass, a boundary change of more than 2 %), 35 towns on 31 bypasses remain, 13 on class I roads and 22 on class II; they opened in 2020 (6), 2021 (7), 2022 (5), 2023 (10), 2024 (7). The comparison group has 50 towns on 46 bypasses that opened in 2025 or later or are planned at a dated stage, each serving only until its works start; treated towns also serve before their own works.

How this was done

The model is a Poisson regression of each town's yearly count of injury accidents on town and year effects, with separate indicators for each cohort of opening years (bins 2008–10, 2011–13, 2014–16, 2017–19, 2020–22, 2023–24) in each construction year and each year after opening, the extended two-way fixed-effects estimator for staggered openings8, fitted in Python with statsmodels9. The baseline is the town's years before construction started; construction years and the opening year are kept apart from it. The Q1 estimate is the exponent of the average of the coefficients for post years 1 to 3, weighted by the treated town-years in each cell, minus one: a ratio of mean counts, not an average of towns' own changes.

Intervals come from 2 000 bootstrap draws of whole bypasses with all their towns and years, treated and comparison towns resampled separately, seed 20261015. A draw in which the Poisson fit of Q1 or Q2 failed or had a cell without accidents was redrawn (2 times); a check whose own fit failed in an accepted draw is left missing for that draw. Each question takes the first registered label that applies: fewer or more accidents after opening if the 95 % interval excludes zero and the estimate is at least 10 % from it; no change larger than 15 % if the 90 % interval lies within ±15 %; underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than 30 points; inconclusive otherwise. Under stage A the first two labels would carry the words “with differing trends before construction” and the third could not be given, because the pre-trend test is uninformative by rule.

The registered run stopped twice before producing an estimate; both causes were fixed after the code freeze and logged on 26 October 2026: the run command lacked two geodata packages that the town step imports, and the town-trend fit of check 9 failed inside one bootstrap draw outside the guard the main fit already had. The fit now sits inside that guard and the code was frozen again; the run restarted from draw 1 with the same seed, so the accepted draws are the same as they would have been.

The design discloses what was seen before registration (§0). The author had not looked at accident data around any bypass. A feasibility check opened the police files of 2018, 2022 and 2024 for their national row counts, field descriptions and coordinate coverage only, and reported the Norwegian study, which the author heard of before stating her expectation. While the list was built, one agent briefly displayed six rows of a regional prioritisation table whose columns include accident scores, and another opened a 2009 study ranking candidate bypasses and stopped at its safety criteria; neither was used. The treated window was widened and class III kept out after counts of the list by class and year were seen, which are construction facts. A methods reviewer read the draft design, without accident data.

Stage A: why it is the headline and what it covers

The design registered two data stages: stage A on the public police files from 2016, and stage B adding the accident records of 2006–2015 held by CDV, the transport research centre, which would have brought in bypasses whose works started from 2008. The rule was that stage B would be the headline if its records arrived by 31 March 2027. On 16 October 2026, before any accident count was computed, the author decided not to request them, which by the same rule fixed stage A as the headline; any analysis of the older records would be reported as added after registration.

All 35 towns opened their bypass between 2020 and 2024, in two cohorts, so the post years fall in 2021–2025: 35 towns contribute a first year, 28 a second and 18 a third. Each town has two to seven years before construction. Leads −3 and −2 exist for 30 and 32 towns (32 with either); their coefficients are +11 % and −1 % and the Wald test gives p = 0.43, but under stage A the design declared the test uninformative whatever it showed, as passing it would not show parallel trends. The placebo, which moves each town's construction start two years earlier and uses only years before construction, gives −1.9 % (95 % −13.7 to +11.4 %, cluster-robust by bypass, 30 towns).

Q1: injury accidents inside the town

Relative to towns whose works had not started, injury accidents inside the built-up area of the treated towns changed by −18.8 % in post years 1 to 3 (90 % interval −44.2 to −0.03 %, 95 % −53.4 to +3.4 %), or −1.37 accidents per town and year (95 % −3.07 to +0.05). The 95 % interval includes zero, so the estimate does not meet the rule for fewer accidents, and the 90 % interval is 44 points wide, more than the 30 points the rule allows, so the label is underpowered. The design named underpowered as the likely label with fewer than about 40 treated towns.

Q2: the whole municipality

On the whole territory of the municipality, which holds most of each bypass, injury accidents changed by +2.3 % (90 % −11.6 to +23.5 %, 95 % −14.4 to +28.1 %), again underpowered, with a 90 % interval 35 points wide. In accidents per town and year the model splits this into −1.37 inside the built-up area and +0.96 outside it (95 % −0.07 to +2.13), −0.41 in all (95 % −2.29 to +1.47). The offset share, the part of the inside change matched by the outside change, was registered to be reported only if Q1's 95 % interval excluded zero; it does not, so the share is not reported. Neighbouring municipalities are not counted.

Q3: accidents on the bypass roads

Q3 is descriptive and carries no label: a bypass is a different road, with a different length and traffic, so its rate cannot be compared with Q1. An accident counts on the bypass if it lies within 50 m of the mapped line, outside the built-up area and on the bypass's road number. For the 17 bypasses with OpenStreetMap ways3, the police recorded 118 injury accidents over 148 kilometre-years after opening, 0.80 per kilometre and year, ranging from 0.00 to 1.35 by bypass; 67 of them (57 %) were at a junction or in its zone. The other 14 bypasses, all on class II roads, have no mapped line in the list and are left out, so Q3 rests mostly on class I roads. Before construction, the old through road inside the town had 2.1 injury accidents per town and year (293 accidents in 138 town-years), a rate per town, not per kilometre, as the old road's length is not in the list.

Prague and the damage-only counts in detail

In single years, Prague's records with a recorded damage of up to 100 000 CZK went from 17 697 in 2016 to 10 293 in 2022 and 5 618 in 2025, while those above 100 000 CZK were 3 327 and 3 316; outside Prague the small ones changed by −11 % and the large ones by +75 %. The bands are nominal, and rising prices move accidents into the top band everywhere. The limit for calling the police rose to 200 000 CZK on 1 July 2025, after Prague's fall had begun in 2020. Damage-only collisions with game fell nationally from 13 857 in 2022 to 2 433 in 2025, mostly outside built-up areas, which bears on check 1 for the municipality rather than inside the town.

Inside the treated towns, damage-only accidents on the old through road went from 3.5 per town and year before construction to 1.3 in post years 1 to 3, in step with injury accidents (6.6 to 5.1 on all roads).

Reproduction and change log

Every number on this page is filled in by a script from results.json, the screening file and describe.json. From the repository root, with the raw files in tools/data/bypass/:

  1. tools/bypass/fetch.py, harmonise.py and screen.py: the police, RÚIAN and OpenStreetMap files, one schema for all years, and the screening.
  2. estimate.py --stage A --headline-fixed: the registered estimates, checks and Q3, with the town rules in towns.py, Q3 in q3.py and tests on made-up data in test_bypass.py.
  3. describe.py: the counts behind the note on damage-only accidents, after the results.
  4. without_prague.py and towns_map.py: the re-estimate without Prague, the map and the example town, after the results.
  5. article.py: the figures and this page.
