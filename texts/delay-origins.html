Where do Prague's trams fall behind?

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Every time a tram goes from one stop to the next, its delay changes. Over 21.4 million such passes in 2025, the 62 of Prague's 618 tram segments with the largest totals recorded 50.6 % of all delay gained (50.3 to 50.9 %). That is just over the half set in advance, so concentration is supported, narrowly.
  • They are the same segments from week to week: 95 % of the top tenth in odd weeks is also the top tenth in even weeks. With tens of thousands of passes per segment, that stability is close to guaranteed, so it says the pattern is structural, not why.
  • A measured gain is not always lateness. A tram that reaches a stop early and waits for its departure time gains delay without being late. On the top tenth, about 24 % of the recorded gain is early trams coming back to time; counting lateness alone, the top tenth still record 52.0 %.
  • The largest are Václavské náměstí → Jindřišská, Hlavní nádraží → Viktoria Žižkov and Anděl → Arbesovo náměstí. 16 of the 62 largest leave one of six large stops picked after seeing the list; dwell, boarding and holding to the timetable all fit, and none of them was measured. These descriptions came after the results and were not registered.
  • City buses show the same concentration: a tenth of 1 286 segments record 51.2 % of their delay.

A tram that reaches the end of its line five minutes late did not lose those five minutes in one place. A few seconds slipped away at one stop, some came back on an open stretch, and the people waiting at the far end only see the sum.

The lost time does not vanish at the end of the line either. The author's thesis found that a sudden delay is still about half there some half an hour later1. So it is worth asking where along the line the delay is recorded in the first place.

Each time a tram goes from one stop to the next (a pass), its delay changes by some number of seconds. The change is positive if the tram falls further behind and negative if it catches up. Adding up those changes over every pass of the season gives each stretch of track between two stops (a segment) a total. That total is the delay recorded there, or made up.

The question

Two questions were written down before any segment's delay was read.

The first asks whether delay is concentrated in a few places. The test is whether the tenth of segments with the largest totals record more than half of all the delay gained anywhere. Segments where trams catch up are left out of that sum, so the share is of the delay gained before anything made up is taken off.

The second asks whether those places stay the same. The segments are ranked by the delay per pass separately in odd and in even weeks. The rank correlation, a score of how closely the two orders agree, should be at least 0.70.

Both answers are read from a bootstrap, which redoes the sums many times on whole days drawn at random from the season, and so also redraws which segments count as the top tenth. A question is "supported" if its 95 % interval lies wholly above its line, "not supported" if wholly below, and "inconclusive" otherwise.

A segment here is one direction between two named stops, so the trip from Anděl to Arbesovo náměstí and the one back are two segments. The delay change on a pass is the delay at the next stop minus the delay at this stop. So it includes the time the tram stands at this stop (the dwell) as well as the run to the next.

Data

The passes are those of the author's thesis, Prague Integrated Transport's stop-time history from Golemio2, 15 March to 8 September 20251, with the screening of the earlier studies.

Some passes are dropped before anything is added up. Planned closures remove 2–31 % of tram passes a month, and passes without a delay change another 2–3 %. Passes whose delay changed by more than ten minutes on one segment are left out as diversions or faults. The 48 segments with fewer than 1 000 passes (9 116 passes in all) are treated as one-off routings and left out too.

That leaves 21.4 million tram passes over 618 segments on 170 days.

One column in the thesis data did not hold what its name said. It was meant to be the delay gained on a segment. On a sample of 42 407 passes it turned out to be the running time against the timetable, which leaves out the time at the stop, and it differed from the change in delay in 99.9 % of them.

The design had a rule for this case, fixed in advance. The change in delay is recomputed as the delay at the next stop minus the delay at this one, and running time alone is reported as a check.

Half of the delay on a tenth of the network

Over the season, trams lost time on some stretches and won it back on others. They gained 71 201 hours of delay on 347 segments and made up 56 244 hours on 271 others, 43.9 % of the segments.

The net is 14 957 hours. The split between gained and made up depends on booking the time at a stop to the segment leaving it, so the net is the firmer number.

Half of the delay gained was recorded on a tenth of the network, only just past the bar set in advance. The 62 segments with the largest totals recorded 50.6 % of it (95 % CI 50.3 to 50.9 %). The whole interval lies above one half, so the concentration is supported, but only narrowly.

One half was a round number fixed in advance, and 50.6 % clears it by less than a percentage point. Over all segments, the concentration curve has a Gini coefficient of 0.73 (fig. 4), a score that is higher the more unevenly the delay is spread.

The largest single total is Václavské náměstí → Jindřišská, with 1 971 hours over the season, +74 seconds on an average pass. Next come Hlavní nádraží → Viktoria Žižkov (1 385 h), Anděl → Arbesovo náměstí (1 249 h) and Kobylisy → Ke Stírce (1 074 h) (figs. 1 and 3).

Busy segments are bound to collect more, since a segment with more trams has more passes to add up. A comparison computed after the results, and not registered, gives a sense of how large that part is.

The tenth of segments with the most passes carry 20.4 % of all tram passes. If every segment added the same delay per pass, the top tenth would record about 20.4 % of the delay. They record 50.6 %.

The largest totals are not simply the busiest segments either. They carry 15.3 % of the passes, and 40 % of them are also among the busiest tenth. Road traffic was not measured.

The same places in odd and even weeks

The ranking barely moves from one week to the next. Ranked by the delay per pass in odd weeks and again in even weeks, the segments come out in nearly the same order. The rank correlation is 0.999, and its 95 % interval, 0.996 to 0.999, lies above 0.70, so stability is supported.

That bar was easy to clear. With tens of thousands of passes on each segment, a segment's average barely moves from week to week, so a near-perfect rank correlation is close to guaranteed by how the test is built. The recorded pattern is a stable feature of the network, and the correlation carries no information about why.

The overlap of the top tenth itself is the more useful figure. Of the 62 segments with the largest totals in odd weeks, 95 % are also among them in even weeks, and 98 % when ranked per pass.

One quirk does not matter for the result. The point estimate of the correlation sits just above its own bootstrap interval, for trams (0.9994 against an upper bound of 0.9990) and for buses (0.9984 against 0.9976). Resampling whole days draws some days twice and spreads them unevenly between odd and even weeks, which pulls the correlation down a little, and the label does not depend on it.

Rush hours and quiet middays pick out much the same places. Weekday peaks and weekday middays share 92 % of their top tenth by delay per pass (a Jaccard index of 0.85, a score of how much two lists overlap). On the top segments the delay per pass is about the same at both, 43 seconds at peaks and 42 at middays.

So the places where delay is recorded stay much the same from week to week, and from the rush hours to the quieter middle of the day.

Early trams waiting for their time

Some of the delay gained on a segment is not lateness at all. A tram that reaches a stop a minute early and waits there for its scheduled departure goes from −60 to 0 seconds of delay. It gains a minute without ever being late.

This was not part of the design. It was computed after the results, once the large-stop pattern below made it the leading explanation.

Early starts are not rare. Of all tram passes, 26.6 % start a segment early. On the 62 segments with the largest totals, 43 % of the recorded gain comes from passes that started early.

Only part of that is trams waiting for their time. On those top segments, 24 % of the recorded gain is early trams coming back to time rather than lateness made, the lateness being the part of the delay above zero. On Anděl → Arbesovo náměstí, it is 31 % (fig. 5).

Counting lateness alone, the network's net is 13 974 hours against 14 957 for the delay change. The top tenth record 52.0 % of the lateness made, so the concentration does not depend on early trams.

Arriving at and leaving six large stops

Many of the largest totals are on the way out of a few large stops. Of the 62, 16 leave Václavské náměstí, Anděl, Hlavní nádraží, Kobylisy, Karlovo náměstí or Palmovka, six stops picked after seeing the list.

Leaving each of them costs time. At four of them, Anděl, Hlavní nádraží, Václavské náměstí and Palmovka, trams make time up on the way in. At Kobylisy and Karlovo náměstí they arrive about even.

From a seat on board, the tram wins back time rolling into Anděl and loses more pulling away. On an average pass, weighted over the segments, a tram's delay changes by −35 seconds arriving at Anděl and by +55 leaving it. At Hlavní nádraží the two are −33 and +44. On Václavské náměstí the segment from Vodičkova changes it by −56 seconds, and the next one, on to Jindřišská, by +74 (fig. 6).

Four explanations fit what was recorded, and nothing measured here can tell them apart.

The first is early trams holding. A tram that arrives ahead of its time at a timing stop, where an early tram is meant to wait, stands there, and the wait is booked to the segment leaving the stop, as the previous section measured.

The second is the timetable's slack. Schedules give the run into a large stop some time in hand, so a late tram can arrive closer to time.

The third is dwell and boarding. The delay on a segment includes the time spent at its first stop, so a tram that stands long while people board shows it on the segment leaving the stop. Dwell was not measured here, and how long boarding takes is the subject of dwell-time models for buses7.

The fourth is tram volume, which the comparison above shows explains only part of the concentration. How timetables spread slack, and how late vehicles pass delay on, is an old question for railways45 and buses6.

Checks

The share recorded on the top segments holds across the checks registered in advance (fig. 7). It is 34 % for the top 5 % of segments and 73 % for the top 20 %. Ranked by delay per pass instead of by total, the top tenth record 42 %.

Leaving out the first and last segment of each trip gives 51 %, and keeping only passes within five minutes gives 51 %.

Month by month the share runs from 49 to 53 %. In March it is 49.0 %, below one half. In September, with 7 days, it is 50.0 %.

The running-time check turns out to be degenerate, too lopsided to say anything. Running time is the time a tram takes between stops against the timetable, leaving out the stop itself. Of the 521 segments with a running time, trams run faster than the timetable on 87 %, and over the season they ran 87 202 hours faster than it in all.

Only 67 segments lose time while moving, so the top tenth record practically all of that (100 %). The 97 segments without a running time are left out of it.

City buses show the same concentration. They were analysed alongside, with their own labels. On routes 100 to 299, the top tenth of 1 286 segments record 51.2 % of their delay (50.7 to 51.8 %, supported).

The bus ranking is as stable as for trams (0.998, supported). Bus peaks and middays share less of their top tenth (a Jaccard index of 0.52).

What this means

On Prague's trams in 2025, delay was recorded in the same places again and again. Half of it fell on a tenth of the segments, which cleared the bar set in advance only narrowly, and on the same ones from week to week.

Many of the largest totals are segments leaving large stops. That pattern was described after the results, and it is consistent with dwell, boarding and trams holding to the timetable.

About a quarter of what the largest record is early trams coming back to time, a gain in delay with nobody running late.

Which of these causes the delay, and how much, is not measured here.

What this does not show

A segment where delay is recorded is not always where it is caused. A tram held at a junction or a stop shows the delay on the segment where it is booked. The timetable also decides where a late tram can catch up.

The split into lateness and early trams, the comparison with volume and the pattern at the large stops are descriptions found after the registered questions were answered. They are not tests.

The delay is per vehicle, not per passenger, so a minute lost on a full tram counts the same as on an empty one.

Dwell and road traffic were not measured. The season is one spring and summer, and winter, the metro and other years are not covered.
