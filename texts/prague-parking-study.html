Prague, measured · Part 6, the long version · registered analysis plan · paid parking zones and every car in the Czech register

Bigger cars, the same kerb

Three short texts asked how much of Prague's paid parking bigger cars have taken, and what the flat resident permit does about it. This version merges them into one study. It measures every car in the national register, reads the zones' own geometry, and writes down its tests and estimates, and the sentences that report them, before the car lengths were read. Two tests were registered and one passed. The size effect is real and small, and the permit's size subsidy is small too. The subsidy to parking at all is not.

Cars are getting bigger, and on a street where they park end to end, a longer car leaves less room for the next one4. Text 01 put a number on this for Prague's paid zones: 2 745 stalls. Text 02 measured the Czech market through its wheelbases and got 2 263. Text 03 set that against the growth in the number of cars and priced it against the flat resident permit. Each borrowed at least one number it could not check. The parallel share came from Berlin, the car lengths after 2022 were extrapolated, and the age of the fleet in 2012 was extrapolated too.

Parking economics has long argued that the kerb is underpriced land and that its price, not its supply, is the lever111213. The size of cars is a newer strand of the argument4. This text asks the size question again with the numbers measured rather than borrowed, and asks how it compares with the price question.

How this was done

The analysis plan was written, reviewed by three referees (transport economics, statistics, a data audit), revised and committed to the site's public repository before any car length from the register was read8. The code was then frozen and run once on scrambled lengths, and only after that on the real ones. The plan fixes:

  • two tests, with a 5 % family-wise error rate shared through Holm's procedure16;
  • three estimates that are reported, not tested, with the sentence for each possible result written in advance;
  • the inputs and their ranges.

The size of the cars comes from the Ministry of Transport's register, which lists every vehicle ever registered in Czechia with its overall length2. The export read here has 19.4 million rows. Passenger cars and their off-road variant (M1 and M1G) number 13.4 million, of which 6.9 million are in operation. Length is recorded for at least 99.6 % of cars made since 2012, but for fewer than half of those made before 1990.

The kerb comes from the city's zone layer: 16 874 segments and 168 424 stalls on 28 September 2026. These are "administrative stalls", a count the city assigns to each segment1. Each segment's band width, solved from its area and perimeter, says whether cars park along it or across it. Checked against 1 706 segments that the city had labelled in 2019, the rule classifies 97 % of stalls correctly. Applied to today's layer, it puts 59 % of stalls on parallel kerb, not the 68 % borrowed from Berlin in the earlier texts.

p-values: studentised cluster bootstrap over 796 model lines, 9 999 draws. Holm thresholds 0.025 and 0.05; only H2b passes. The ±4.4 cm margin is the change in new-car growth that would move the kerb estimate by a quarter of a percentage point, fixed from constants before the data were read.

New cars, measured

For each year the register gives the cars that were new to Czechia that year. They are weighted to the EEA's registration counts model by model, so cars that have since been scrapped or exported count as they did when they were sold3. Measured this way, the average new car grew by 12.7 cm per decade over 2012–2022.

Text 02 had to go through the wheelbase, the only dimension the EEA publishes. The plan re-ran that route with the off-road variant included, which Text 02 had dropped, and converted wheelbase to length with the register's own ratio within each model line: 1.47 mm of length per mm of wheelbase. That route gives 10.4 cm. The two differ by 2.3 cm, and the test cannot say whether that is agreement: the 90 % interval runs from −4.3 to +9.2 cm, far wider than the margin. This was predicted. The plan's power check, run before any length was read, put the chance of showing agreement at 16 %.

The years Text 02 could not see are the result that holds. From 2022 to 2025 new cars grew by 2.3 cm (95 % interval −0.3 to +5.2), not the 6.0 cm that T&E's European series implies4. The test against 6.0 passes (p = 0.007). Whether the Czech market kept Text 02's pace of about 3 cm, the plan's other question, the data cannot say: the interval covers both 3 and the midpoint of 4.5. T&E's figures are rounded to the centimetre, which moves a three-year difference by up to about 0.7 cm.

Re-running Text 02's own pipeline with the off-road variant included changes its numbers. The wheelbase grew 80 mm over the decade, not 68, and Text 02's estimate rises from 2 263 stalls to about 2 700, with a band of 2 400–3 300. A correction note on Text 02 says so.

What the kerb lost

Car lengths enter the parked fleet slowly: the average car in the Czech fleet is almost seventeen years old24. The plan models the fleet from the register itself: every car entering in each year, at the age it entered (new, or imported used), survives to 2012 and to 2025 on a curve calibrated to the fleet's published mean age. The average car in the fleet grew from 4.23 m to 4.37 m. On a parallel kerb, where capacity is kerb length divided by car length plus gap, that is:

The whole interval sits in one of the bands fixed in advance, and the plan's sentence for it reads: on today's kerb, 2012's cars would fit between one and two and a half more cars in every hundred: real, and small next to the count of cars. The second half of that sentence was written for a comparison this text could not yet make. The count of permits by year has been requested from TSK (below).

The earlier texts land in the same place for different reasons. Text 01 had a longer fleet growth and a larger parallel share; this study has a longer fleet growth measured and a smaller share measured. Per parallel space, 2.7 % over thirteen years is far below T&E's headline pro-rated to the same span (7.4–12.1 %)4. It is close to what T&E's own parameters imply (2.9–3.4 %), a figure Text 01 could reproduce where the headline could not be.

Where bays are painted, a longer car does not cost a bay unless it no longer fits. The share of cars longer than 5.25 m, half a metre short of a painted parallel bay, rose from 0.3 % to 1.1 %. Which of Prague's bays are painted is not in the open data. The estimate above therefore treats every parallel bay as unmarked, which makes it an upper bound, and the painted scenarios show how far it would fall.

What moves the answer

The plan asked, for every input, how far it would have to move to change the band. No single input can within its registered range. The one that could, the parallel share, would have to be 93 % or more, against the 58–60 % measured.

Almost all of the uncertainty (a first-order share of 0.92 in a variance decomposition18) comes from one thing: how long the cars made before 2010 really were. The register records them only as survivors. Many of the rest were scrapped before the register's history begins, and the plan brackets that ignorance with a symmetric allowance of up to ±10 cm for the oldest cars. The survival curve itself is pinned down well. Fitted to the fleet's published mean age, it reproduces the published fleet counts for 2016–2019 to within 5 % only for one narrow range of shapes (within 3 % at the shape used), and that range was fixed before any length was read.

One check the plan wrote down failed. The modelled fleet of 2025 is 3.9 cm shorter than the fleet the register shows in operation. The registered remedy, choosing the curve's shape to close the gap, could not close it, because the gap is smallest at the edge of the allowed range. A shortfall common to 2012 and 2025 would hardly move the loss. One that differs between the years would, and nothing here can rule that out.

The price

A resident's first car pays 1 200 Kč a year in the main areas, whatever its length9. In the fleet on the road, a short car (the tenth percentile, 3.96 m) therefore pays about 250 Kč per metre of kerb a year and a long one (the ninetieth, 4.84 m) about 210.

  • The size component. A permit priced by length, with today's average kept, would run from about 1 100 Kč for the short car to about 1 300 for the long one: about 100 Kč a year apart. It would change the price by more than a tenth for 2 % of cars.
  • The level. The plan brackets the subsidy to parking at all, the rent forgone on 13.5 m² of zone, and never states it as one number.
    • At the city's own price for a second car, 7 000 Kč, the permit is 5 800 Kč under it.
    • At the city's fee for reserving a permanent bay, 10 Kč per m² a day10, a fee for exclusive round-the-clock use and so an upper end, it is 48 000 Kč under it.
    • The two middle points the plan named, land value at a 4 % return and garage rents, had no dated source fixed in time and are not shown.

At every price level the gap between a long and a short car is about a tenth of the price of the space itself. The flat permit's size subsidy is about 100 Kč a year between a short and a long car, and the implicit subsidy (forgone rent) to parking at all is between 5 800 and 48 000 Kč. Pricing by length corrects the price, and changes little unless the price itself rises.

Count against size

Not run: awaiting TSK data. The plan's third estimate compares, in the districts whose zones have not grown since 2012 (Prague 1, 2, 3 and 7), how much more kerb the growing number of cars claims with how much more their length claims. It needs the number of valid permits at each year's end, and no such series is public. The only public figure is 215 108 valid permits on 1 May 202625. The request for the series is for the author to send, and the estimate will be added when the data arrive.

What changed after registration

  • A coding error. The painted-bay share counted cars with no recorded length as long. It was found in the first real output, because it gave an impossible negative loss, and fixed. The headline estimates did not change beyond the fourth decimal.
  • The fleet calibration check failed by 3.9 cm, and its registered remedy could not close the gap (above).
  • Painted bays were not measured, because neither the orthophoto sample nor TSK's inventory exists yet. The estimate treats all parallel bays as unmarked and shows two painted scenarios.
  • Two points of the price bracket were not computed, because no dated source was fixed. The comparison with other cities stays in their own currencies.
  • The blind dry run scrambled lengths across years within each make, as registered. Scrambling within each year would have kept, and so revealed, the trend being tested.
  • Text 02's numbers change, because the off-road variant it dropped is included (above).

What this does not show

These are counterfactuals on today's kerb, not a history of how many stalls Prague had. The register describes the cars registered in Czechia, not the ones parked in Prague's zones, whose mix nobody publishes. The 2012 fleet is modelled, and the lengths of its oldest cars are an assumption bracketed on both sides, not a measurement. Stalls are the city's administrative count. Permits count entitlement, not presence. Nothing here says how drivers would respond to a different price.
