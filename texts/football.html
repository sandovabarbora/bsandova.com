When did the train leave, and who else missed it

The same five questions, asked of six nations the same way: how many players reach the strongest leagues per million, how many minutes the home league gives its own under-21s, when the first move abroad comes, how strong the home league is, and what the national-team squad is built from. Czechia was the first edition; Denmark, England, Germany, Norway and Spain followed, and a Nations page puts them side by side. Czechia turns out to be the one small nation whose presence in the Big-5 fell and has not come back, and the data can say on which rung.

The atlas maps every Czech professional FBref knows about, 440 players, 192 of them with a full 2025/26 season in one of nineteen leagues, and reads the pool the way a federation would want to: against peers, per head, along the pathway abroad, and at the moment a tournament squad is named1. No predictions, no selection advice. Every number on the page is computed from the run and the build refuses to render a sentence its data no longer supports; there are 317 tests and two dated ledgers behind it.

Where the pathway leaks

Three numbers along the road from a Czech academy to a top-9 roster. Minutes at home: players aged 21 or younger get 6.4 % of all minutes in the Czech First League; in Denmark 15.3 %, Hungary 14.1 %, Croatia 14.0 %, Norway 11.5 %1. Where they go: 27 of the 192 mapped players play abroad; 63 % of them in a top-9 league, but 19 % moved sideways, to a league no stronger than the Czech one by the multiplier4. How they fare: Czech exports keep a median 50 % of their club's minutes, third of nine countries of origin, behind Slovakia (76 %) and Hungary (57 %) and ahead of Denmark (46 %) and Norway (37 %). Fewer leave; the ones who do, play.

And the squad. Of the 26 players named for the 2026 FIFA World Cup, 35 % play in the nine strongest leagues. Switzerland's squad: 88 %. Croatia 73 %, Austria 69 %, Norway 65 %5.

When the train left, dated twice

Count Czech players with at least 450 minutes in the Big-5 leagues, every season since 1995/96, thirty-one seasons. The peak is 26 in 2007/08, the trough 6 in 2015/16, the current figure 10. A local-level model with two step changes at unknown seasons dates them: a rise in 2000/01 (×1.66, posterior probability 0.55) and a fall in 2014/15 (×0.60, 0.56), the pair together at 0.36 against every other pair. At the 2007/08 peak the nation's Big-5 players had a median age of 28; in the fall season it was 32 and no debutant arrived. A generation retired and what followed was thinner110.

The same series for sixteen other countries is what makes this a finding and not a lament. Croatia fell too, in 2003/04, and came back eleven seasons later; Norway fell in 2004/05 and came back seventeen seasons later. Every other small peer's later step is a rise. Czechia is the one whose fall has no recovery yet1.

This is presence, not quality: goalkeepers count, a lineup of who was there. But it dates the problem to a season, and it says the problem is a fall without a rise, which is a different thing from a slow decline.

Two problems at once, and the rung where the mistake shows

Each edition decomposes its gap to every peer into three channels, youth minutes at home, home-league strength and the age of the first move, by a ridge regression across the peers and an Oaxaca–Blinder accounting of the gap. Which mechanism carries the gap depends on the peer: against Norway, home-league strength carries 73 % of a 7.0-per-million gap; against Denmark, youth minutes at home carries 72 % of a 10.2-per-million gap. They add rather than compete. A league that gives its young few minutes and is weak besides loses on both counts1.

The cross-section cannot see change, so the atlas now carries six seasons of every covered league's own under-21 share at home. Czechia's went from 10 % to 6 %; over the same seasons Norway's from 8 % to 11 % with +2.7 per million more players in the Big-5, Denmark's 16 % to 15 % with +2.3, Czechia −0.1. Across the sixteen leagues the change in youth minutes and the change in Big-5 presence lean the same way, a weak signal with the right sign, and the page calls it that1.

Where Czechia is out of line is the first rung. Own under-21s get 6.4 % of home-league minutes and 1.1 regular under-21 starters per club, last among the peers (Denmark 15.3 % and 2.5); the first move abroad comes at a median 24 where the peers that grew moved at 22–23; 32 first moves in the covered seasons against Denmark's 112. Where it is not out of line: the home league itself, multiplier 0.43 and second of nine among the peers, and how its exports fare, a median 50 % of their club's minutes, third of nine. What the peers show is reachable: two regular under-21 starters per club, which Denmark, Croatia and Hungary already do; the first move at 22–23; more of them, not better ones. Four numbers to watch every summer1.

Can a reform cause it? The data can follow two documented cases, and only as a sequence. Germany after the DFB/DFL academy licensing of 2001/02: its own under-21s' share of Bundesliga minutes went from 5 % to 11 % by 2009/10 and is 6 % now. England after the Elite Player Performance Plan of 2012/13: from 3 % to 6 % by 2019/20 and 3 % now. In both, the turn did not hold. A sequence in one country against none in another is the strongest thing this data can say, and it is not a counterfactual. Read as a heuristic: minutes for the young at home are the lever a federation holds, the effect is counted in seasons, and a weaker league yields less from it1.

Six editions are six sentences of the same kind, each written from its own numbers at build time so it moves when they do. Denmark's: 443 players, Euro 2024 as the core squad. Norway's: 511 players, a rise after the fall is a recovery. Spain's: 1 047 players, a flat series says so. Germany's: 932 players, the Bundesliga as home league. England's: the Premier League as home league, which is why per-million ranks are against a nation's own peer set and never across editions1.

The map, and how it is kept honest

Every player-season is one five-feature vector, identical across all nineteen leagues because only the basic table exists for all of them: non-penalty goals and assists per 90, share of club minutes, age, cards per 90. Rates for players with few minutes are shrunk towards the median of their league and season with \(K = 10\) phantom matches, \(\hat r = (\text{events} + K\cdot 90\,\tilde r)/(\text{minutes} + K\cdot 90)\); at the 450-minute floor the league median carries 67 % of the weight, at 900 minutes the player and the median weigh the same4. PCA to two components, K-means, drawn twice: a style map on raw rates and a quality map with the league multiplier applied first.

The multipliers are UEFA association coefficients, current five-year ranking, normalised to the strongest league (Premier League 1.000, Serie A 0.856, Bundesliga 0.788, Czech First League 0.434, Slovak Super Liga 0.217); second tiers at 0.6 of their country's first tier, a stated assumption7. ClubElo was the plan and was unreachable at run time, which the atlas says rather than hides. So each multiplier was perturbed ±20 % on its own and all of them together, 41 scenarios; 37 change nobody in the union of the three position groups' top tens, and the largest churn is one player, on the Czech league's own multiplier4.

The data-quality log is on the page, with counts: 92 women's entries dropped from FBref's country page by surname (the rule and its limits are stated), 2 namesakes disambiguated by club, 125 professionals in leagues without season tables listed by name only, 441 split-season rows collapsed after mid-season transfers, 18 call-up names that match no row8. Every wrangling decision that changed a count, with the count.

The second estimate: what the movers say

The UEFA multipliers are one estimate of what a league is worth. The atlas carries a second, and the two do not have to agree. League strength from the transfer graph: a hierarchical Poisson model of non-penalty goals plus assists per 90, fitted in PyMC on the 2 125 players observed in at least two leagues across 8 108 qualifying player-seasons and seven seasons. Only a mover's own before-and-after separates his level from the league's scoring environment; a model on all players recovers the environment instead (open, high-scoring Eredivisie reads as the strongest league, tight Serie A as the weakest), which is why the corpus is restricted to movers, the same within-subject logic as plus-minus ratings in other team sports. Sampled with NUTS, no divergences, R-hat ≤ 1.00310.

In these terms a Czech First League season is worth 0.67 of a Premier League one (90 % HDI 0.59–0.75), against 0.434 from the UEFA coefficient. The two rankings correlate at Spearman 0.74 over eighteen leagues and disagree loudly in places: Hungary's NB I is sixth by the movers and seventeenth by UEFA; the Eredivisie fifteenth and eighth. Held out on the 2025–26 season (863 candidates), the model predicts next-season production better than either the UEFA multiplier or a naive carry-forward, log predictive density −2.17 against −2.73 and −2.63, mean absolute error 0.127 against 0.151 and 0.13710. The map on this page still uses the UEFA numbers, because the sensitivity table above was run on them; the atlas shows both and says which is which.

Two more models sit beside it. Three models, one task, five seasons: predict a player's league-adjusted production next season from this one, evaluated by rolling-origin cross-validation over five target seasons and 8 850 test pairs. A gradient-boosted regressor wins on pooled RMSE, 0.0706, with the hierarchical Bayesian regression at 0.0713 and persistence at 0.0754; the Bayesian model is the only one that reports an interval, and its 90 % interval covers 91.6 % of outcomes. The multilayer perceptron does not beat the boosting. Dating the break: the count of Czech players in the top-9 leagues, 26 → 6 → 10 since 2007, modelled as a local level with one step change at an unknown season; the change-point falls in 2014–15 with 62 % posterior probability, a ×0.57 (0.38–0.86) drop in the level. A rolling-origin backtest over fifteen origins scores the same model without the step against "same as last season": MAE 2.8 against 2.7, 90 % coverage 0.87, which is to say the forecast is honest and no better than the naive one, and the one forecast made, 11 (5–18) for 2026–27, is a demonstration of the method on a count, not a statement about any player10.

And the question of the previous section, put as a regression: across the eight peer countries, a Bayesian simple regression of top-9 players per million on the U21 share of domestic minutes gives +10.1 per ten percentage points (90 % HDI −1.7 to +23.3, R² 0.27, n = 8), the same sign as a pooled-panel OLS at +7.8 (1.1–14.0); the interval is wide because the panel is small, and the page says so. A ridge decomposition of the gap into three channels, in the linear form of Blinder–Oaxaca, attributes Norway's +7.0 players per million over Czechia mostly to U21 minutes (+4.2) and league strength (+5.1), and Denmark's +10.2 mostly to U21 minutes (+7.4)10.

How it was built

A written design spec, an implementation plan of small tasks, a fresh coding agent per task, a spec-compliance and code-quality review after each, a whole-branch review at the end, and every decision the controller took without me written down as a dated ruling: 23 of them for the first edition. I wrote the framing, the cluster reads and the rulings; the agents wrote the code under the tests9. The whole thing is one repository: pipeline, models, validation, report, site. From a clean clone, uv sync && make restore-snapshot && make render reproduces the report from the committed snapshot without touching the network9.

The live atlas, with 17 player cards chosen by six stated rules, interactive projections and the Czech edition, is at football.bsandova.com. The Bayesian layer described in the next section is on the same page, chapter IV of the methodology.
