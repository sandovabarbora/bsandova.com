Player Pool Atlas, football: Czech players in Europe's strongest leagues

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Czechia has 2.39 players per million in the nine leagues highest-ranked by the five-year UEFA coefficient, seventh of nine countries; Denmark has five times that.
  • The rank holds with a 450-minute floor; Czechia would need 35 players to move up a place or drop to 17 to move down.
  • The Czech Big-5 count fell from 26 (2007/08) to 6 (2015/16) and is 10 now; the model's most probable season for the fall, 2014/15, has probability 0.58, and the size of the fall does not exclude no change.

What the atlas does and comparable work

The atlas asks when Czech presence in the leagues highest-ranked by the five-year UEFA coefficient fell and at which stage of the path from a home academy to a foreign top-flight roster Czechia differs from its peers. It answers with counts per head, cohort tables, pathway measures and a map of player profiles, built the same way for six nations and published at football.bsandova.com1. The CIES Football Observatory publishes demographic and migration studies of players and leagues2, and ClubElo and the Opta Power Rankings rate club strength from match results34. The atlas combines a per-nation peer comparison with pathway and squad measures from public data in one reproducible build. It is exploratory and was not pre-registered, apart from one forecast described under Tests; the specifications are those of the current build, and a superseded one is listed in the change log.

Method

The pool starts from FBref's page of Czech players, 440 professionals, of whom 192 outfield players played at least 450 minutes in 2025/26 in one of the eighteen leagues with FBref season tables (goalkeepers are counted separately)5. The nine strongest leagues are those ranked 1 to 9 by the UEFA association coefficient (five-year ranking 2022/23–2026/27, as of 14 September 2026): the Premier League, Serie A, La Liga, the Bundesliga, Ligue 1, the Primeira Liga, the Belgian Pro League, the Eredivisie and the Süper Lig6. The per-head measure counts the distinct players of each nationality, by FBref's nationality column, who played in one of these leagues in 2025/26, and divides by Eurostat population7. The long series counts players with at least 450 minutes in the Big-5, the first five of the nine, in every season since 1995/96, for Czechia and sixteen other countries. A local-level model (an average level that drifts a little each season) with two step changes at unknown seasons, fitted in PyMC, dates its breaks: for each season it gives the posterior probability that a step falls there, and for each step a factor by which the level changes.

For the map, every player-season is a vector of five features available in all eighteen leagues with tables: non-penalty goals and assists per 90, share of club minutes, age and cards per 90. Rates are shrunk towards the league-season median with a fixed prior of \(K = 10\) matches, \(\hat r = (\text{events} + K\cdot 90\,\tilde r)/(\text{minutes} + K\cdot 90)\), so that at the 450-minute floor the median carries 67 % of the weight and at 900 minutes half. The vectors are reduced with PCA to the two directions of greatest variation and grouped with K-means into clusters of similar players, once on raw rates (the style projection) and once after multiplying by a league factor: the UEFA coefficient normalised to the Premier League (Serie A 0.856, Bundesliga 0.788, Czech First League 0.434, Slovak Super Liga 0.217), with second tiers at 0.6 of their first tier by assumption (the quality projection)1. A second, independent strength estimate comes from a hierarchical Poisson model of goals plus assists per 90 fitted only on the 2 125 players seen in at least two leagues (8 108 player-seasons, seven seasons from 2020/21 to 2026/27, the last of them in progress at the snapshot), because only a mover's own before and after separates his level from a league's scoring environment.

Worked example: the Czech edition

Czechia has 26 players in the nine leagues, 2.39 per million, seventh of nine countries. Denmark, with about half the population, has five times the density. Czechia would need 35 players to pass Slovakia and would fall behind Hungary only at 17. With a 450-minute floor, Czechia has 23 players (2.11 per million) and the order of the nine is unchanged1.

By cohort the largest gap is among midfielders aged 23 to 25, two Czech players in the nine leagues against a peer median of six, and among defenders under 22 Czechia has none against a peer median of 1.5. These are single-season counts of a few players, and the largest gap is picked from all position-and-age cells after the fact; one or two players more or fewer would reorder the table.

Czech players aged 21 or younger get 6.4 % of Czech First League minutes, a floor because FBref leaves some rows without a nationality (at most 7.6 %); the table below sets it and the other pathway measures beside the peers'. The Czech share was 10 % in 2020/21 and 11.1 % in 2024/25; the six-season trend, −0.4 percentage points a season (90 % CI −1.3 to +0.5), does not exclude zero. Of the 192 mapped players, 27 play abroad, 17 of them (63 %) in the nine leagues, and 19 % moved to a league no stronger than the Czech one by its multiplier. The 17 and the 26 above count different things: the 26 include three players under 450 minutes and four goalkeepers, whom the map leaves out, and two players whose longest 2025/26 spell was in the Czech league18.

The Big-5 series peaks at 26 in 2007/08, falls to 6 in 2015/16 and stands at 10 in 2025/26. The two-break model puts a rise in 2000/01 (posterior probability 0.56, level ×1.67, 90 % HDI 0.74–2.40; the HDI, highest-density interval, is the narrowest range that holds 90 % of the model's probability) and a fall in 2014/15 (0.58, ×0.59, 0.37–1.02); the pair together has probability 0.36. The interval for the size of the fall reaches 1, so the model does not exclude an unchanged level. At the 2007/08 peak the Czech Big-5 players had a median age of 28 and in the season of the fall 32, with no debutant arriving. On the small peers the same model finds no step whose size interval excludes no change (table below); Hungary's most probable step is more probable than the Czech fall, but its size is too uncertain to call15.

The gap to each peer is split across three correlates: the under-21 share of home minutes, home-league strength (the mover-based estimate) and the median age at the first top-9 season of players who entered a top-9 league in 2025/26 or 2026/27. A ridge regression, which shrinks its coefficients because three correlates on eight countries leave little to fit, is fitted across the eight countries with data on all three, Czechia and seven peers, and a linear Oaxaca–Blinder split turns each fitted gap into one term per correlate: the country's difference from Czechia on the correlate times its fitted weight. With three correlates and eight countries this is an accounting of a correlation, not evidence that a larger under-21 share or a weaker home league produces more players abroad: the size and wealth of the clubs, coaching, transfer markets and which players stay at home can move both a correlate and the count. The league-strength coefficient is negative (−56.5 per unit of the multiplier), so a weaker home league goes with more players per million, and Norway and Denmark, whose leagues the movers rate below Czechia's, get positive terms. The age term is zero by construction, not because age has no association: by this top-9 measure, which is not the first-move age above, Czechia, Denmark and Norway all have a median of 221.

Tests and measured error

The repository holds 317 software unit tests, and every stochastic step uses seed 429. The robustness and forecasting checks are in the first table below; among them, the mover-based strength model, refitted on earlier seasons, predicts the 863 league changes of 2025/26 better than a UEFA-scaled or an unchanged rate (log predictive density is the average log probability the model gave to what happened; higher is better). The rolling backtest in the table scores the step-free local-level forecasting method, not the dating of the steps. That same step-free model gave the one registered forecast: on 21 September 2026 it put the Czech Big-5 count for 2026/27 at 11 (90 % interval 5–18), to be scored after 30 June 2027 against last season's 10. Refitted on the longer and corrected series it now says 11 (5–19); the registered numbers stand. The data-quality log records every decision that changed a count, as the second table lists1.

Limits

The counts measure presence, not quality, and they are single-season snapshots. The peer comparisons rest on eight countries, too few for the regression and the gap split to be more than descriptive. The two league-strength estimates disagree in places: their rank orders correlate at Spearman 0.74 over eighteen leagues, Hungary's NB I is sixth by the movers and seventeenth by UEFA, and the Czech First League is worth 0.67 of a Premier League season by the movers (90 % HDI 0.59–0.75) against 0.434 by UEFA. The two measure different things, players who moved and the clubs' European results, and the atlas does not reconcile them; the gap split uses the mover-based figure. ClubElo, planned as a third source, was unreachable at run time. FBref has no Slovak league tables, so Slovakia is missing from the youth-minutes panel. Women's entries are removed by a surname rule that can misclassify. The Czech pool is found through FBref's country page, while peers are counted by the nationality column, so the Czech and peer figures come from slightly different routes.

Install and reproduce

From a clean clone, uv sync && make restore-snapshot && make render rebuilds the report from the committed snapshot without network access, and make test runs the tests9. The live atlas is at football.bsandova.com, and the editions are compared on its Nations page.
