When did the train leave, and who else missed it

The same five questions, asked of six nations the same way: how many players reach the strongest leagues per million, how many minutes the home league gives its own under-21s, when the first move abroad comes, how strong the home league is, and what the national-team squad is built from. Czechia was the first edition; Denmark, England, Germany, Norway and Spain followed, and a Nations page puts them side by side. Czechia turns out to be the one small nation whose presence in the Big-5 fell and has not come back, and the data can say on which rung.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • The atlas measures a nation's player pool against its peers with one method, in six editions: Czechia, Denmark, England, Germany, Norway and Spain. Czechia has 2.39 players per million in Europe's nine strongest leagues, seventh of nine, while Denmark has 12.58.
  • Czech players in the Big-5 peaked at 26 in 2007/08, fell to 6 in 2015/16 and stand at 10 now. A model dates the fall to 2014/15, and against sixteen other countries Czechia is the one whose fall has no recovery yet.
  • Czechia falls out of line with its peers at the first rung. Its own under-21s get 6.4 % of home-league minutes against 15.3 % in Denmark, and the first move abroad comes at a median age of 24, where the peers that grew moved at 22–23.
  • Every number on the page is computed from the run, with 317 tests behind it. When each league multiplier was perturbed by ±20 %, 37 of 41 scenarios changed nobody in the three position groups' top tens.
  • The data cannot show whether a reform causes a turn in youth minutes, and the link between youth minutes and Big-5 presence is a weak signal with the right sign. The count measures presence rather than quality, and the atlas makes no predictions and gives no selection advice.

The atlas maps every Czech professional FBref knows about, 440 players, 192 of them with a full 2025/26 season in one of nineteen leagues. It reads the pool the way a federation would want to, against peers, per head, along the pathway abroad and at the moment a tournament squad is named1, and it makes no predictions and gives no selection advice. Every number on the page is computed from the run, the build refuses to render a sentence its data no longer supports, and 317 tests and two dated ledgers stand behind it.

Players in the nine strongest leagues, per million people

The measure counts the distinct players with at least 450 minutes on a 2025/26 roster in the nine strongest European leagues and divides that count by population. Czechia has 26 such players, which is 2.39 per million23.

Minutes at home, moves abroad and the World Cup squad

Three numbers follow the road from a Czech academy to a top-9 roster. The first is minutes at home. Players aged 21 or younger get 6.4 % of all minutes in the Czech First League, against 15.3 % in Denmark, 14.1 % in Hungary, 14.0 % in Croatia and 11.5 % in Norway1. The second is where they go. Of the 192 mapped players, 27 play abroad, 63 % of them in a top-9 league, while 19 % moved sideways to a league no stronger than the Czech one by the multiplier4. The third is how they fare there. Czech exports keep a median 50 % of their club's minutes, third of nine countries of origin, behind Slovakia (76 %) and Hungary (57 %) and ahead of Denmark (46 %) and Norway (37 %). Fewer Czech players leave than in peer countries, and those who leave keep their places.

Of the 26 players named in the Czech squad for the 2026 FIFA World Cup, 35 % play in the nine strongest leagues. In Switzerland's squad the share is 88 %, in Croatia's 73 %, in Austria's 69 % and in Norway's 65 %5.

Czech players in the Big-5 since 1995/96, with two dated breaks

The long series counts Czech players with at least 450 minutes in the Big-5 leagues in every season since 1995/96, thirty-one seasons in all. It peaks at 26 in 2007/08, falls to 6 in 2015/16 and stands at 10 now. A local-level model with two step changes at unknown seasons dates the breaks to a rise in 2000/01 (×1.66, posterior probability 0.55) and a fall in 2014/15 (×0.60, 0.56), with the pair together at 0.36 against every other pair. At the 2007/08 peak the nation's Big-5 players had a median age of 28, and in the season of the fall it was 32, with no debutant arriving, so a generation retired and the one that followed was thinner110.

The same series for sixteen other countries shows how unusual the Czech case is. Croatia fell too, in 2003/04, and came back eleven seasons later, and Norway fell in 2004/05 and came back seventeen seasons later. Every other small peer's later step is a rise, which leaves Czechia as the one country whose fall has no recovery yet1.

The count measures presence rather than quality, with goalkeepers included, as a lineup of who was there. It still dates the problem to a season, and it shows the problem to be a fall without a later rise, which differs from a slow decline.

Youth minutes, league strength and the first move abroad

Each edition decomposes its gap to every peer into three channels, youth minutes at home, home-league strength and the age of the first move, using a ridge regression across the peers and an Oaxaca–Blinder accounting of the gap. Which channel carries the gap depends on the peer. Against Norway, home-league strength carries 73 % of a 7.0-per-million gap, and against Denmark, youth minutes at home carry 72 % of a 10.2-per-million gap. The two channels add up rather than compete, so a league that gives its young players few minutes and is also weak loses on both counts1.

A cross-section cannot show change, so the atlas now carries six seasons of every covered league's own under-21 share at home. Czechia's went from 10 % to 6 %. Over the same seasons Norway's went from 8 % to 11 %, with +2.7 per million more players in the Big-5, and Denmark's from 16 % to 15 %, with +2.3, while Czech Big-5 presence moved by −0.1 per million. Across the sixteen leagues the change in youth minutes and the change in Big-5 presence lean the same way, a weak signal with the right sign, and the page describes it as exactly that1.

Czechia falls out of line with its peers at the first rung. Its own under-21s get 6.4 % of home-league minutes, and there are 1.1 regular under-21 starters per club, last among the peers (Denmark has 15.3 % and 2.5). The first move abroad comes at a median age of 24, where the peers that grew moved at 22–23, and the covered seasons hold 32 Czech first moves against Denmark's 112. The home league itself is in line, with a multiplier of 0.43, second of nine among the peers, and so is how its exports fare, at a median 50 % of their club's minutes, third of nine. The peers show what is within reach: two regular under-21 starters per club, which Denmark, Croatia and Hungary already have, a first move at 22–23, and more players moving abroad rather than better ones. These are four numbers that can be checked every summer1.

The data cannot show whether a reform causes such a turn; they can follow two documented cases, and only as a sequence. In Germany, after the DFB/DFL academy licensing of 2001/02, the German clubs' own under-21 share of Bundesliga minutes went from 5 % to 11 % by 2009/10 and is 6 % now. In England, after the Elite Player Performance Plan of 2012/13, the English clubs' own under-21 share of Premier League minutes went from 3 % to 6 % by 2019/20 and is 3 % now. In neither country did the turn hold. A sequence in one country against none in another is the most this data can support, and it is not a counterfactual. Read as a heuristic, it suggests that minutes for young players at home are the lever a federation holds, that the effect is counted in seasons, and that a weaker league yields less from it1.

The six editions each state their result in a sentence of the same kind, written from the edition's own numbers at build time so that it changes when they do. Denmark's edition covers 443 players, with Euro 2024 as the core squad. Norway's covers 511 players, and its sentence treats a rise after the fall as a recovery. Spain's covers 1 047 players and reports its flat series as flat. Germany's covers 932 players, with the Bundesliga as home league. England's takes the Premier League as home league, which is why per-million ranks are computed against a nation's own peer set and never compared across editions1.

The player map, the league multipliers and the data-quality log

Every player-season is one five-feature vector, identical across all nineteen leagues because only the basic table exists for all of them: non-penalty goals and assists per 90, share of club minutes, age and cards per 90. Rates for players with few minutes are shrunk towards the median of their league and season with \(K = 10\) phantom matches, \(\hat r = (\text{events} + K\cdot 90\,\tilde r)/(\text{minutes} + K\cdot 90)\). At the 450-minute floor the league median carries 67 % of the weight, and at 900 minutes the player and the median weigh the same4. The vectors are reduced to two components with PCA, clustered with K-means and drawn twice, as a style map on raw rates and as a quality map with the league multiplier applied first.

The multipliers are UEFA association coefficients, current five-year ranking, normalised to the strongest league (Premier League 1.000, Serie A 0.856, Bundesliga 0.788, Czech First League 0.434, Slovak Super Liga 0.217); second tiers at 0.6 of their country's first tier, a stated assumption7. ClubElo was the plan but was unreachable at run time, and the atlas states as much. Each multiplier was therefore perturbed by ±20 %, on its own and all of them together, in 41 scenarios. Of these, 37 change nobody in the union of the three position groups' top tens, and the largest churn is one player, on the Czech league's own multiplier4.

The data-quality log on the page records every wrangling decision that changed a count, with the count: 92 women's entries dropped from FBref's country page by surname (the rule and its limits are stated), 2 namesakes disambiguated by club, 125 professionals in leagues without season tables listed by name only, 441 split-season rows collapsed after mid-season transfers, and 18 call-up names that match no row8.

League strength estimated from players who moved

The UEFA multipliers are one estimate of what a league is worth, and the atlas carries a second one that does not have to agree with them. It estimates league strength from the transfer graph with a hierarchical Poisson model of non-penalty goals plus assists per 90, fitted in PyMC on the 2 125 players observed in at least two leagues, across 8 108 qualifying player-seasons and seven seasons. Only a mover's own before-and-after separates his level from the league's scoring environment. A model fitted on all players recovers the environment instead, so that the open, high-scoring Eredivisie reads as the strongest league and tight Serie A as the weakest, and for that reason the corpus is restricted to movers, following the same within-subject logic as plus-minus ratings in other team sports. The model was sampled with NUTS, with no divergences and R-hat ≤ 1.00310.

In these terms a Czech First League season is worth 0.67 of a Premier League one (90 % HDI 0.59–0.75), against 0.434 from the UEFA coefficient. The two rankings correlate at Spearman 0.74 over eighteen leagues and in places disagree widely: Hungary's NB I is sixth by the movers and seventeenth by UEFA, and the Eredivisie fifteenth and eighth. Held out on the 2025–26 season (863 candidates), the model predicts next-season production better than either the UEFA multiplier or a naive carry-forward, log predictive density −2.17 against −2.73 and −2.63, mean absolute error 0.127 against 0.151 and 0.13710. The map on this page still uses the UEFA numbers, because the sensitivity table above was run on them; the atlas shows both and says which is which.

Two more models sit beside the league-strength model. The first compares three models on one task over five seasons, predicting a player's league-adjusted production next season from this one, evaluated by rolling-origin cross-validation over five target seasons and 8 850 test pairs. A gradient-boosted regressor wins on pooled RMSE, 0.0706, with the hierarchical Bayesian regression at 0.0713 and persistence at 0.0754. The Bayesian model is the only one that reports an interval, and its 90 % interval covers 91.6 % of outcomes, while the multilayer perceptron does not beat the boosting. The second model dates the break in the count of Czech players in the top-9 leagues, 26 → 6 → 10 since 2007, as a local level with one step change at an unknown season. The change-point falls in 2014–15 with 62 % posterior probability, a ×0.57 (0.38–0.86) drop in the level. A rolling-origin backtest over fifteen origins scores the same model without the step against "same as last season" at an MAE of 2.8 against 2.7, with 90 % coverage of 0.87, so the forecast is honest and no better than the naive one. The one forecast made, 11 (5–18) for 2026–27, demonstrates the method on a count and makes no statement about any player10.

The question about youth minutes from the earlier section can also be put as a regression. Across the eight peer countries, a Bayesian simple regression of top-9 players per million on the U21 share of domestic minutes gives +10.1 per ten percentage points (90 % HDI −1.7 to +23.3, R² 0.27, n = 8), the same sign as a pooled-panel OLS at +7.8 (1.1–14.0); the interval is wide because the panel is small, and the page says so. A ridge decomposition of the gap into three channels, in the linear form of Blinder–Oaxaca, attributes Norway's +7.0 players per million over Czechia mostly to U21 minutes (+4.2) and league strength (+5.1), and Denmark's +10.2 mostly to U21 minutes (+7.4)10.

How it was built

The work followed a written design spec and an implementation plan of small tasks, with a fresh coding agent per task, a spec-compliance and code-quality review after each, a whole-branch review at the end, and every decision the controller took without me written down as a dated ruling, 23 of them for the first edition. I wrote the framing, the cluster reads and the rulings, and the agents wrote the code under the tests9. Pipeline, models, validation, report and site sit in one repository, and from a clean clone uv sync && make restore-snapshot && make render reproduces the report from the committed snapshot without touching the network9.

The live atlas, with 17 player cards chosen by six stated rules, interactive projections and the Czech edition, is at football.bsandova.com. The Bayesian layer described above is on the same page, chapter IV of the methodology.
