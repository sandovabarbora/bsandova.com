The problem

Every server, network device and application at a university writes a log, and Czech cybersecurity law expects a public institution to look at them. When this thesis began, the Prague University of Economics and Business kept logs from more than 2 200 sources on a central server, and its security tooling analysed about 30 of them. The rest were stored and not read. The thesis set out to build a tool that could read all of them, tell the university's IT centre which kinds of events occur and how often, and make unusual ones easy to find1. The thesis shows the design can connect to the sources; it does not report how many were in the end processed.

What was built

The key idea is old and simple. A log line such as a login record has a fixed part, the words the software always writes, and variable parts: a user name, an address, a time. Replace the variable parts with placeholders and many lines collapse into far fewer patterns, each one a kind of event. The thesis used the Extended Nagappan–Vouk algorithm for this, a frequency-based method descended from Nagappan and Vouk's abstraction of log lines into event types2: a word that appears in the same position across many lines is likely fixed, and a word that varies is likely a parameter. The extension is not the thesis's own; it comes from Daniel Tovarňák's doctoral work at Masaryk University4. It treats a position as variable when the share of different words seen there is above a threshold, set by hand.

Around it sits a pipeline: a connector that pulls logs from the server in chunks over SSH (a secure remote connection), processing split across several processor cores at once, and storage in Apache Parquet, a columnar file format. The thesis reports Parquet files 70 to 85 % smaller than CSV and loading up to 90 % faster, the latter when only some columns are read. A Streamlit dashboard shows the patterns, their frequencies and their variable parts, and lets the IT staff filter and export them. The thesis reports that a month's logs took about 15 minutes to analyse, against an estimated 12 hours before. That baseline is loosely defined: the requirements call it an estimate, and the award leaflet describes it as the time to download a month of data from the 30 analysed sources. It is not a like-for-like measurement, and the gain combines parallel processing, Parquet and the algorithm.

How well it found patterns

To test the pattern detection, a sample of log lines from several sources was labelled by hand, marking which parts of each line are fixed and which vary, and the algorithm's output was compared with the labels part by part. Precision is the share of parts the method marked as variable that really vary; recall is the share of truly variable parts it caught; F1 combines the two into one score. The method reached a precision of 97.3 %, a recall of 94.8 % and an F1 score of 96.0 %, against 82.1 %, 76.4 % and 79.1 % for a baseline of regular expressions (hand-written text-matching rules) that I wrote to catch common values such as IP addresses and timestamps. The threshold was tried at several values on the labelled set itself, so these are in-sample figures.

The thesis counts detection of unusual events as met, using simple statistical methods, but did not measure how well the tool finds them. Of 22 requirements set with the IT centre, the thesis counts 18 as met, 3 as partly met and one as not verifiable. Its two requirement tables disagree on which one: the main text names scalability to very large data volumes, the appendix names long-term availability.

What I would do differently now

The accuracy figures rest on one hand-labelled test set, and the thesis does not report its size or an interval around the F1 score; I would report both, and how far the labellers agreed. The threshold was tuned on that same set, so 96.0 % is an optimistic, in-sample figure; I would tune on one part of the labelled data and report on another. The comparison was with my own regular expressions, which is a low bar: the standard benchmark for log parsing compares automatic log parsers such as Drain and Spell across sixteen public log datasets3, and that is where a new parser should be measured. And the speed-up mixes three changes against a loosely defined baseline, so it cannot say which mattered. The engineering stands; the evaluation is where the thesis was a first draft of what this site now tries to do by default.
