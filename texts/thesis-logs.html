Patterns in a university's logs (master's thesis, 2025)

A note on a master's thesis that built a pipeline to read the logs of more than 2 200 university systems, of which about 30 were being analysed, by turning log lines into patterns.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Universities keep far more logs than anyone reads; this thesis built a way to read them all.
  • Each log line is split into the fixed text and the parts that change, so millions of lines become a few thousand patterns.
  • On lines labelled by hand, the method found the changing parts far better than regular expressions did.
  • The numbers come from the thesis itself and were not re-run for this note; what I would do differently is at the end.

The problem

Every server, network device and application at a university writes a log, and Czech cybersecurity law expects a public institution to look at them. When this thesis began, the Prague University of Economics and Business kept logs from more than 2 200 sources on a central server, and its security tooling analysed about 30 of them. The rest were stored and not read. The thesis set out to build a tool that could read all of them, tell the university's IT centre which kinds of events occur and how often, and make unusual ones easy to find1.

What was built

The key idea is old and simple. A log line such as a login record has a fixed part, the words the software always writes, and variable parts: a user name, an address, a time. Replace the variable parts with placeholders and millions of lines collapse into a few thousand patterns, each one a kind of event. The thesis used the Extended Nagappan–Vouk algorithm for this, a frequency-based method descended from Nagappan and Vouk's abstraction of log lines into event types2: a word that appears in the same position across many lines is likely fixed, and a word that varies is likely a parameter.

Around it sits a pipeline: a connector that pulls logs from the server over SSH in chunks, parallel processing across cores, and storage in Apache Parquet, a columnar format that the thesis reports made the files 70 to 85 % smaller and loading up to 90 % faster than CSV. A Streamlit dashboard shows the patterns, their frequencies and their variable parts, and lets the IT staff filter and export them. The thesis reports that a month's logs went from about 12 hours of processing to about 15 minutes.

How well it found patterns

To test the pattern detection, a sample of log lines from several systems was labelled by hand, marking which parts are fixed and which vary, and the algorithm's output was compared with the labels. It reached a precision of 97.3 %, a recall of 94.8 % and an F1 score of 96.0 %, against 82.1 %, 76.4 % and 79.1 % for a baseline built from regular expressions. Of 22 requirements set with the IT centre, the thesis counts 18 as met, 3 as partly met and one, availability over a long period, as not yet verifiable.

What I would do differently now

The accuracy figures rest on one hand-labelled test set, and the thesis does not report its size or an interval around the F1 score; I would report both, and how many labellers agreed. The comparison was with regular expressions, which is a low bar: the standard benchmark for log parsing compares methods such as Drain and Spell across sixteen public log datasets3, and that is where a new parser should be measured. And the speed-up mixes three changes, parallelism, Parquet and the algorithm, so it cannot say which mattered. The engineering stands; the evaluation is where the thesis was a first draft of what this site now tries to do by default.
