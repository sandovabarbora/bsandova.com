Can a wrong number be seen coming?

On 87 held-out questions, run-time signals barely separate an AI assistant's silently wrong numbers from correct ones; the best of eight models, selected after the fact, reaches an AUC of 0.64 (95 % CI 0.51–0.77).

Run-time signals of silent failure

An assistant that answers business questions with SQL can fail silently: the query runs and returns a plausible number that is wrong. Quaesitor measured how often this happens by planting known traps in synthetic warehouses and asking questions whose true answer is known1. Checking every figure against a verified source is expensive, so a signal available at run time that flags the likely wrong numbers would let the check be reserved for them. Several such signals have been proposed for language models in general. Self-consistency trusts the answer that most samples agree on2, models can be asked to estimate the probability that their own answer is correct3, and semantic entropy measures disagreement between sampled answers in meaning rather than in wording4. Selective classification frames the use of any such score as a trade-off between the share of cases answered and the error among them5. Text-to-SQL benchmarks such as BIRD and Spider 2.0 report execution accuracy on realistic databases but not whether the errors can be predicted6, 7. This text asks whether agreement across repeats, the shape of the SQL and the circumstances of the run predict which numeric answers are silently wrong, on held-out questions. Its contribution is a mostly negative result for this setting, with the size of the one signal that does exist.

Data

The data are the 125 run reports of the Quaesitor harness, committed between 20 and 25 August 2026. The harness plants the reasons for silent failure in a warehouse (duplicated rows from a slowly changing dimension, soft deletes, mixed currencies, a timezone, a wrong denominator) and asks each question three times, in fifteen languages, of several models. Of 5 794 answers, 885 were correct, 3 839 silently wrong, 485 refused, 458 implausible, 87 invalid SQL, 31 without a figure and 9 unmeasured. The silent-failure rate ranges from 56 % to 92 % across packs and model configurations1, 8.

Of the 3 704 numeric answers used for training, 3 698 came from Claude models called through the Claude Code command-line client under the aliases opus and sonnet, in eight configurations, and 6 from a local Llama 3.1 model (per-answer file, private; not verifiable). The harness sets neither a sampling temperature nor a seed, so the client's default applied and was not recorded. Agreement between repeats depends on that temperature, and at a temperature of zero it would be close to trivial. Training excludes the abstention pack and two packs with fewer than 100 numeric answers, which leaves five packs and 87 questions8.

How this was done

The unit is one numeric answer, one repeat of one question, and the label is silently wrong against correct; implausible answers are a separate class and are not trained on. A detector may use only what exists at run time. The first feature set is agreement across the three repeats: how many distinct SQL statements and distinct numbers came back, whether this answer is the majority value and whether all repeats agree. The second is the shape of the SQL: its length, joins, tables, WHERE terms, boolean flag filters, DISTINCT, GROUP BY, date arithmetic, casts and coalesces, subqueries and NULL checks. The third is the run: the model alias, the pack and the language. A fourth set combines all three. The type of trap planted in a question never enters a model8.

Each feature set is fitted with a logistic regression and with gradient boosting, eight combinations in all, and scored by the area under the ROC curve on held-out questions. Cross-validation uses five folds grouped by question, so all fifteen languages of a question are held out together and a model cannot learn which question carries which trap. The best of the eight is selected after seeing all eight scores, without nested cross-validation, so its AUC is an optimistic estimate. The intervals come from a bootstrap over questions9: the 87 questions are the clusters, and each of 2 000 resamples draws 87 of them with replacement and recomputes every AUC on the held-out predictions. The models are not refitted in each resample, so an interval covers which questions happened to be asked but not the variability of fitting, and it is if anything too narrow. The effective sample of every AUC on this page is therefore 87 questions, not 3 704 answers, and within one pack it is 9 to 26. An AUC is read as above or below chance only when its 95 % interval excludes 0.5; otherwise it is read as indistinguishable from chance. Refitted from the published answers, all eight AUCs, the agreement rule and the nine transfer AUCs match detector.json exactly8.

Agreement across repeats

The highest held-out AUC, 0.64 (95 % CI 0.51–0.77), comes from gradient boosting on the agreement features. It is the maximum over eight combinations, chosen after seeing all eight, and therefore optimistic: in the bootstrap it ranks first in 43 % of resamples, and the logistic regression on all features ranks first in 21 %. Its interval is the only one of the eight that excludes 0.5. The other seven lie between 0.43 and 0.62, and all their intervals include 0.5 (fig. 1). The logistic regression on the shape of the SQL, at 0.43 (0.32–0.55), is therefore indistinguishable from chance rather than evidence that the shapes of wrong answers reverse between questions. The run features, model, pack and language, score 0.55 and 0.56 with intervals from 0.36 to 0.78, which neither shows that the wrong answers are spread evenly over models and languages nor that they are concentrated in one8.

Used as a filter at a 50 % threshold, the best model forwards 3.4 % of answers (95 % CI 0.0–11 %), 86 % of them correct (67–87 %), and catches 16 % of all correct answers (0–44 %); against a base rate of 18 %, that lane is about 4.7 times richer in correct numbers than the pool. Of the 126 answers it forwards, 120 are repeats of one e-commerce question (Q09) and the other 6 of one benchmark question, which is why the intervals are wide and lopsided: a resample that leaves out those two questions forwards nothing. Its average precision for correct answers is 0.34 against the 0.18 of an uninformative score, and its Brier score is 0.134 against 0.149. A high-confidence lane of this size could skip the cross-check for about one answer in thirty, at a cost of one wrong number in seven among those skipped, while every other answer would still need checking, and since the lane rests on two questions it may not recur on new ones8.

When all three repeats return the same number, that number is correct 21 % of the time (95 % CI 9–35 %) against the base rate of 18 % (9–28 %). The paired difference, 2.7 pp (−4.6 to 9.2 pp), is indistinguishable from zero, so self-consistency on its own adds little, and when the three repeats wrote three different SQL statements and still agreed on the number, it is correct 20 % of the time (8–35 %). The run reports show the models repeating the same mistake, such as ignoring the duplicated dimension rows or counting soft-deleted rows on every attempt, but the share of agreeing answers that fail on each trap type is not computed in the published output, so this mechanism is described, not measured1, 8.

SQL shape across warehouses

Trained on the e-commerce warehouse, the SQL-shape model scores 0.53 on the SaaS warehouse (95 % CI 0.37–0.68) and 0.62 on the taxi one (0.13–1.00); trained on taxi, it scores 0.41 on SaaS (0.26–0.71) and 0.44 on e-commerce (0.22–0.68) (fig. 2). All six transfer intervals include 0.5, and those scored on SaaS or taxi, with 9 and 10 questions, span most of the scale. What the model can learn is what the traps of a particular warehouse look like, and a detector deployed on new questions in a new warehouse would not have that to go on. Within a pack, held-out questions score 0.29 on e-commerce (0.10–0.52), 0.67 on SaaS (0.34–0.90) and 0.12 on taxi (0.00–0.36). The e-commerce interval includes 0.5, so that value is read as noise. The taxi interval lies wholly below 0.5, so the within-taxi model is anti-predictive, which is a known artefact of leave-group-out cross-validation with few groups: holding out a question shifts the class balance of the training folds the other way, and a model that learns that balance ranks the held-out question backwards. With 10 taxi questions, each fold holds out two, so the taxi value is treated as an artefact of the design, not as a finding about taxi SQL8.

What changed during the analysis

  • 15 September 2026: the abstention pack was excluded after first results. Its correct response is to decline, so every numeric answer in it is wrong by construction and "returned a number" becomes the label; with the pack included the SQL-shape model scored 0.81, and without it 0.58.
  • 15 September 2026: two packs with fewer than 100 numeric answers (benchmark bronze v0 and TPC-DS) were excluded.
  • 29 September 2026: question-level bootstrap intervals and the rule for reading them (above or below chance only when the 95 % interval excludes 0.5) were added after all point estimates had been published.

What this means

What is measured is that, on 87 questions with planted traps, the best of eight run-time scores separates silently wrong numbers from correct ones with a held-out AUC of 0.64 (95 % CI 0.51–0.77), and that its high-confidence lane forwards 3.4 % of answers, 86 % of them correct (67–87 %), almost all of them repeats of one question. That a similar lane would appear on new questions or in another warehouse is not measured, and the transfer intervals, all of which include 0.5, give no support for it. For a system that cross-checks figures against a catalogue of verified ones, such as the trust layer the author runs at work (private; not verifiable), these results do not support replacing the check with a run-time score; at most they support a narrow lane of answers that skip it, whose error rate would have to be measured on that system's own questions and be acceptable to its users1, 8.

What this does not show

The answers come from one harness with planted traps, not from production questions, and production errors may be easier or harder to predict. The features are written by hand from the SQL text; an embedding of the query might score higher, although the transfer result suggests it would also learn the warehouse. The label is binary, so a 6 % miss and a 2 000 % miss count alike. The sampling temperature of the repeats is unknown, almost all training answers come from Claude configurations, and none of the uncertainty-based signals in the literature, such as semantic entropy or self-reported confidence, were tested. The analysis is exploratory, and the best result was selected from eight: its interval, the only one that excludes 0.5, has a lower bound of 0.51 and accounts neither for the selection nor for refitting the models. The intervals treat the 87 questions as a sample from a wider population of questions, although they were written with planted traps and were not drawn at random. Whether a silently wrong number leaves a trace that recurs across new questions is therefore open, and the one lane that looks useful rests on two questions8.

Reproduction

The 3 704 parsed answers with their run-time features are published as answers.csv. tools/detector/auc.py in the site repository refits the eight combinations, the agreement rule and the transfer matrix from that file with scikit-learn 1.9.1, checks every AUC against detector.json, draws the question-level bootstrap and writes auc.json; uv run --with scikit-learn==1.9.1 --with pandas==3.0.5 --with numpy==2.5.1 python tools/detector/auc.py takes about a minute and a half. tools/charts/silent_detector.py and tools/figures/detector_figures.py draw the two figures from those files. The parser, method/src/detector.py in the private quaesitor repository at commit b99c89e with tests in tests/test_detector.py, and the 125 run reports it reads remain private, so the step from a run report to a row of answers.csv cannot be checked by a reader.
