A wiki that an agent keeps

A personal Obsidian wiki that an agent compiles from primary sources under a written schema, with one example of a gap that became visible once two documents' pages were linked: NIST AI 600-1 cites the extraction papers that vary decoding settings, but none of its memorisation actions names them.

Three layers, a schema and the ingest step

The vault has three layers with different owners. Raw sources are mine and immutable; the agent reads them and never edits them. The wiki belongs to the agent, which creates, updates and prunes its pages. The schema, the file that tells the agent what a page is, how to link and when to split, is revised by both of us as it proves itself or fails1. An index is generated from the pages, and an append-only log records every change. The pattern is Andrej Karpathy's LLM wiki, with a vault layout adapted from an open Obsidian second-brain repository23. It belongs to a longer line of note systems in which knowledge is written once into linked notes and revised, and it resembles LLM memory systems such as MemGPT, which move information between a model's context and an external store4.

Ingesting one source means reading it, writing a summary, updating five to fifteen existing pages, updating the index, appending to the log, and reporting what was touched and what contradicted what. Contradictions between sources are recorded on the page rather than averaged into a consensus, and coverage gaps are marked so that a later source can close them. Machine checks (the vault's lint) look for orphan pages that no other page links to, broken links, sprawl, stale seeds and drift between the index and the pages; sprawl and stale seeds are the schema's own check names and are not defined further here1. The question the vault is meant to answer, informally, is whether integrating each source once into linked pages brings out tensions between documents that answering each question from separately retrieved passages would miss. The next section is one example; it is not a test of that question.

Decoding settings missing from NIST AI 600-1's memorisation actions

NIST AI 600-1, the generative-AI profile of the AI Risk Management Framework, describes data leakage in its section 2.4 (Data Privacy): "during adversarial attacks, LLMs have revealed sensitive information (from the public domain) that was included in their training data. This problem has been referred to as data memorization..." Its suggested actions concern red-teaming for training-data output (MS-2.10-001), deduplication of training data (MS-2.10-003), and reviewing training data for CBRN and intellectual-property content together with "reasonable measures to prevent, flag, or take other action in response to outputs that reproduce particular training data" (MG-3.1-004). None of them names decoding, the step in which the model picks each next word from its probabilities: greedy decoding always takes the most likely word, while temperature, top-k and top-p add controlled randomness. A text search of the vault's copy of the document finds no mention of temperature, top-k, top-p or decoding5.

The link that made this visible came from a textbook. Raschka's textbook introduces decoding strategies with the stated aim of reducing training-data memorisation, after showing a small overfit model reciting its training text verbatim under greedy decoding6. The research literature supports a narrower point, that the decoding choice changes how much memorised text a model emits: Carlini and colleagues, working as attackers, found more memorised GPT-2 text with some sampling schemes than with others, and a later study defines text as extractable when greedy decoding reproduces it from a prefix78. Neither tests decoding settings as a deployer's mitigation. NIST AI 600-1 cites both of these papers in its references; what it does not do is carry their decoding settings into any suggested action5. The wiki's page puts it more strongly, as greedy decoding being the setting most likely to emit fine-tuned data, and that stronger claim rests on the textbook example and has not been tested here9. Whether NIST left decoding out by oversight or on purpose, as a deployment detail, cannot be told from the document.

Incidents, audits and limits

A fabricated quotation once reached the wiki, and the checker written after it is now a separate tool; that incident is described in How to catch a fabricated quote and is not retold here10. The log also records a parallel ingest on 26 August 2026, in which thirteen subagents (separate agent instances working in parallel) took the vault from 69 to 193 pages. The same entry records that the shared files (the index, the log and the schema) kept a single writer, but that two agents both checked that a new page did not yet exist and one then overwrote the other's; nothing was lost only because the second agent noticed11.

Nothing on this page can be checked by a reader, since the vault is private. It has one author and one agent, so what it "surfaces" is shaped by what that author chose to ingest and ask, and the lint results over time, the share of pages with verified quotations and the time and cost of an ingest are not reported. The counts in the header measure the size of the vault, not the accuracy of its pages.
