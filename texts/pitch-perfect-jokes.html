Who is the joke on in Pitch Perfect?

In two runs, a language model mostly agreed with itself but barely with a human on whom the films' jokes target, so every result is exploratory, and one film's satire reading did not repeat.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Across its two runs the model agreed with itself at κ 0.84 on the target and 0.71 on the butt, yet it found 194 and 207 jokes in the first film and 228 and 230 in the second. With the one human coder it barely agreed, and with one coder and 15 cues the study cannot tell which reading is closer to the films.
  • In run 1 the model's codes put the laugh on the speaker more often than on the group among protected-trait jokes in both films, but in Pitch Perfect's run 2 the interval includes zero, and each film has only about twenty such jokes.
  • Both films aim about one joke in thirteen at America, and each has 4 jokes that would not pass today, the study's label for a joke at a protected trait with the laugh on the group, a classification of joke structure and not a judgement of current taste; the films cannot be told apart on either.
  • This is a count of joke structure in subtitle text, not of intent, delivery or effect, and not a verdict on the films.

Pitch Perfect (2012) and Pitch Perfect 2 (2015) are comedies about a university a cappella group12. This study asked three questions of their jokes: whether the second film aims a larger share of its jokes at America (Q1); whether jokes about a protected trait, such as gender or race, laugh at the person who tells them or at the group (Q2); and whether the second film has more jokes that would not pass today (Q3), the study's name for a joke at a protected trait whose laugh falls on the group, a classification of joke structure and not a judgement of current taste or of the films. A language model found and coded the jokes in the films' English subtitles, as recent studies have compared such models with human coders34, and its codes were checked against a blind sample coded by the author, by Cohen's κ5. The design fixed the questions, the codebook, the readings and a gate for that agreement before any subtitle file was read.

The check failed. On the 15 sampled cues that both the model and the author called part of a joke, their agreement on the target was κ 0.05 (95 % CI 0.00 to 0.17, bootstrap by cue) and on the butt κ 0.35 (0.09 to 0.71), against a registered gate of 0.6 on both. The model agreed with itself far more: between two independent runs over both films, κ on the target was 0.84 and on the butt 0.71, on 1 199 cues. Even so its runs differed, finding 194 and 207 jokes in the first film, and one film's reading on Q2 did not repeat. The study is therefore labelled Exploratory, and every result below describes what this model found; none is a validated measurement of the films.

Data

The author supplied English subtitle files, of the kind written for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, from her own copies of both films; their hashes were committed before any model run. The subtitles are the films' scripts, so they are not published and this page quotes none of them. After the registered cleaning, Pitch Perfect kept 1 356 of 1 551 cues and Pitch Perfect 2 1 889 of 2 071; the 195 and 182 cues dropped were left empty once sound descriptions and formatting were removed. Neither file marks song lyrics with ♪ or #, and neither has a run of three or more wholly italic cues, the registered fallback, so no line was removed as a lyric: whatever sung text the files carry stayed in and could be coded as a joke.

Cues were grouped into scenes wherever a gap of more than 8 seconds separated them: 89 scenes and 1.12 hours of dialogue in the first film, 72 scenes in the second. One scene of the second film, with 35 cues, failed twice in pass 1 of run 1 and was dropped from run 1 by the registered rule, leaving 71 scenes and 1.24 hours of dialogue; run 2 kept all 72 scenes and 1.26 hours. The full runtimes are 1.77 and 1.84 hours.

How this was done

The model worked in two passes. The first gave it one scene at a time and asked for every joke, as a span of cues, and whether it was a joke or an allusion to something outside the film. The second gave it one joke at a time, inside its scene, and asked for three codes from the registered codebook: the target (a group of people, America, the genre of musicals and showbusiness, or a character or situation); for a group, its type; and the butt, whether the laugh falls on the speaker, on the target, or cannot be told from the text. Seven group types count as protected traits: gender, race, weight, sexuality, nationality, religion and disability. Every example in the codebook is invented. One of them, a man saying he is naturally excellent at directions while walking into a lake, is a gender joke with the butt on the speaker (an invented example, not from either film). The prompts name neither film.

The model was claude-sonnet-5-5 at effort high. It ran through the Claude Code command-line client, version 2.1.311, under the author's account, not through the API as registered, because no API key was available. Each call was a fresh process in an empty folder, with the study's system prompt in place of the client's own, the schema as structured output, and no tools, settings files, hooks, auto-memory, MCP servers, skills or saved session. The client still keeps a few lines of its own around the study's prompt: a one-line agent preamble, the description of its structured-output tool, and a block with the working folder, platform, model name, date and the signed-in account's e-mail address; none names the films, the study or its questions. Three plugins still loaded, one of the author's with no hooks and two built-in ones, and a test call on invented text came back unchanged with no hook run. Managed-policy settings and inherited environment variables were not suppressed. The schema was enforced by the client's structured-output tool, which validates the reply, rather than by the API's constrained output format; neither temperature nor thinking was sent, so the client's default thinking applied. These are dated deviations in the change log, with the change that let a run stopped by the account's usage limit resume. Both full runs used identical settings. Run 1 is the run of record and found 194 jokes in Pitch Perfect (16 of them allusions) and 228 in Pitch Perfect 2 (18); run 2, which measures how far the model agrees with itself, found 207 and 230.

Every interval is a 95 % percentile interval from 10 000 bootstrap replicates that resample scenes within each film, because the jokes of one scene share a set-up. The bootstrap resamples 89 and 71 scenes, and the questions on protected traits rest on 21 and 24 jokes. A difference whose interval includes zero is read inconclusive.

Does the model code jokes as a person would?

Before any model run, the code drew 100 kept cues, half from each film, and the author coded each highlighted cue without seeing any model output: part of a joke or not, and if so its target and butt. She coded the first 50 in the form's random order and stopped there, and 3 of those she could not code; so 53 cues are skipped and the validation rests on 47 coded cues, not 100. The form mixed both films in one random order, so the coded cues are not split evenly between the films by design, and the results file does not record the split. The shortening was recorded before any model run, and the gate was not changed.

On whether a cue was part of a joke, the two agreed on 74.5 % of the 47 cues, κ 0.48. The author marked 20 joke cues and the model 22; the model found 75 % of hers, and she marked 68 % of the model's. Target and butt can be compared only on the 15 cues that both called a joke. There, agreement on the target was barely above chance and, even at the top of its interval, far below the gate. The interval for the butt reaches above the gate, but the registered gate is set on the point estimate, 0.35 against 0.6, so it failed whatever the interval. The results file does not break these cues down by target, so agreement on protected-trait jokes, the cases Q2 and Q3 rest on, was not measured on its own.

The model's agreement with itself shows that its codes are not mostly noise from one run to the next. It does not show that they are right, and one coder's codes do not show that they are wrong: the author is a single coder, who had seen both films several times and stated her expectations before the data. The subtitles also leave out the delivery, the look or the cut that often decides on whom a laugh falls.

Q1: jokes at America

In run 1 the model coded 15 of the 194 jokes of Pitch Perfect as aimed at America, 7.7 % (95 % CI 3.4 to 12.6 %, bootstrap by scene), and 18 of the 228 of Pitch Perfect 2, 7.9 % (2.9 to 13.9 %). The registered reading is inconclusive: the two films aim a similar share of jokes at America, or differ by less than this sample can tell, +0.2 pp (95 % CI −6.8 to +7.5 pp, bootstrap by scene). A difference of about 8.0 pp would have been detected four times in five. Run 2 gives shares of 8.7 % (4.4 to 13.9 %) and 10.4 % (4.9 to 16.7 %) and the same reading, +1.7 pp (−5.7 to +9.5 pp). Exploratory.

Q2: on whom the laugh falls in jokes on protected traits

The model coded 21 jokes of Pitch Perfect and 24 of Pitch Perfect 2 as aimed at a protected trait, most often gender (8 and 10); the table gives the shares of each butt. In run 1, D, the share on the speaker minus the share on the group, was +57.1 pp in Pitch Perfect (95 % CI +20.0 to +90.5 pp, bootstrap by scene) and +58.3 pp in Pitch Perfect 2 (+27.8 to +85.7 pp), both above zero, the registered reading that jokes on protected traits more often laugh at the speaker than at the group, but in Pitch Perfect's run 2 the interval includes zero (D +17.4 pp, 95 % CI −25.0 to +60.9 pp, with 12 on the speaker and 8 on the group of 23 such jokes), which would read inconclusive. For Pitch Perfect 2 run 2 gives +66.7 pp (+20.0 to +100.0 pp). The butt agreement with the author, κ 0.35, was measured on 15 joke cues of every kind; agreement on protected-trait jokes alone was not measured, although the design (§8) said the page would give that number. With about twenty jokes per film, both readings are exploratory. The difference of D between the films, +1.2 pp (95 % CI −45.8 to +47.1 pp) in run 1 and +49.3 pp (−13.0 to +104.3 pp) in run 2, carries no reading by design.

Q3: jokes that would not pass today

In run 1 the model coded 4 jokes in Pitch Perfect and 4 in Pitch Perfect 2 as aimed at a protected trait with the laugh on the group, 3.6 and 3.2 per hour of dialogue (95 % CI 0.7 to 7.7 and 0.8 to 6.2). The registered reading is inconclusive: the two films have a similar rate of jokes that would not pass today, or differ by less than this sample can tell, −0.3 per hour (95 % CI −5.2 to +4.0). A difference of about 7.4 per hour would have been detected four times in five. In run 2 the model counted 8 and 3 such jokes, 7.1 and 2.4 per hour (2.7 to 12.5 and 0.0 to 6.1), and the difference was −4.7 per hour (−11.0 to +1.1), larger but still inconclusive: at counts this small, a few jokes coded differently move a rate by several per hour. Exploratory.

Checks

The design registered checks that repeat the questions with one thing changed (§7); the results hold 9 of them. Only one changes a reading: run 2 in place of run 1, for D in Pitch Perfect, as above. Counting every unclear butt as on the group, or every one as on the speaker, leaves D above zero in both films; so do a scene gap of 5 or 12 seconds, leaving out allusions, and widening the protected traits to every group type. Q1 and Q3 stay inconclusive in every check, including with the full runtime as the denominator of Q3. Counted per hour of dialogue instead of as a share, jokes at America were 13.3 and 14.5 per hour, a difference of +1.2 per hour (95 % CI −11.9 to +15.3). A check does not relabel a result.

The author's expectation

On 27 October 2026, before any subtitle was read, the author wrote down what she expected: yes on Q1, a larger share of jokes at America in the second film; mostly satire on Q2, the laugh on the speaker; and yes on Q3, more jokes that would not pass today in the second film. Q1 and Q3 came out inconclusive. Q2 came out as she expected in run 1, but not stably in the first film, and on codes that did not pass the agreement gate. She was also the blind coder, so her expectations could shape her own codes, though not the model's.

What this means

In this study, the decision-relevant result is the agreement: the model agreed with its own second run on the target of a joke at κ 0.84, and with one human coder at κ 0.05 on 15 cues. Self-agreement is a check on noise, not on validity; a model can be reliably different from a person. On the films, the measured quantities are shares and rates of the model's codes: about one joke in thirteen at America in both, 4 jokes that would not pass today in each, and, in run 1, more laughs on the speaker than on the group among about twenty protected-trait jokes per film, a reading that the second run did not repeat for the first film. None of this supports a claim that one film is more or less offensive than the other, or than any other comedy.

What this does not show

It does not show what the writers meant, how audiences took the jokes, or any effect of the films; a difference between the films would be an association with the film, not caused by its year or director. It does not measure today's standards: “would not pass today” is a label for a joke structure. Subtitles are not the performance: tone, timing, visual gags and songs carry much of the humour, and jokes with no line are not in the data. No lyric lines were removed, so sung text may have been coded as jokes. It does not show how well the model codes the butt of protected-trait jokes in particular: that agreement was not measured. Joke segmentation shapes every denominator, and the two runs differed by 13 and 2 jokes. The coding model may know the films from its training and code a line by what it knows of the scene. Pitch Perfect 3 (2017) and a comparison with comedies of today are left to a planned second part, with its own design.

Method, details and reproduction

What was seen before registration, and the deviations

The design discloses it (§0). The author had seen both films several times and stated her expectations before any data; she had not counted or coded their jokes, and no subtitle file had been supplied when the design was tagged. The drafting assistant and the coding model are AI models that have very likely seen quotes from and commentary on both films; every codebook example was written for the design and none paraphrases a scene.

The deviations, each dated in the change log: temperature 0 could not be sent to the model and was replaced by explicit settings, then by the command-line client's defaults; the blind sample was cut to 47 coded cues; the runs went through the Claude Code client, not the API, with the differences described above. Run 1 stopped under the 2 % failure rule when the account's usage limit ended a run of 46 calls in pass 1 of Pitch Perfect 2 without an answer. From then on, calls with no answer were waited out and repeated, while failures in the model's reply kept their single retry. On resuming, the attempts logged as “CLI exited” were called again: 48 in pass 1 of Pitch Perfect 2 and 3 in pass 2 of Pitch Perfect. The old client logged every non-zero exit that way, so the log cannot tell whether the 5 isolated exits, outside the final run of 46, had a reply that the client discarded; re-calling them may have given those units, at most 4, a fresh draw. The client had updated itself from 2.1.311 to 2.1.316 between the stop and the resume; a kept copy of 2.1.311 was used for both runs. The page also departs from the design (§8) in one place: it does not give the butt agreement on protected-trait jokes, because the results file does not hold it. No code, count or estimate had been looked at when any change to the method was decided, and the gate, the codebook and the readings were not changed.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in by tools/pitch_article/article.py from results.json, which the frozen code at pitch-code-cli-2 wrote (tools/pitch/cli.py --analyse), except the counts of the validation form and of the resumed calls, which are from the design's change log. The stages, from hashing the files to the analysis, are in tools/pitch/README.md; they need the subtitle files, which are not distributed, and the model runs cannot be repeated exactly. The tests use invented subtitles only. The design is docs/research/pitch-design.md. Software: Python 3.13.15 and numpy 2.5.36; the bootstrap follows Davison and Hinkley7.
