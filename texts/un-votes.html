Research · politics · Czechia at the UN, 2014–2025

Did Czechia change its UN votes with each government?

Under three governments Czechia sided with the EU majority on 94.8 to 97.6 % of UN votes, with no registered shift (inconclusive, then underpowered), and fewer yes votes on Israel and Palestine were seen only afterwards.

At the UN General Assembly, the EU's members coordinate in New York and usually vote alike.

When a state asks for a recorded vote, every member's vote is kept, and the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library publishes these records1. How often a member votes with the rest of the EU is a plain measure of where it stands among them. Research on these records more often estimates each state's position on a scale2, but agreement can be read vote by vote.

This study asks whether Czechia's agreement with the EU majority, and with the United States, Russia, Hungary and Slovakia, changed between the periods of its governments by more than the other EU members' agreement changed over the same votes. The design was registered before any Czech vote was read. This page reports the changes of 2017 and 2021.

For those two changes the answer is no visible change. Czechia voted with the majority of the other members on 97.6 % of the votes of the Sobotka period, 96.3 % of the Babiš period and 94.8 % of the Fiala period. The other members' mean over the same votes was 96.2, 95.9 and 95.9 %.

Relative to them, Czech agreement changed by −1.0 points in 2017 (inconclusive) and −1.4 points in 2021 (underpowered). Neither change met the registered rule for a shift. The intervals do not rule out a change of a few points either, and most of the 2021 figure rests on the votes of the emergency special sessions.

Over the past decade, then, a change of government in Prague did not come with a visible change in how Czechia voted at the UN overall. That is the yardstick for the third Babiš government: a shift of more than a few points would be something that did not happen in the past decade.

Israel and Palestine, described after the results

The votes on which Czechia left the EU majority grew from 14 in the Sobotka period to 26 in the Babiš period and 34 in the Fiala period. In the Fiala period, 26 of them concerned Israel and Palestine.

The positions on all votes of that topic were counted after the results (table). There were 66, 57 and 52 such votes in the three periods, so the rise in departures did not come with more of them.

The other members voted yes in 77, 74 and 75 % of their positions, a share that changed little. Czechia's yes-share fell from 76 to 67 to 40 %. Its no votes went from 0 to 7 to 13.

The three periods are those of governments of different parties. Under Bohuslav Sobotka it was ČSSD with ANO and KDU-ČSL3. Then came governments led by Andrej Babiš's ANO456, the second with ČSSD's chair as first deputy prime minister7. Under Petr Fiala it was ODS, KDU-ČSL, TOP 09, the Pirates and STAN89.

New texts on Gaza came to the vote only from October 2023, and the data cannot say why either step came. The votes differ between periods, so the comparison is not like for like, and the other members' flat yes-share is the only comparator. Read this way, the Czech exception is one issue, across parties, rather than a pattern of left and right or of one government.

Published accounts describe Czech support for Israel as long-standing. According to Čejka, it goes back to Czechoslovakia's arms and training for the new Israeli army in 1948–1949. During the presidency of Václav Havel, Čejka adds, Czechoslovakia and then the Czech Republic “became one of the most prominent supporters of Israel”10.

In November 2012 the General Assembly made Palestine a non-member observer state by 138 votes to 9 with 41 abstentions11. According to Čejka, no state in Europe other than Czechia fully supported Israel's position10. Dyduch found in 2018 that the Visegrád governments had “frequently abstained from voting in the UN General Assembly or even voted against” on the conflict12.

In 2014–2017, however, the recorded votes did not set Czechia apart from the EU majority on these texts. It left the EU majority on none of them, and its yes-share matched the others'.

On 21 December 2017, in the gap between the Sobotka and Babiš periods, Czechia abstained on the emergency-session resolution on the status of Jerusalem. It said it supported the EU position but did not believe the draft would contribute to the peace process13. The resolution titled “Jerusalem” of 1 December 2021, on which it voted no, is a different text, under the item on the situation in the Middle East.

During a visit to Israel in October 2023, Prime Minister Fiala said that “the Czech Republic stands by Israel and will stand by it as long as necessary”14. Czechia, with Austria and eight others, voted against the call for a humanitarian ceasefire of 12 December 202315. On 11 December 2024 it was one of nine members voting against the demand for a ceasefire in Gaza. Hungary was another, and Austria voted yes16.

The ministry's stated reason was that the resolution “fails to unequivocally link a durable ceasefire to the release of all the hostages”17. In June 2025 Czechia abstained on a resolution on Gaza, stating that the text “fails to unequivocally condemn Hamas’ unspeakable atrocities of 7 October”18. These are the ministry's reasons, and the data do not test them.

The resolution of May 2024 on Palestine's rights in the UN passed by 143 votes to 9 with 25 abstentions19. The data file records Czechia's vote against it.

What to look for in the third Babiš government

Andrej Babiš's third government took office on 15 December 2025, with Petr Macinka as foreign minister20. Its registered test compares Czechia's agreement with the EU majority in the Fiala period (16 January 2022 – 14 December 2025) with the votes cast from 16 October 2026 to 31 December 2026. It is net of the other members' change, uses the same frozen code and is repeated for the four partners. Votes cast before registration are listed in stage B, untested.

Version 5 of the file, ending in December 2025, came out in February 2026, so results are expected around February 2027. If no version covering December 2026 is out by 30 April 2027, the change log will say so.

The test is the overall figure, and it may well come out underpowered, with too few votes to tell. With about 80 to 82 votes in one autumn, the power analysis put the median member's 90 % interval at up to 5.0 points. So for a typical member a change of about 3 points can be told from ordinary movement.

Czechia's standard error in the two earlier changes was 1.4 to 1.9 points, against 1.1 to 1.2 for the median member, which fits departures clustered in a few agenda items. Scaling by that ratio, a rough projection computed in article.py and not registered, gives a Czech 90 % interval about 5.6 to 8.1 points wide, around the 6-point bound beyond which a result is underpowered.

Czech agreement in the Fiala period, 94.8 %, leaves only 5.2 points for higher agreement. If Czech votes do move away from the EU, the past decade suggests looking at particular issues rather than the overall figure.

The author stated her expectation on 15 October 2026, before any Czech vote was read: “I expect a greater lean towards 'Trumpist' policy, and perhaps a greater lean towards Hungary, Russia and Slovakia, though with the Russian invasion of Ukraine in mind” (in Czech: „očekávám větší náklon k 'trumpistické' politice a možná větší náklon Maďarsko, Rusko, Slovensko, ale s ohledem na ruskou invazi na Ukrajinu“). It concerns the partners in the third Babiš period and will be checked there. She stated no expectation for the EU majority or for the earlier changes, so nothing on this page tests it.

Data

The file is version 5 of the UN's General Assembly Voting Data, published in February 2026, with one row for each member's vote on each resolution adopted by a recorded vote1. From 29 January 2014 to the end of the file on 30 December 2025 it holds 1 190 recorded votes. Paragraph votes, amendments and failed drafts are not in it.

A position is yes, no or abstain, and a member that did not vote has none. The EU is the 27 members of February 2020 in every period, without the United Kingdom.

The periods follow the cabinets, each appointed with its foreign minister, who instructs the mission in New York. Each later period starts 30 days after the appointment, because the December plenary adopts texts voted in committee under the old government.

The Sobotka period runs from 29 January 2014, the day the cabinet was appointed, to 12 December 2017. It has 295 votes, on 104 of which the other EU members did not all vote alike. The Babiš period runs from 12 January 2018 to 16 December 2021 (377 votes, 127 split), and the Fiala period from 16 January 2022 to 14 December 2025 (381 votes, 127 split).

The first Babiš cabinet, appointed on 13 December 2017, lost its confidence vote and is pooled with the second. The Fiala cabinet was appointed on 17 December 2021. The 38 and 16 votes in the two gaps belong to neither period.

How this was done

On each vote, Czechia's agreement with a position is 1 if it voted the same way, 0.5 if one of the two abstained and the other did not, and 0 for yes against no. The EU majority is the most common position of the other EU members that voted, and a tie drops the vote. For a partner such as Russia, the position is that partner's own vote.

The registered estimate, β, is a difference in differences on votes. On each vote, it takes Czechia's agreement minus the mean agreement of the other 26 members, each of them scored against the majority of the members other than itself and Czechia. β is the mean of that difference over the later period minus its mean over the earlier one.

A β of −1 point means that Czechia's agreement fell by one point more, or rose by one point less, than the other members' over the same votes. So a change in the agenda or in a partner's own votes is not counted as a Czech change. Intervals come from resampling agenda items, the families of resolutions that return every year, within each period (2 000 draws).

The same β is computed with each other EU member in Czechia's place. The rank p is the share of all members, Czechia included, whose β divided by its standard error is at least as far from zero as Czechia's. With 27 members, a p of 0.10 or less puts Czechia among the two or three members whose change stands out most.

Each β takes the first label that applies. It is lower or higher agreement after the change if β is at least 2 points from zero, its 95 % interval excludes zero and p is at most 0.10. It is no change larger than 3 points if its 90 % interval lies within ±3 points. It is underpowered if the 90 % interval is wider than 6 points, so that the previous rule could not have applied. Otherwise it is inconclusive.

The margin was set from a power analysis run on the other members only, before any Czech vote was read. For changes like the two of stage A, the median member's 90 % interval was 4.2 points wide, and nine in ten members moved by less than 1.3 points relative to the rest.

Checks, partners and the numbers of members

Seven checks were registered with the estimate, listed in the robustness table; two need a word. The confidence-date check starts the later periods 30 days after the confidence votes of 12 July 2018 and 13 January 2022, and the US check cuts the Fiala period at 20 January 2025, when the US government changed. Czechia's share of votes it did not cast is reported, because a government that avoids votes would be invisible to β. A cross-check against UNGA-DM, a database of General Assembly decisions compiled independently of the UN file21, tests the positions themselves.

When the partner is Hungary or Slovakia, that member is neither in Czechia's place nor among the comparison members, so its row ranks 26 members instead of 27. Other members are ranked only if they voted on at least 20 votes of the later period. The partners give twelve labels over the three changes, eight of them in this stage, reported without a correction for multiplicity; none is read on its own.

Agreement with the EU majority in 2017 and 2021

For the change of 2017, β is −1.0 points (90 % interval −3.2 to +1.4, 95 % −3.6 to +1.8, rank p 0.22 of 27). For 2021 it is −1.4 points (90 % −4.7 to +1.6, 95 % −5.3 to +2.1, p 0.26 of 27) (fig. 1).

The 2017 interval reaches past −3 points. So although the estimate does not meet the rule for a shift, a fall of about that size is consistent with the data, and the label is inconclusive.

The 2021 interval is 6.3 points wide, more than twice the margin (the median member's was 4.2 in the power analysis), so the label is underpowered.

The 2021 estimate rests largely on the 16 votes of the emergency special sessions in the Fiala period, 7 under the item on the occupied Palestinian territory and 9 under the item on Ukraine. Without them it is −0.3 points, so about 79 % of it comes from those votes. Czechia left the EU majority on 7 of them, all under the item on the occupied Palestinian territory.

In 2021, Czechia's β with the EU majority was the second lowest of the 27 members' and its β with Russia the lowest, 0.1 and 0.3 points from the nearest other member.

The rank p measures each β against its own uncertainty, and Czechia's was larger than most. Its standard error was 1.9 points against a median of 1.1 for the other members, which fits departures concentrated in a few recurring agenda items. The changes that stood out most among the other members were Hungary's in 2017 (−4.8 points) and Sweden's in 2021 (+5.7).

A yearly view, drawn after the results and not registered, shows the levels without subtracting the other members' change (fig. 2). Each member is scored against the most common position of the other 26. Czechia's agreement ranged from 93.2 % to 98.0 % per calendar year.

It was below the other members' median in every year from 2017 to 2025, by 1.0 to 3.1 points, at its lowest place 22 of 27 in 2022 and 2024. Czechia can sit above the members' mean but below their median because a few members, most often Cyprus and Austria, pull the mean down.

The United States, Russia, Hungary and Slovakia

The levels below are means over the votes on which each member and the partner both have a position. β keeps only the votes on which Czechia has one, so the levels and β do not subtract exactly.

With Russia, Czech agreement went from 69.2 % in the Sobotka period to 63.7 % in the Babiš period and 52.8 % in the Fiala period. The other members' mean went from 68.5 to 64.8 and 56.0 %. Relative to them, β was −2.1 points in 2017 (inconclusive, p 0.11) and −2.1 points in 2021 (underpowered).

With the United States, Czech agreement was 60.6, 58.5 and 63.8 %, above the other members' 57.3, 53.7 and 58.2 % in each period. β was +1.1 and +0.9 points (inconclusive, underpowered).

With Hungary (Czechia 98.8, 95.5 and 95.8 % against the others' 95.4, 91.0 and 89.4 %) β was +1.1 and +1.9 points. With Slovakia (97.8, 97.5, 95.7 % against 96.3, 95.8, 95.1 %) it was +0.2 and −1.2.

Across all 10 estimates of this stage, 5 are inconclusive, 4 underpowered and 1 is no change larger than 3 points. The rank p ranges from 0.11 to 0.73, and none is 0.10 or less.

Votes on which Czechia left the EU majority

The registered list holds every vote on which Czechia's position differed from the majority of the other EU members: 14 of 292 votes in the Sobotka period (4.8 %), 26 of 376 in the Babiš period (6.9 %) and 34 of 381 in the Fiala period (8.9 %). The totals count votes on which Czechia voted and the majority was not tied. Unlike β, the counts do not subtract what the other members did.

The votes were grouped into four topics after the results had been seen, by words in the agenda item and the title. In order: nuclear weapons and disarmament, Israel and Palestine (including the Golan and UNRWA, the UN agency for Palestine refugees), racism (the follow-up to the Durban conference) and everything else.

In the Sobotka period, 10 of the 14 departures were on nuclear weapons and 3 on the Durban follow-up, and none on Israel and Palestine. In the Babiš period, 10 were on Israel and Palestine, 8 on nuclear weapons, 3 on the Durban follow-up and 5 on other topics.

In the Fiala period, 26 of the 34 (76 %) were on Israel and Palestine, among them the annual resolutions on settlements, East Jerusalem, the question of Palestine and UNRWA, and the emergency-session votes on Gaza.

Most departures were half a step away from the majority. In the Fiala period, Czechia abstained where most of the EU voted yes on 18 votes and voted no where most abstained on 10. On 6 votes it voted no while most of the other members voted yes, all of them on Israel and Palestine. In the Sobotka period there were none, and in the Babiš period 2: Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (19 December 2018) and Jerusalem (1 December 2021).

Robustness

The checks are not used to relabel an estimate (§7). Under the same rule, applied to their intervals with the primary estimate's rank p, some would carry a different label: the 2017 change on split votes only would be underpowered (registered: inconclusive); the 2017 change on no half point for an abstention would be underpowered (registered: inconclusive); the 2021 change on regular sessions only would be inconclusive (registered: underpowered). None would meet the rule for a shift.

The largest moves come from split votes only, the votes on which a member's departures have room to show. There β is −2.8 points for 2017 and −4.2 points for 2021, with 90 % intervals of −9.0 to +3.5 and −13.3 to +5.0, from 126 and 127 votes in the later periods.

Without the emergency special sessions, the 2021 estimate is −0.3 points. With the United States, the Fiala period cut at 20 January 2025 gives +1.5 points (−2.0 to +5.4). The other checks stay within 0.4 points of the registered estimates.

What this does not show

It does not show what a government did. A UN vote is cast by the mission on instructions, and this design cannot say who decided a vote, or whether a change came from the government, the ministry, the officials or the agenda. Each period mixes changes of prime minister, coalition and foreign minister.

The foreign minister was Lubomír Zaorálek, nominated by ČSSD, through the Sobotka period22. In the Babiš period it was Martin Stropnický from December 2017 to June 20185. Jan Hamáček, chair of ČSSD, ran the ministry from June to October 2018 and again in April 20217. Tomáš Petříček was minister from October 2018 to April 2021 and Jakub Kulhánek from April 20216. In the Fiala period it was Jan Lipavský9, and in the third Babiš government it is Petr Macinka, chair of Motoristé sobě20.

Positions on failed drafts, amendments and single paragraphs are not seen. Agreement sits near its ceiling, so a handful of votes moves the estimate, and the 2021 estimate rests largely on the emergency special sessions.

The count of positions on Israel and Palestine pools different texts in each period, and the ministry's stated reasons cover only 2 of the 52 votes of that topic in the Fiala period.

The partners change too: the United States in January 2025, Slovakia in October 2023, and Hungary held a parliamentary election in April 2026. So the partner estimates mix Czech changes with theirs. The headline question rests on one autumn's votes, about 80 to 82. The topic grouping and the position count were made after the results and are reading aids, not tests.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in by a script from results.json, the screening and power files, departures.json, describe.json and positions.json. From the repository root, with the UN file in tools/data/un/:

  1. tools/un/screen.py and power.py: screening and power, without Czech positions.
  2. estimate.py --stage A: the registered estimates, checks and the list of departures, with the definitions in votes.py and tests in test_un.py.
  3. departures.py, describe.py and positions.py: the descriptions drawn after the results.
  4. article.py: the figures, the check labels, the rough projection and this page.

The design is docs/research/un-votes-design.md.
