Are Prague's trams later when it rains?

In rain hours a tram gained about 9.7 seconds more delay than in a similar dry hour, but a check failed, so part of that comes with the rain rather than from it.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • The question was set down before any delay was read: does a Prague tram fall further behind schedule in an hour with rain than in a dry hour?
  • Trams gained about 9.7 seconds more delay per trip and hour in rain hours (5.8 to 13.6), against a dry-hour average of about 55.9 seconds; buses about 10.6 seconds. Heavier rain hours went with larger differences, and the metro shows no clear change.
  • The registered placebo failed: rain in the next hour goes with 5.8 seconds more delay now (2.5 to 9.1), about 60 % of the main estimate. Something that comes with rain, not only the rain hour itself, is in the contrast, so the 9.7 seconds are an adjusted association, not a measured effect of rain.
  • A check added after publication, with the hour's temperature as a control, leaves the estimate at 9.5 seconds (5.7 to 13.3). Storm timing, events and the choice of trips with at least two stop passes remain untested.
  • If the association reflected an effect, ten seconds an hour would be small for one trip and would add up across the network on a wet afternoon; the design does not establish that.

Picture a Prague tram stop on a wet afternoon. Umbrellas crowd the shelter, and people shake out their coats as they climb aboard. The tram pulls away a little after the timetable says it should.

Rain can slow a tram in several small ways at once. Boarding may take longer, people who would have cycled may drive and crowd the junctions, and drivers may brake earlier on wet rails. Studies of road traffic agree that rain slows it56, and studies of ridership find that bad weather changes how many people travel7.

How much of that reaches a tram is a smaller question, and one that can be measured. For much of the city a tram runs on its own rails, but it shares junctions and stops with everything else. Every Prague tram reports its position, and the national weather service publishes rain by the hour.

The question was written down before any delay was read. It asks whether, in a clock hour with rain in Prague, a tram gains more delay than in a dry hour of the same route, at the same hour of the day and on the same weekday, in the same season.

The answer is counted in seconds of delay gained during the hour. A tram that left its terminus late stays late for the rest of its trip whatever the weather, so the delay it already carries would be the wrong measure.

Buses were measured alongside, as a secondary estimate. The metro runs underground on its own track and serves as a control, a comparison that should show no difference at all.

Data

The delays come from the history of Prague Integrated Transport that the author collected from Golemio, the city's open-data platform, for a master's thesis on how delays persist through the day12. It covers 15 March to 8 September 2025 and holds 121 197 794 rows.

Each row is one vehicle passing one stop on its way to the next, with the scheduled time, the observed time and the delay at that stop. The design had expected raw positions, but the rows turned out to be these stop passes, so "position" in the registered definition is read as a stop pass.

For each trip and clock hour with at least two passes, the delay gained is the delay at its last pass in the hour minus the delay at its first. The comparison works on units. A unit is one route in one direction on one date in one hour, from 05:00 to 23:59 Prague time, and its outcome is the mean delay gained over its trips. The direction is the trip's terminal stop, so short turns and diversions form units of their own.

The rain comes from the open climate data of the Czech Hydrometeorological Institute3, which records the hourly precipitation total at Ruzyně, Libuš, Kbely and Klementinum. An hour counts as a rain hour if the four stations average at least 0.5 mm.

An hour counts as dry only if all four recorded nothing in that hour and in the two before it. That keeps wet rails and lingering crowds after a shower from passing for dry. Hours in between are left out of the main comparison and come back only in the check by amount of rain.

Most days stayed dry. Over the window, rain fell in 122 of the service hours on the dates analysed, against 2 608 dry hours (fig. 1). Those rain hours were spread over 47 dates, and the wettest day was 25 April, with 9 rain hours.

Three screening rules, filters fixed in advance, were applied before any delay value was read. The first removes route-days with a planned closure, diversion or replacement service. Prague Integrated Transport deletes its change pages once a change is over, so the announcements were recovered from the Internet Archive4. They cover 240 planned changes touching a tram or city bus line in the window.

The 8 archived snapshots of the list of changes name 173 such closures. Of these, 125 also have their own archived page, and the rest were taken from the snapshots. Closures that began and ended between two snapshots without an archived page are missed, and their number is unknown.

Incidents such as accidents and breakdowns were not removed, because rain can cause them. Three tram closures lasted the whole window, among them the Libeň bridge, closed to trams since November 2024. A route that is diverted on every date has that diversion as its normal pattern here, so those routes are kept. The rule removes 1 320 tram line-days.

The second rule drops dates on which the feed went silent for more than an hour between 05:00 and 23:00. That happened on 8 dates, two of them at the edges of the window. The third drops units with fewer than two trips, 4.2 % of tram trip-hours. What remains is 132 443 tram units in rain or dry hours on 170 dates.

How the estimate works

The comparison is between rain and dry hours of the same route, direction, hour of the day and weekday, and within the same date. In a regression, that means a separate level for every route, direction, hour and weekday, which absorbs the fact that line 22 at 17:00 on a Friday is always slower than line 31 at 10:00 on a Sunday. It also means a separate level for every date, which absorbs anything that affects a whole day, such as the season, a football match or the start of the school holidays. The rain estimate is then identified from rain and dry hours within the same day and from the same route-hour on different days. It would measure an effect of rain only if, within a date and a route-hour, rain hours differed from dry hours in nothing else that affects delay. That assumption cannot be checked directly, and the failed placebo below suggests it does not fully hold. The model is estimated by ordinary least squares with pyfixest8, and the uncertainty is clustered by date, because rain falls on the whole city at once and a wet afternoon is one piece of evidence, not hundreds. A wild bootstrap was registered for the case of fewer than 30 rain dates9. With 47, it was not needed.

The rule for reading the result was set in advance. The hypothesis is "supported" if the 95 % interval lies wholly above zero and "not supported" if the 90 % interval lies wholly within ±10 seconds, a margin fixed as smaller than any delay a passenger would notice and tested in the manner of an equivalence test10. Anything else is "inconclusive". Before the estimate, the power calculation was re-run with the spread the data imply, read without the rain indicator. Units vary by about 37 seconds around their usual level and the whole city by about 12 seconds from hour to hour, which gives a standard error of about 2.0 seconds and a smallest detectable effect of 5.6 seconds.

Trams, buses and the metro

Trams lost a little more time in rain hours than in dry ones, though the placebo, a check that should have come out near zero, did not, so part of the gap may not be the rain itself. In an hour with rain, a Prague tram gained 9.7 seconds more delay than in a dry hour of the same route, hour and weekday (95 % CI 5.8 to 13.6).

The interval lies wholly above zero, so by the rule set in advance the hypothesis is supported. Because the registered placebo failed (see Checks), the estimate is read here as an adjusted association. That is a difference that remains after the like-for-like matching, not a measured effect of rain.

The raw averages, before any adjustment, are of the same order. A tram trip gained 67.3 seconds in an average rain hour and 55.9 seconds in an average dry hour. Against that dry-hour average, the estimate is about 17 % more delay per hour.

City buses, the secondary estimate, gain 10.6 seconds (6.5 to 14.7), also supported. On average they go from 45.6 seconds in dry hours to 55.2 in rain hours.

The design expected buses, which share the road with cars, to lose more than trams. Their estimate is a little larger, but the intervals overlap almost completely, so the study cannot tell the two apart (fig. 2).

The metro, the control, runs underground on its own track and meets the rain only through crowding at the stations. Its estimate is −2.8 seconds (−6.1 to 0.6), an interval that includes zero, so there is no clear change.

Delay gained by amount of rain

If rain itself lengthened tram trips, heavier rain should go with more delay. The registered check splits the hours by how much rain the four stations averaged and compares each band with dry hours.

Delay rose step by step with the amount of rain, though anything that comes with heavy showers would rise with it too. In a drizzle of 0.1 to 0.5 mm, trams gained 3.3 seconds (−0.1 to 6.7). Between 0.5 and 2 mm they gained 7.7 (3.3 to 12.1).

Between 2 and 5 mm it was 13.4 (8.5 to 18.4), and in an hour with 5 mm or more 33.5 seconds (8.4 to 58.5). Each band's estimate is larger than the one before (fig. 3).

That pattern is consistent with an effect of rain, and also with anything that comes with heavy showers, such as storms at rush hour. The heaviest band has few hours and a wide interval, but even its lower end is above the estimate for a drizzle.

Checks

The other registered checks run the same analysis with one choice changed, and they move the estimate little (fig. 4). Measuring rain at the station nearest each route, instead of the four-station mean, gives 11.5 seconds (6.7 to 16.3). Measuring the mean delay a tram carries in the hour (its level), instead of the delay it gains, gives 8.3 (3.7 to 13.0).

Keeping the planned closures gives 10.0 (6.4 to 13.6). Reading the closure rule literally, so that the three closures lasting the whole window also exclude, gives 10.7 (5.7 to 15.6). So the result does not rest on how the closures were screened.

Rain in the two hours before shows no clear extra delay, though a few seconds either way cannot be ruled out. With rain one and two hours earlier added to the model, the current hour stays at 9.9 seconds (5.2 to 14.5). The earlier hours get 0.3 seconds (−5.1 to 5.7) and −1.5 seconds (−7.1 to 4.1), with intervals of about ±6 seconds.

One check was added after publication, on 9 October 2026, and is not registered. Rain hours were cooler than dry hours, 15.2 °C against 17.0 °C on average. Part 2 of this series found less delay gained in hot hours, so temperature could carry part of the contrast.

Taking temperature into account barely moves the estimate. With the hour's four-station temperature (read for part 2) as a control in 2 °C bands, it is 9.5 seconds (5.7 to 13.3). With temperature as a straight-line (linear) term it is 9.1 seconds (5.2 to 13.0).

The registered placebo is a test that should find nothing. It swaps rain in the current hour for rain in the next one, which cannot cause anything now, and was expected to come out near zero.

It failed. It gave 5.8 seconds (2.5 to 9.1), about 60 % of the main estimate, with an interval wholly above zero.

One possible reason is that rain persists. When the next hour is wet, the current one often is too, or has just stopped below the 0.5 mm line, so the placebo would pick up part of the rain contrast. That explanation is untested.

The other possibility is that something which comes with rainy spells and is not the rain hour itself, such as storms, heavier traffic or events, raises delay. In that case part of the 9.7 seconds belongs to it.

A cleaner placebo, added after this result and not registered, uses only hours that are dry now and asks whether rain in the next hour goes with more delay. It gives 4.2 seconds (−3.5 to 12.0), on only 507 units with rain in the next hour. Its interval includes zero but is too wide to rule out a few seconds, so it neither confirms the persistence reading nor contradicts it.

Routes

The design asked for a map of routes by their own estimate, as a description rather than a test. Of the 27 tram routes with at least 60 rain units, every one has a positive estimate. They run from 3.5 seconds on line 31 to 21.3 on line 11, and 14 of them have an interval wholly above zero (fig. 5).

With one estimate per route, some of the spread is chance. The routes were not ranked or tested against each other, so the map is not evidence that any line suffers more than another.

What this means

In an hour with rain, a Prague tram fell about 9.7 seconds further behind schedule than in a dry hour of the same route at the same time, though the registered placebo failed, so not all of that need be the rain. The gap grows with the amount of rain, and buses show about as much.

That rain itself is behind it is an inference, held with less confidence. The placebo failed at about 60 % of the main estimate, so the 9.7 seconds are an adjusted association, and part of them may belong to whatever comes with rainy spells.

For one passenger on one trip, ten seconds an hour is hardly noticeable. If the association reflected an effect, it would add up across the network on a wet afternoon, since the thesis behind this data found that delays persist through the day1. The design does not establish that.

The study also cannot say which part of the difference is boarding, traffic or braking, only their sum.

What this does not show

The estimate is the total of everything that differs between rain and dry hours, not the effect of water on a tram. A rain hour can bring more passengers boarding slowly, more cars on the road, lower speeds and wet rails.

The registered model did not control for several things that come with rain. They are temperature (checked after publication, see above), the timing of storms, which in summer cluster in the late afternoon rush, and events or disruptions that happen to fall on wet days.

The units keep only trips with at least two stop passes in an hour. If rain changes which trips meet that rule, the selection could move the estimate.

The window runs from March to September, so it says nothing about snow or frost. It is also one season of one year, while the network, timetables and closures change from year to year.

The delay is an average per trip and hour. It does not tell how late a given tram will be.

Rain is measured at four stations, but summer showers can be local. Some hours counted as dry may have been wet on part of the network, which would make the estimate smaller rather than larger.

The list of closures comes from an archive in which about seven in ten of the listed closures have their own page, and the screening may have missed short closures. The checks that keep or widen the closures suggest this matters little.

Finally, the placebo registered for this study failed, for a reason that is not known.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from the result files by a script. From the repository root:

  1. tools/rain/power.py: rain and dry hours and the power calculation from precipitation alone.
  2. screen_pid.py and screen_feed.py: the three screening rules.
  3. units.py: the units, the only step that reads delays; test_rain.py checks it on a hand-worked example.
  4. power_rerun.py, estimate.py and describe.py: the power with the data's spread, the registered estimates and the descriptive and post-hoc numbers.
  5. posthoc.py: the temperature check added after publication, written to docs/research/rain-delays-posthoc.json.
  6. article.py: the figures and this page.

The design is docs/research/rain-delays-design.md, the screening rain-delays-screen.md.
