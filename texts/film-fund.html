Do films just above the fund's line reach cinemas more?

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version

  • Just above the line, more projects reached Czech cinemas than just below it, but zero is within the interval, and the bunching check of the design's assumption cannot work by construction, so the data cannot say whether the money changes release.

In the Czech film fund's decision tables, every application in a call has a score in points, and the funded applications sit above a line in that ranking. Somewhere in each list there is a line. The project just above it gets support, and the one just below it does not.

That line is what this study uses. Public film funds decide which films are made, but it is hard to say what their money changes, because the projects they fund are the ones that look most likely to succeed anyway.

A ranking with a hard budget line offers a way round this. When applications are funded in order of their points until the money runs out, those just above the line and those just below it were judged almost alike. Comparing them estimates what the money changes, provided nothing else changes at the line.

Jacob and Lefgren used the funding line of the US National Institutes of Health in this way to estimate what a research grant changes1. Li asked whether reviewers' scores foresee the later success of what they fund2. The method is set out by Cattaneo, Idrobo and Titiunik3.

The Czech film fund publishes, for every call, the points and the award of every application, funded or not. That makes the same two questions answerable for Czech film. Do projects just above the line reach cinemas more often and draw more viewers than those just below it? And do the points of the Council, the fund's board that scores applications, foresee audiences?

Data

The State Cinematography Fund, renamed the State Audiovisual Fund in 2025, publishes a decision table for each call. It lists every application with its total budget, the support requested, the Council's points by criterion and in total, and the support awarded45.

The 514 tables published between 2014 and 2026 hold 6 026 applications in 261 calls. Results for 2015 exist only as PDF documents and are not used.

This study takes the production calls of 2016–2021 for feature fiction, documentary, animation and minority co-production. That leaves every project at least four years to be finished and released by 30 September 2026. A project that applied more than once enters with its first application.

That gives 588 applications in 40 calls, of which 246 were funded. The median award was 1.5 million Kč.

The animation calls include series and combined development-and-production calls, whose output often goes to television rather than cinemas. So for them a release in Czech cinemas is rare whatever the decision: 19 % of funded and 10 % of unfunded animation applications. The registered check on feature-length projects only is in the robustness table.

Short-film production calls are reported on their own, as planned, at the end of the robustness section.

A call drew a median of 14.5 applications, between 2 and 30, and funded a median of 43 % of them.

Where the line fell depended on how much money the call had and how strong the field was. The cut-off lay between 61.4 and 84.1 points out of 100, with a median of 75.7. So a project scoring 75 would have been funded in some calls and rejected in others.

Release is read from two sources. The first is the premiere lists of the Czech distributors' union, which name every film opening in Czech cinemas from 2016 to 20256. The second is LUMIERE, the European Audiovisual Observatory's database of admissions, which lists 999 films with Czech production or co-production from 2014 to 2026 and their Czech admissions7.

The fund's tables and the release sources are public, and every file used is listed with its SHA-256 hash.

How this was done

In every one of the 77 production calls in the tables the line is sharp: every funded application has more points than every unfunded one (fig. 1).

The design is a regression discontinuity, which compares applications just above and just below the line. The running variable, the number that decides funding, is an application's points minus its call's cut-off. The treatment is an award above zero.

The estimand, the quantity to be estimated, is the difference that support makes to a project at the cut-off. It is estimated pooled across calls.

The comparison identifies that effect only under one assumption: that without the award, release and admissions would change smoothly across the cut-off. That requires that the Council did not place projects on one side of the line on purpose, and that nothing else changes at the line.

A straight line is fitted on each side of the cut-off (local linear), and applications closer to the line count more (a triangular kernel). The bandwidth, how many points either side are used, is the one that minimizes mean squared error.

The 95 % confidence intervals give the range the data leave plausible. They are robust bias-corrected, which allows for the curvature a straight line misses, and clustered by call, so applications in the same call are not treated as independent3. The effective sample, the applications inside the bandwidth, is given next to every estimate.

The weak point is linking an application to a released film, because working titles change. The linking was done blind: the code that matched titles never loaded the points or the awards.

An exact match of the normalized title within six years of the call counted directly, with a random tenth of them checked by hand. Every looser match was checked by hand without the funding status, and the links were frozen before any points were joined.

A blind audit of 60 unmatched applications then searched for films released under other titles (see What changed after registration).

The second outcome is Czech admissions, analysed as the logarithm of one plus admissions, with zero for a project that was not released. For 15 linked projects there is no admissions figure, and they are left out of that outcome.

The Council scores knowing how much money a call has, so it could in principle place projects just above or below the line on purpose. Two checks look for that.

The first tests whether applications bunch on one side of the cut-off. It is the local polynomial density test of Cattaneo, Jansson and Ma8, which refines McCrary's9. The second tests whether the budget, the amount requested, the share of the budget requested and the producer's earlier funded applications jump at the cut-off.

Versions 1 to 5 computed the producer's earlier funded applications with each producer's applications in alphabetical order of title rather than in order of time, counting later calls too. Since version 6 it counts only funded production applications of the same producer in calls of earlier years.

All four balance intervals include zero, so the registered balance rule finds no imbalance.

The density test is uninformative by construction. The cut-off is placed between the last funded and the first unfunded project after the money was allocated, so no application sits right at it and few sit close to it. Its p-value, the chance of bunching at least this uneven if there were none, therefore cannot show the absence of sorting.

So the balance checks support the assumption, but the density test cannot, and the estimates below are read with that qualification.

How much the data can show was worked out before this analysis joined any outcome, from the points alone.

Counting every application, repeat ones included, there were about 250 within five points of the cut-off in 2016–2021. With that many, a difference in the share released of about 25 percentage points would be detected four times in five at the 5 % level.

The analysis keeps first applications only, of which 175 lie within five points, so the difference it can detect is larger than 25 points. A difference of 10 points, which would still matter to a producer, would mostly be missed.

The labels were fixed accordingly. An interval that excludes zero is "supported", one that stays within ten points of zero is "not supported", and anything between is "underpowered".

Does support change whether a film reaches cinemas?

Funded projects did reach cinemas more often, though that raw gap is not the answer. Across all 588 applications, 50 % of the funded and 29 % of the unfunded projects were linked to a release in Czech cinemas by 30 September 2026.

That gap mixes the money with everything that made the Council rank a project highly. At the cut-off the comparison is between projects scored almost alike.

There, the share released is an estimated 39 percentage points higher just above the line than just below it. But the 95 % confidence interval runs from −14 to +100 points (fig. 2).

With 182 first applications near the line, the interval is so wide that it includes both no effect and an effect of +100 points. It includes zero and the design's 25-point bound, so the registered label is "underpowered", not "no effect". The data cannot say whether funding at the line changes release.

The plan also asked for this result to be given as a range that bounds the linking errors. Read literally, counting every unmatched project as released, it leaves nothing to estimate, since every application would then count as released. It was therefore computed after publication, on 13 October 2026, from the blind recall audit instead (see What changed after registration).

Each unmatched application counts as released with the share of missed releases the audit found in its group. That share is taken at the ends of its 95 % interval that most favour and most disfavour a difference: 2 to 27 % for funded and 1 to 27 % for unfunded projects.

The estimate then lies between +22 and +45 percentage points. Every interval includes zero, so the label stays "underpowered".

In the worst case, with every unmatched funded project released and no unfunded one, or the reverse, it lies between −26 and +65 points.

The fund's own records may explain part of why the difference is hard to measure. Of the 88 applications rejected within the bandwidth below the cut-off, 28 were funded in a later production call up to 2026 under the same title and applicant.

This count was not in the registered plan, and it is a lower bound, since projects that changed title are not followed.

For a third of the near misses, then, the fund's answer was "not yet" rather than "no", and the comparison at the first application measures the effect of being funded at once against being funded later or never.

Czech admissions show the same pattern, also labelled "underpowered". The estimate at the cut-off is positive, +3.18 on the log scale. Its interval runs from −2.30 to +9.23.

That outcome is hard to read. It is the logarithm of one plus admissions, with zero for unreleased projects, so it mixes whether a film was released with how many people saw it. It also depends on the unit in which admissions are counted and is dominated by the zeros10.

It is not an elasticity, a percentage change in admissions. And since its interval includes zero, not even its sign is established.

The near misses can also be followed descriptively. Of the 94 projects funded within the bandwidth above the line, 54 % reached cinemas.

Of the 28 rejected projects that were funded in a later call, 54 % did too. Of the 60 not funded in any production call up to 2026, 25 % did.

These shares are exploratory, outside the registered plan, and not estimates of an effect, because the projects that tried again and succeeded may differ from those that did not. The last two shares are not produced by any committed script.

Do the Council's points foresee audiences?

How do the Council's points relate to audiences? Among the 195 first applications from these calls whose films reached cinemas and have an admissions figure, the median was 12 200 Czech admissions.

As the plan sets out, a project enters with its first application only. Versions 1 to 4 of this article counted every application, 254 with repeats, which gave ρ = −0.09 (95 % CI −0.22 to +0.04).

Twenty of the 195 are linked to a film that another first application is linked to as well, usually because a different applicant or title applied for the same film. So a few films still count twice.

Within a call, points and admissions show a negative relation that the interval cannot tell apart from none. The rank correlation (Spearman's ρ, from −1 to +1) is −0.14, with a 95 % interval from −0.29 to +0.01 when calls are resampled (fig. 3).

This does not show that the points fail to foresee audiences. It is computed among released films only, a selection that happens after the funding decision. And within a call their points vary less than all applications' points do.

The interval rules out a positive relation larger than about 0.01 but cannot tell a weak negative one from none. It is descriptive and needs its context too.

A criterion named "potential for an audience" appears in the fund's decision tables only from the 2025 scheme4, 5, and none of its films has been released yet.

The plan also asked for the same correlation for each criterion with a fixed meaning across the schemes. Four criteria were scored under the same name in all 40 calls, and this was computed after publication, on 13 October 2026.

For artistic quality ρ = −0.19 (95 % CI −0.32 to −0.05), and for the personnel of the project −0.06 (−0.21 to +0.10). For the contribution to Czech and European cinema it is −0.13 (−0.28 to +0.01), and for the applicant's record +0.02 (−0.18 to +0.21).

Among released films, the artistic-quality points go slightly against admissions. That is one of four comparisons, not corrected for multiplicity, among a selection of films made after the decision. It is not a verdict on the criterion, whose purpose is not to forecast audiences.

Robust bias-corrected intervals can be wider than the share they describe and can exclude their own point estimate when the bandwidth is small. At half the bandwidth, with 57 applications in 21 calls, the estimate (+3 pp, 95 % CI −323 to −46) lies outside its own interval; it is unstable, is not read, and is left out of the range and the count below. For admissions, the halved bandwidth's interval is entirely negative too (−32.05 to −3.45, 28 / 27 applications, 21 calls). Among the stable variants the point estimate for release ranges from +24 to +57 points, so its size depends on choices such as the bandwidth and the window. Three of them have an interval that excludes zero: the donut, call fixed effects and feature-length calls only, the last two added after publication on 13 October 2026, the feature-length one only just; with call fixed effects the admissions interval excludes zero too. These are robustness checks, and the registered labels come from the main analysis, whose intervals include zero, so the labels stay underpowered; read together, every variant puts the estimate above zero, but none of them is precise. Short-film production calls, analysed on their own as planned: 91 first applications in 7 calls of 2016–2021, of which 29 % of the funded and 5 % of the unfunded reached Czech cinemas; at the cut-off +44 pp (95 % CI −16 to +137, 19 / 17 applications), too few calls for clustered errors to be reliable.

What this means

Put plainly, the point estimate is positive, but its range still includes no difference at all, so this study cannot say whether a grant at the line changes whether a film reaches Czech cinemas.

What was measured: in production calls of 2016–2021, projects just above the funding line reached Czech cinemas by an estimated 39 percentage points more often than projects just below it. The 95 % interval runs from −14 to +100 points.

With 182 first applications near the line, that interval includes no effect. Allowing for the released films the title matching missed, the estimate lies between +22 and +45 points.

Across the stable robustness variants the estimate ranges from +24 to +57 points (+3 at half the bandwidth, where it is unstable). Three robustness variants have intervals that exclude zero, but the main analysis does not.

The checks of the assumption the design rests on are weak. The density test is uninformative by construction, though all four balance checks find no imbalance.

In a count outside the registered plan, a third of the projects rejected near the line were funded later.

Among released films, points and admissions showed no clear relation (ρ −0.14, 95 % CI −0.29 to +0.01, first applications). The interval rules out a positive relation above about 0.01 but not a weak negative one.

For someone deciding how the fund should work, the data support little. A rejection near the line was often followed by funding later, and these data cannot show that points given for artistic and production merit foresee audiences.

Whether a single grant changes whether a film reaches Czech cinemas remains open. A design with more calls, or one that follows projects through their later applications, would be needed to settle it.

What this does not show

The effect is estimated at the funding line only and says nothing about projects far above or below it.

Release means release in Czech cinemas. Television premieres, streaming and festival screenings are not in the data, so a documentary made for television counts as unreleased.

Linking misses films whose titles changed beyond recognition. The blind audit of 60 unmatched applications found films released under another title for 3 of 30 funded applications. It found them for 2 of 30 unfunded applications, with one more unfunded case unsure.

Because fewer unfunded projects were linked, the share of released films missed is somewhat higher among unfunded ones (recall, the share of released films the linking found, about 91 % for funded against 82–87 % for unfunded). That would push the estimate for release upward if the same gap holds at the line.

The fund's award is the only money observed. Czech Television, regional funds and the separate production incentives are not, and a rejected project may have found them.

The points cannot show whether a funded film was better than it would have been without support. None of this evaluates the fund as a whole, its other programmes or the 2025 scheme.

Reproduction

The estimates, the validity checks, the robustness variants and the count of near misses funded later are read from assets/film/results.json. The descriptive counts and shares (applications per call, cut-offs, the median award, the table by kind of project and the release share of the 94 funded near misses) are computed from applications.csv. The scripts below write both.

The release shares of the 28 rejected near misses funded in a later call (54 %) and of the 60 never funded (25 %) are the exception. The file applications.csv holds only first applications of 2016–2021 with no flag for later funding, and analyse.py writes only the count of 28. So no committed file or script reproduces these two shares.

Run them from the repository root, in this order, under uv run --no-project with the packages named in each header. The raw files are downloaded into tools/data/film/, which is not committed, and their hashes are in docs/research/film-fund-files.sha256.

  1. tools/film/collect_tables.py and parse_tables.py: the fund's decision tables and one row per application.
  2. collect_outcomes.py and producers.py: UFD premieres, LUMIERE films and Wikidata production companies.
  3. link.py, freeze_links.py, rule4.py and recall_audit.py: the blind links, their review (docs/research/film-fund-links.csv, film-fund-rule4.csv) and the recall audit (film-fund-recall-audit.csv).
  4. analyse.py: the registered analysis (rdrobust and rddensity).
  5. figures.py: the published rows, chart data and static figures.

The design is docs/research/film-fund-design.md.
