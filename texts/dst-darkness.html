When the clocks go back: evening darkness and pedestrian crashes in Czechia

A registered study of ten autumns of police records: in the two weeks after the clocks go back, crashes with a pedestrian between 16:00 and 19:00 rise by about 75 % relative to midday. The registered hypothesis is supported.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • When the clocks go back at the end of October, dusk moves from after the evening rush into it, overnight.
  • In the two weeks after the change, Czech police recorded about 75 % more crashes with a pedestrian between 16:00 and 19:00 than the midday hours would predict.
  • The hour from 17:00 to 18:00, which goes from light to dark, carries most of it: from 1.90 to 2.93 such crashes a day in the three evening hours together.
  • A fake change two weeks earlier finds nothing, so the shortening days alone do not produce the jump. A stricter specification is too imprecise to confirm it, and the article says so.

Twice a year, Europe moves its clocks, and twice a year the argument returns about whether it should stop. The European Parliament voted in 2019 to end the changes, and the member states have not agreed on what to keep since. Most of that argument is about sleep, energy and preference. A narrower question has a cleaner answer: when the clocks go back in autumn, does the sudden darkness in the evening rush cost pedestrians?

The question is narrow on purpose. On the last Sunday of October, Czech clocks go from summer time to winter time, and the sun sets an hour earlier by the clock. In Prague, sunset moves from about 17:45 to about 16:45 overnight. Nothing else about the day changes: offices, schools and shops keep their clock times, so the same people walk home at the same clock hour, but the second half of that walk is now in the dark. In the morning it works the other way: the hour around 7:00 gets lighter. Few things that matter for safety change so sharply on a known date, which is why the clock change has been used to measure what darkness does to road users. Sullivan and Flannagan, comparing the weeks around the changes in the United States, found that darkness raises the risk of a fatal crash for pedestrians far more than for people in cars1; Smith, using the spring change, found that the lost hour of sleep raises fatal crashes too2. This study asks the autumn question of Czech police records, which cover every recorded crash, fatal or not, for ten years.

Data

The Police of the Czech Republic publish every road accident they record, one row per accident, in yearly archives3. Each row has the date and the clock time, the region, and the kind of accident; kind 4 is "collision with a pedestrian". The archives with records go back to 2016; earlier years exist only as printed yearbooks. Across the ten years they hold 979 707 accidents. Until 2022 each region's records are in a separate file without a district; from 2023 the district is included. To treat all ten years alike, the study works with the fourteen regions.

For each autumn, the window is two weeks before the change and two weeks after it, with the day of the change itself left out, because it has 25 hours. In those windows the police recorded 2 768 crashes with a pedestrian, about 9.9 a day for the whole country. For 0.4 % of them the hour is unknown; for other kinds of crash it is 13 %, mostly minor damage reported later. The unit of analysis is a region on a date in a clock hour, with zero where no crash was recorded: 164 640 cells in all.

Three groups of hours matter. The evening group is 16:00 to 18:59, which goes from mostly light to mostly dark at the change. The morning group is 06:00 to 07:59, which goes the other way. The control group is 10:00 to 13:59, which is light on both sides. For every region and hour, the sun's position was computed from astronomy alone4: an hour counts as dark for the share of it after civil dusk or before civil dawn, when the sun is more than six degrees below the horizon. By that measure, 17 % of the evening hours were dark in the two weeks before the change and 64 % after it; the morning went from 47 % dark to 16 %. The police form records the light conditions at each crash as well, and it agrees: the share of evening crashes recorded "at night" went from 24 % to 65 %, and in the morning from 43 % to 15 %.

The raw counts already show the shape (fig. 1). Before the change, pedestrian crashes have two peaks, at 7:00 and in the afternoon from 14:00 to 16:00, and the hour from 17:00 to 18:00 is quieter than the hours around it. After the change, that hour becomes the worst of the day: 17:00 to 17:59 is where the new peak sits. The morning peak at 7:00 shrinks. The midday hours change little. Across the three evening hours, the national rate went from 1.90 to 2.93 pedestrian crashes a day.

How this was done

The design, the hypotheses and the rule for reading the result were committed before any accident record was downloaded. The code was committed and tested on invented data before the records were read. The registration is self-timestamped: commit times are recorded by the author's own machine and pushed to a public repository the same evening, so they show the order of steps, not an independent seal.

The comparison is a difference in differences across hours. The midday hours stand for everything that changes across the four weeks of the window and is not darkness at a particular hour: the weather turning, school holidays, the first frosts, the general drift into November. The evening hours carry all of that plus the jump in darkness. So the effect is the change in evening crashes from before to after the clock change, relative to the change in midday crashes. The model is a Poisson regression on the cell counts, estimated by pseudo-maximum likelihood, which is consistent for counts with many zeros as long as the mean is right5. It has a separate level for every region in every year, for every day of the week and for every clock hour, plus a common linear trend across the window, and standard errors are clustered by date, so that a bad-weather day counts as one piece of evidence, not as many6. The registered estimate is the ratio by which the change multiplies evening crashes relative to midday. The rule was set in advance: "supported" if its 95 % interval lies wholly above 1, "not supported" if it lies within 0.90 to 1.10, and "inconclusive" otherwise. The morning got the same rule with the direction reversed.

One choice was changed before the data were opened, and it matters for how to read the result. The first design also gave the evening and the morning their own day-by-day trends, to separate the jump at the change from the steady shortening of the days. A power calculation from the police's published monthly totals showed that with ten autumns this version could reliably detect only a doubling of evening crashes. The trends were dropped from the main model and kept as a registered check. The cost is that the main estimate includes some natural darkening, about 25 minutes of earlier dusk over the two weeks, besides the clock's hour. Two checks were registered to bound it: a placebo that pretends the change happened two weeks earlier, inside a window with no real change, and a model that uses the sun's position directly.

What the change does to the evening

In the two weeks after the clocks go back, crashes with a pedestrian between 16:00 and 19:00 are 1.75 times what the midday hours would predict (95 % CI 1.38 to 2.23). The interval lies wholly above 1, so by the rule set in advance the evening hypothesis is supported. In the windows of the ten autumns, the police recorded 266 pedestrian crashes in the evening hours before the change and 410 after it. If the ratio is right, about 176 of the 410 would not have happened in the light, around 18 a year in two weeks. That is a back-of-the-envelope figure, the ratio applied to the count, and it covers only the crashes the police recorded.

In the morning, crashes fall to 0.77 times the midday rate (0.57 to 1.04). The direction is the one darkness predicts, the morning getting lighter, but the interval includes 1, so the morning hypothesis is inconclusive. Mornings have fewer pedestrian crashes to begin with, and a smaller sample gives a wider interval. The asymmetry is still informative: the morning gains about as much light as the evening loses, yet the evening effect is much the larger. One reading is that the evening rush is busier and longer, with more people walking to shops and errands after work than walking to work before it; another is that drivers are more tired after work. The data cannot separate the two.

Is it the darkness?

The clock change moves darkness and nothing else, so it can serve as an instrument for darkness itself. The second registered model asks a more general question: how much does an hour in the dark, rather than in the light, raise the expected number of pedestrian crashes in a region? The clock change shifts darkness only in the morning and evening hours, so the hours 6 and 7 and 16 to 18 after the change are used to predict each cell's dark share; the prediction is then related to the crash counts, with the same fixed effects and trend. The instrument is very strong, because astronomy is exact. The result: a region-hour in full darkness has 0.054 more pedestrian crashes than the same hour in daylight (95 % CI 0.038 to 0.069). Before the change, an evening region-hour averaged 0.045, so darkness more than doubles the rate: +118 % (+84 to +153 %).

This model uses the sun's actual position, so the shortening days enter as darkness too, not as a nuisance. It is a linear model on counts, chosen in the design for its simplicity, and its percentage depends on the evening mean it is compared with. It agrees with the main estimate in direction and order of magnitude, and with the American finding that light, not the clock, drives the evening risk1.

The fourteen regions do not get dark at the same time. Czechia spans about five degrees of longitude, so dusk in Moravia-Silesia comes about twenty minutes before dusk in Karlovy Vary. In the two weeks before the change, 37 minutes of the three-hour evening rush were after civil dusk in Moravia-Silesia, the earliest, and 17 in Karlovy Vary, the latest; after the change, 123 and 103 (fig. 3). In Prague the figure jumps from 24 to 110 minutes. The clock change moves every region by about the same hour; the regional differences are small against it, which is why the map is shown for orientation rather than as a separate test.

Checks

The placebo is the most important check, because it measures the alternative explanation directly. It applies the same model to the four weeks before the change and pretends the clocks went back halfway through them. In those weeks, the days shorten just as they do around the real change, but there is no jump. The placebo ratio is 0.92 (0.71 to 1.18): no effect. Two weeks of natural darkening alone do not raise evening crashes relative to midday, which is what the main estimate needed.

The registered stricter specification, with separate day trends for the evening and the morning, gives 1.08 (0.65 to 1.80). It does not confirm the effect; it does not contradict it either. Its interval is so wide that it could hold no effect or a doubling, which is what the power calculation predicted before the data were opened. It is reported because it was registered, and a reader who trusts only that specification should read this study as inconclusive.

The other checks move the estimate little. With a window of one week either side, the ratio is 1.55 (1.10 to 2.18); with three weeks, 1.85 (1.51 to 2.27). Leaving out the autumns of 2020 and 2021, when curfews and closures changed who was on the streets, gives 1.78 (1.36 to 2.32). Crashes without a pedestrian, which were registered as a secondary outcome rather than a placebo, also rise in the evening after the change, by less: 1.28 (1.20 to 1.37). Darkness is harder on drivers too, but it is hardest on the people they cannot see.

What this means

Measured: in the two weeks after the clocks go back, crashes with a pedestrian in the Czech evening rush rise by about 75 % relative to midday, and the rise is concentrated in the hour that switches from light to dark. The two weeks before the change, which darken just as steadily, show nothing comparable. An hour of darkness roughly doubles the expected number of pedestrian crashes in that hour.

For someone deciding how to spend a road-safety budget, the finding is practical rather than political. The first two weeks of November are when the evening risk jumps, and they are predictable to the day. Campaigns about reflective clothing, enforcement at crossings and lighting checks are usually spread over the autumn; this suggests timing them to the week of the change. For the European argument about the clocks, the study says less than it may seem to. Permanent winter time would bring evening darkness earlier all year, and permanent summer time would move it into the morning in winter; the one-hour jump measured here is not the same as either. What it does show is how much a single hour of darkness in a rush costs people on foot.

What this does not show

The data are crashes the police recorded. Crashes with a pedestrian usually involve an injury and are more likely to reach the police than a scraped bumper, but some are surely missing, and the share is unknown. The study cannot see how many people walk in each hour, so it holds exposure fixed only to the extent that people keep their clock-time routines across the change, which is the premise of the design but not something the records can confirm. It says nothing about severity: the counts include light injuries and deaths alike, and ten autumns have too few deaths to study them separately. It does not measure the spring change, sleep, or health. The main estimate includes some natural darkening, as explained above, and the registered specification that removes it is too imprecise to confirm or rule out the effect. Finally, the unit is the region, because districts are recorded only since 2023; a finer map would need finer data.

Changes after registration

All three are dated in the design file and were made before any estimate was run. (1) The archives with records start in 2016, so the sample is ten autumns, and the power calculation led to dropping the evening and morning trends from the main model, which became a check. (2) Districts are recorded only from 2023, so the unit became the region. (3) The first run of the data step dropped every record whose minute was unknown, although its hour was known; this was corrected before any estimate. None changes the rule for reading the result.

Reproduction

Every number on this page is filled in from assets/dst/results.json by a script. From the repository root:

  1. tools/dst/collect.py: the ten yearly archives and their hashes.
  2. prepare.py: the region × date × hour cells and the sun's position (sun.py).
  3. estimate.py: the registered estimates and checks.
  4. article.py: the figures and this page.

The design is docs/research/dst-darkness-design.md, the power calculation tools/dst/power.py.
