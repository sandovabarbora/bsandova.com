Who says no in Prague

Part 1 found an assembly that has almost stopped voting against anything. This version asks why, across four terms and 10 565 roll calls: is it the same people voting differently, or different people? Whose no is it? And on what? Three tests were written down before the votes were modelled. One passed. Along the way the data showed something the tests did not ask about. How a list votes depends on whether it governs, not on the party behind it.

Prague's City Assembly publishes every councillor's vote on every resolution it adopts: 65 seats, four terms since 20101. Part 1 read the current term and found that disagreement had moved off the record. Votes against fell in every term, from 1.06 per 100 councillors present in 2010–14 to 0.28. Pressing nothing rose. Legislative scholars have tools for such records: scaling votes into positions23, asking whether a chamber divides by government and opposition or by left and right4, and asking whether change comes from members converting or from members being replaced5. This text uses them. It uses them under rules fixed in advance: a design reviewed by three referees and committed before any vote was modelled6.

A caveat that frames everything below: the files do not hold every vote. Comparing the numbered votes of each sitting with the rows published shows that the files hold between 55 and 67 % of the roll calls taken. Procedural votes, agenda changes and amendments are missing, and in national parliaments those are where government and opposition fight most78. Everything here is about the final votes on the resolutions the assembly adopted.

The same people, voting differently

Fifty-nine people sat in two consecutive terms and were seated for at least 300 votes in each, 73 such pairs in all. For each pair the test compares a councillor's rate of voting against with their own rate one term earlier. Only votes against are used: they need a button press, so no change in how the system records a member who presses nothing can move them.

The same people also move between government and opposition, and opposition councillors vote against more. Each change is therefore adjusted for the change in how much of the councillor's term their club spent in opposition. Adjusted this way, a councillor's rate of voting against roughly halves from one term to the next (× 0.49, p < 0.001, sign-flip test over people).

The pooled number hides three different transitions.

  • 2010–14 to 2014–18: conversion. The same 21 people voted against a fifth as often.
  • 2014–18 to 2018–22: replacement. The 20 people who stayed voted as before, and the whole fall of the assembly's rate, 0.35 votes against per 100 present, comes from who left and who arrived.
  • 2018–22 to 2022–26: a smaller conversion. The rate fell to about two thirds.

The pattern holds without the adjustment for status (× 0.59), on passed votes only (× 0.47) and without the first transition (× 0.72). It does not hold among the 26 people whose status did not change at all: × 0.86, p = 0.14. People who stay on the same side vote against less, but that small group cannot show it with confidence. Each new cohort also arrives voting against less than the one before: 0.67 per 100 present among 2014's newcomers, 0.30 in 2018, 0.26 in 2022.

It is the role, not the party

The adjustment above turned out to matter more than anything else, and it is worth seeing why. Put each list's share of yes votes side by side for the periods it governed and the periods it did not. The dates come from coalition agreements and news reports9.

Every list that has governed and opposed looks like two different parties.

  • TOP 09 voted yes 91 % of the time while it led the city in 2011–14, and 43 % in opposition in 2016–18.
  • ANO voted yes 95 % in the coalition of 2014–18, and 58–60 % in opposition since.
  • Praha sobě, a local movement, voted yes 95 % in the coalition of 2018–22 and 77 % in opposition since 2023. That is still the most of any opposition club, which is what Part 1 noticed.

Nothing here is causal, and new coalitions bring new agendas. But the size of the swing puts a list's voting on the city's ordinary business mostly down to its seat, not its programme.

No opposition bloc

H3 asked a classic question4. On the contested votes, does a single dividing line between coalition and opposition sort the councillors better than a cut along the left–right order of their national parties? For left and right the design used the Chapel Hill expert survey10: economic left–right for 2018–22, and general left–right for 2022–26, where the economic scale lines the coalition up next to itself (Appendix B of the design).

The answer is no, in both terms. The best left–right cut makes 10 fewer errors per 100 councillors in 2018–22 and 6 fewer in 2022–26. The reason is visible in who votes no.

The coalition clubs almost never vote no. When a club does, it is usually one opposition club on its own.

  • 2018–22: ODS alone on 23 % of the contested votes, ANO alone on 12 %, the two together on 15 %.
  • 2022–26: SPD alone on 20 %, ANO alone on 11 %, the two together on 17 %.

A left–right cut can separate ODS, or SPD, from everyone else, because they sit at one end of the scale. A coalition-against-opposition line cannot, because it must put ANO on the same side. The registered comparison gave left–right that flexibility: any cut against two fixed sides. That asymmetry was in the design before the data were seen. It is reported, and the test is not re-specified.

A one-dimensional scaling of the votes, reported as description3, still puts every coalition club on one side and every opposition club on the other in both terms.

  • 2018–22: the Pirates +0.96, Praha sobě +0.82, the TOP 09 and STAN list +0.20; ANO −1.06, ODS −1.10.
  • 2022–26: the Pirates +0.83, STAN +0.70, SPOLU +0.58; Praha sobě −0.39, SPD −1.08, ANO −1.23.

Both readings are true. The main line runs between government and opposition, and the opposition is several separate noes, not one.

What the opposition votes against

Resolution titles were sorted into subjects by a keyword dictionary. It was fixed before any title was joined to a vote, and checked against an independent coding of 200 titles it had not been tuned on (agreement 90.5 %, κ 0.88)6. The expectation was that land-use changes and property deals, where Prague's scandals have been, would draw more opposition than grants. On the 2 237 council-proposed votes since February 2023 they do not. Opposition councillors present withheld a yes on 41 % of grant votes, 41 % of property votes and 37 % of land-use votes. The registered contrast is −0.4 points (p = 0.53). The lowest shares are on the budget (30 %) and on appointments (31 %). The opposition's no is spread across the agenda, not concentrated on development.

How abstaining ended

Part 1 found abstentions falling abruptly between 2014–18 and 2018–22 and could not say why. Nothing documented changed in how votes are taken.

  • The rules of procedure were amended in 2019, a change the assembly's own transcript calls mostly technical.
  • The chair has asked "for, against, abstaining?" in the same words from 2015 to 2023.
  • New voting equipment came only in 2023.
  • The open data changed how it counts "present" in October 2020, but that field is not used here.

The sittings show a drift and a step. Abstaining fades within each term, and there is a step down at the 2018 boundary that a trend alone does not produce. The step appears even among the 20 councillors who sat in both terms. That argues against pure turnover and for something shared: a norm, a club practice, or a habit of how the room votes. This analysis was demoted to description before it was run, so no test is claimed for it.

What changed after registration

  • The abstention hypothesis became description. This happened before estimation, because its answer had been seen (above).
  • The left–right axis for 2022–26 moved from economic to general left–right. This was a pre-specified fallback, applied because on the economic scale the coalition occupies a contiguous stretch.
  • Three pieces were added after the results and are labelled as such: the breakdown by which club says no, the one-dimensional scaling, and the comparison of lists in and out of government.
  • Some registered robustness checks were not run:
    • a conditional logit and bounds for who returns;
    • alternative contested-vote cut-offs;
    • an audit against complete session records.

The full list, the councillor-to-list crosswalk and every source are in the repository6. Every number comes from four scripts:

What this does not show

Pressing nothing cannot be told apart from stepping out while logged in, from the chair not voting, from a declared conflict of interest, or from a club's collective decision. So the record describes the record, not intent. Party positions are national expert scores for national parties, and Prague's lists are not always those parties. Praha sobě has no score at all. A third to a half of the roll calls are not in the files. Nothing here names or ranks individual councillors, and nothing is a causal effect.
