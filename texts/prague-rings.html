Prague votes in rings, not along lines

The metro is the obvious suspect: three lines that carry the city from its centre to its estates. Put every precinct of the 2025 election next to the nearest station, the distance from the centre and the flats around it, and the metro adds almost nothing to what the other two explain. Prague's vote changes with how far a precinct is from Můstek and with how many of its flats are in panel buildings.

In the Chamber election of 3–4 October 2025 Prague voted SPOLU 33.9 %, ANO 19.9 %, the Pirates 16.9 % and STAN 13.4 %, on a turnout of 71.4 %1. The Statistical Office publishes the count of every one of the city's 1 119 precincts, and the cadastre publishes their boundaries and every residential building inside them, with its number of flats and what its walls are made of2. Together they allow a question that a city map invites and rarely answers: which of the obvious lines through Prague does its vote actually follow?

The map

The pattern is concentric. ANO takes 13 % within two kilometres of Můstek and 25 % between eight and ten, then falls back to 22 % beyond ten, where the estates give way to family houses and villages inside the city boundary. The Pirates run the other way, from 22 % in the centre to 13 % at the edge. SPOLU, the largest party, moves least, from 37 % to 31 % and back to 34 %.

Three rulers

Three measurements can be put on every precinct: how far its centre is from the nearest metro station, how far from Můstek, and what share of its flats are in panel buildings. Laid side by side on the same scale, only one of the first two moves anything.

Near the metro or far from it, ANO's share stays between 19 and 23 %, and the small rise that exists comes with distance from the centre, because the stations thin out as the city does. The estates are where the gradient sits. Where fewer than one flat in ten is in a panel building, ANO takes 16 % and SPOLU 37 %; where more than seven in ten are, ANO takes 26 % and SPOLU 29 %. A third of the city's flats are in panel buildings, so this is not a margin.

Put all three into one weighted regression, with the share of flats in family houses as a fourth, and the metro's coefficient for ANO is −0.05 points per doubling of the distance to the nearest station (95 % interval −0.28 to +0.19): nothing measurable. Every ten percentage points of panel flats go with 0.9 points more for ANO (0.8 to 1.0), and every doubling of the distance from Můstek with 2.0 more (1.5 to 2.4). For the Pirates the metro has no measurable effect either, and the centre is most of the story: 2.0 points fewer per doubling. The model explains 56 % of the variation in ANO's share across precincts, and 54 % of the Pirates'.

The other parties, and who turns out

SPOLU is the mirror of ANO, with a second ingredient. It loses 0.6 points for every ten points of panel flats and gains 0.5 (0.4 to 0.7) for every ten points of flats in family houses, which is why it recovers beyond ten kilometres: the villa quarters and the villages inside the city boundary are its outer ring. The rest follow the rulers weakly. STAN loses a quarter of a point for every ten points of panel flats and Stačilo! gains a sixth; SPD and the Motorists rise a little with the distance from the centre, by a third and a half of a point per doubling. The model explains only 13–29 % of how these four vary: their voters are spread thinly everywhere. The metro's one measurable trace in the whole election is SPD's, a tenth of a point more per halving of the distance to a station (0.02 to 0.17).

Turnout runs against the grain of the metro. It is lowest in the centre, 66.8 % within two kilometres of Můstek, and about 73 % from four kilometres out, and it barely changes with the share of panel flats (71–72 % across the four groups). One reading is that the centre has more registered voters who live elsewhere; the files cannot show it.

The map above has all of this: pick a party, or turnout, or the share of panel flats, and hover over any precinct.

What the rulers are, and are not

These are the places people vote from, not the people. A panel estate is also younger or older, richer or poorer, and more or less often a place people moved to from elsewhere than a street in Vinohrady; the regression cannot tell which of those the panels stand for. The census could; this text does not use it. What the data can say is narrower and still useful: the metro, which planners and campaigns both treat as the city's spine, is not where its politics divides.

The long version

A pre-registered follow-up adds the 2021 census and the 2017 and 2021 elections and asks what this section could not: how much of the estates' gradient is who lives there. Place or people: Prague's estates, measured again.

What was checked

The precinct counts add up to the Statistical Office's published result for Prague: 1 119 precincts, 898 829 registered voters, 632 938 valid votes, SPOLU 33.94 %, SPD 5.24 %1. Boundaries come from the cadastre's current register, which keeps only the latest version of each precinct2: every record is dated after the election, most from two bulk updates in October and November 2025, and 22 were changed from December on. Dropping those 22 moves no estimate by more than a tenth of a point. The metro stations come from Prague's public-transport timetable3, which today lacks Flora: the station has been closed for reconstruction since 2 February 20264, so it is added back as it ran on election day. A building's construction type is recorded for 98 % of the flats; "panel" here is the cadastre's wall panels, concrete included. Every number is produced by tools/praha/metro.py from the published files.
