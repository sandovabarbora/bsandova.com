Prague votes in rings, not along lines

In the 2025 Chamber election Prague's vote changed with the distance from Můstek and with the share of a precinct's flats that are in panel buildings, and the distance to the nearest metro station added almost nothing to what those two explain. A pre-registered follow-up with the 2021 census and the elections of 2017 and 2021 finds that education and citizenship account for about two fifths of ANO's estate gradient, and that the gradient steepened between 2017 and 2025.

Overwhelmed? Here's the short version
  • Across Prague's 1 119 precincts in the 2025 Chamber election, ANO took 26 % where most flats are in panel buildings and 16 % where almost none are, and its share rose with the distance from Můstek. The distance to the nearest metro station added almost nothing to what those two explain.
  • In a pre-registered follow-up with the 2021 census, education and citizenship explain 43 % of ANO's estate gradient, between 31 and 44 % in 48 specifications and never half. The hypothesis that they explain more than half is not supported.
  • ANO's estate gradient steepened from 0.55 to 0.80 points per 10 pp of panel flats between 2017 and 2025, the one registered hypothesis of six that is supported. The test of whether the metro's effect is negligible is inconclusive, because with 282 clusters a negligible effect cannot be told from a small one.
  • The centre's low turnout is not explained by voters who are registered there but live elsewhere; both registered turnout tests are not supported. This is the least secure result, because the published register count includes voters who came with a voting pass.
  • Every measurement is of areas, so nothing here says how any resident votes, and none of the estimates is a causal effect of living in a panel building. The census is from March 2021 and its 1 km grid is coarse, which caps what composition can explain.

In the Chamber election of 3–4 October 2025 Prague voted SPOLU 33.9 %, ANO 19.9 %, the Pirates 16.9 % and STAN 13.4 %, on a turnout of 71.4 %1. Part 1 of this series followed the city council's votes22; this part follows the voters, whose count the Statistical Office publishes for every one of the city's 1 119 precincts, while the cadastre publishes their boundaries and every residential building inside them, with its number of flats and what its walls are made of2. Put together, the two sources show which of the obvious lines through Prague the city's vote follows. The metro is the first candidate, because its three lines carry the city from its centre out to its housing estates; the distance from the centre and the kind of housing a precinct has are the other two.

A study registered in advance adds the 2021 census and the elections of 2017 and 2021 to the 2025 precincts and asks four things the 2025 map cannot answer on its own: how much of ANO's estate gradient is explained by who lives in the estates, whether the metro's effect is small enough to be called negligible, whether the estates' vote has moved over eight years, and why turnout is lowest in the centre.

The 2025 vote, precinct by precinct

ANO's share rises with distance from the centre, from 13 % within two kilometres of Můstek to 25 % between eight and ten, then falls back to 22 % beyond ten, where the estates give way to family houses and villages inside the city boundary. The Pirates run the other way, from 22 % in the centre to 13 % at the edge, while SPOLU, the largest party, moves least, from 37 % to 31 % and back to 34 %. Above the map a party, turnout or the share of panel flats can be picked, and hovering over or tapping a precinct gives its numbers.

Three measurements can be put on every precinct: how far its centre is from the nearest metro station, how far it is from Můstek, and what share of its flats are in panel buildings. Of the 735 755 flats in the precincts, 32 % are in panel buildings. Figure 2 lays the three side by side on one scale.

Near the metro or far from it, ANO's share stays between 19 and 23 %, and the small rise that exists comes with distance from the centre, because the stations thin out as the city does. Across the share of panel flats the change is much larger: where fewer than one flat in ten is in a panel building, ANO takes 16 % and SPOLU 37 %; where more than seven in ten are, ANO takes 26 % and SPOLU 29 %. A third of the city's flats are in panel buildings, so the gradient concerns a large part of the city rather than its margins.

In one regression weighted by valid votes, with the three measurements and the share of flats in family houses as a fourth, the metro's coefficient for ANO is −0.05 points per doubling of the distance to the nearest station (95 % interval −0.28 to +0.19), which cannot be told from zero. Every ten percentage points of panel flats go with 0.9 points more for ANO (0.8 to 1.0), and every doubling of the distance from Můstek with 2.0 more (1.5 to 2.4). The metro has no measurable effect on the Pirates either, whose share falls by 2.0 points for every doubling of the distance from the centre. The model explains 56 % of the variation in ANO's share across precincts, and 54 % of the Pirates'.

SPOLU loses 0.6 points for every ten points of panel flats, the reverse of ANO, and gains 0.5 (0.4 to 0.7) for every ten points of flats in family houses, which is why it recovers beyond ten kilometres, in the villa quarters and the villages inside the city boundary that form its outer ring. The remaining parties follow the three measurements only weakly: STAN loses a quarter of a point for every ten points of panel flats and Stačilo! gains a sixth, while SPD and the Motorists rise a little with the distance from the centre, by a third and a half of a point per doubling. For these four the model explains only 13–29 % of the variation, as their voters are spread thinly across the whole city. The metro's one measurable trace in the whole election is SPD's, a tenth of a point more per halving of the distance to a station (0.02 to 0.17).

Housing stock or residents

The 2025 regression measures precincts, and a panel estate is both a housing stock and a population: people who were allocated flats there in the 1970s and 1980s and grew old in them, their children, and later buyers of the cheapest flats in the city. Compared with a street in Vinohrady, an estate can be younger or older, richer or poorer, and more or less often a place people moved to from elsewhere, and the 2025 regression cannot tell which of those the panels stand for. ANO may do better in the estates because of the estates themselves (their distance, their shops, their neighbours, what the place does to what people think) or because the estates hold more of the people who vote ANO anywhere. Electoral geography has argued about this for four decades, as context against composition34. For immigration attitudes in large European cities, the best recent panel evidence finds that most of what looks like place is people5.

Aggregate data cannot settle this for individuals67, but they can measure how much of the estates' gradient is left once what the census knows about their residents is held equal, and the registered study does that. It also tests whether the metro's effect, which the 2025 model could not separate from zero, is small enough not to matter, whether ANO's estate gradient changed between 2017 and 2025, and whether the centre's low turnout comes from voters who are registered there but live somewhere else.

How this was done

The design was written, sent to three reviewers (a statistician, an electoral geographer and a census-data specialist), revised, and committed to the site's public repository before any census count or earlier election was joined to a precinct8. It fixes the questions, the hypotheses, the one test for each, the decision rules and fourteen robustness checks. The 2025 estimates above were already known when it was written, which is why the metro hypothesis is framed as a test of whether the absence of a measurable effect in 2025 holds up as a negligible effect. Everything that changed afterwards is listed, dated, at the end of that file and summarised below.

The 2025 precinct counts add up to the Statistical Office's published result for Prague: 1 119 precincts, 898 829 registered voters, 632 938 valid votes, SPOLU 33.94 %, SPD 5.24 %1. Boundaries come from the cadastre's current register, which keeps only the latest version of each precinct2. Every record in it is dated after the election, most from two bulk updates in October and November 2025, and 22 were changed from December on; dropping those 22 moves no estimate by more than a tenth of a point. The metro stations come from Prague's public-transport timetable9, which today lacks Flora. That station has been closed for reconstruction since 2 February 202610, so it is added back as it ran on election day. A building's construction type is recorded for 98 % of the flats, and "panel" here means the cadastre's wall panels, concrete included.

The census counts people on a grid of 1 km squares11. Prague's median precinct is an eighth of a square, so every cell's counts were spread over the precincts it overlaps, in proportion to the flats standing in each on census day, from the cadastre's building register2. This dasymetric allocation12 puts 1 301 482 residents into Prague's precincts against the census's 1 301 432, and an independent allocation from the census's 958 basic settlement units in Prague agrees within 25 % for all but 13 precincts.

A precinct still gets only the composition of the square it sits in, while its housing is measured building by building. Comparing the two at different resolutions would flatter housing, so the main models add precincts up into the square that holds most of their flats, which gives 282 clusters, each measured at one resolution. The regressions weight by valid votes. Uncertainty comes from a wild cluster bootstrap over Prague's 57 districts1314, with conventional, small-sample-corrected15 and spatial16 standard errors computed alongside. The six confirmatory tests share a 5 % error rate through Holm's procedure17.

How much of the estate gradient is composition

Across the 282 clusters, ANO's share rises by 0.99 points for every ten percentage points more flats in panel buildings, holding the distance from the centre, the distance to the metro and the share of family houses equal. Adding what the census knows about education and citizenship lowers the gradient to 0.56 points (95 % interval 0.47 to 0.66), and the census block raises the share of variation explained from 70 % to 87 %.

A decomposition of the gap18 puts 0.44 of the 0.42 points on education and nothing on the share of foreign citizens, so education accounts for almost all of the fall. Of the gradient, 57 % survives, and the test in the other direction, whether more than half is left, rejects at p = 0.03. For SPOLU the pattern is the same and weaker, with 42 % explained and neither direction significant.

Two qualifications to this result were written down before it was seen. The census measures composition over a square kilometre, which is coarser than a street, and a coarse measure under-controls for composition, so the gradient left over is an upper bound on what the estates contribute as places and 43 % is a lower bound on what composition explains. The registered primary block also leaves out age, because the estates' age structure is itself partly a product of the housing: flats were allocated to young families in one decade, and those families aged in place. With the share of over-65s and under-15s added, composition explains 52 %, and the test can no longer tell the answer from one half. The 2021 election, a replication fixed in advance, gives 44 % for ANO and 31 % for SPOLU.

Distance to the metro

Planners and campaigns both treat the metro as the city's spine, and the 2025 precinct model found no measurable effect of it on the vote. An interval around zero does not show that an effect is absent, so the registered test asked the stronger question of whether the metro's effect, in the full model with composition, is small enough not to matter. The threshold was set at a quarter of a point per doubling of the distance to a station. Across the middle half of Prague's precincts that is half a point, a fortieth of ANO's vote in the city.

Every estimate is small and every interval crosses zero, but every interval also reaches past −0.25. The design-stage power check, run before any outcome was joined, had predicted this, since with 282 clusters the standard error was bound to be larger than the margin. The margin was not widened afterwards, because the 2025 estimate was already known and a margin chosen after seeing it would prove nothing. The test is therefore inconclusive: with 282 clusters a negligible metro effect cannot be told from a small one. If the estimates lean anywhere, it is towards ANO doing slightly better closer to stations once the rest is held equal.

ANO's estate gradient in 2017, 2021 and 2025

Precinct boundaries move between elections and the cadastre keeps only the current ones. The comparison over time therefore uses the 819 precincts that exist under the same number in 2017, 2021 and 2025 and whose register grew or shrank no more than 10 % beyond what new flats inside them predict. Matched precincts have more panel flats than the rest (40 % against 27 %), because the unmatched are mostly where the city is still building.

Between 2017 and 2025 ANO's estate gradient grew by 0.26 points per 10 pp of panel flats (p < 0.001), from 0.55 to 0.80, with 2021 in between. It is the one registered hypothesis that passes the family-wise threshold. It holds under the stricter and the looser matching rule (+0.24 and +0.30) and with weights that correct for which precincts could be matched (+0.26). The census comes from between the elections, so this comparison holds housing fixed but not people, and it shows that ANO's vote became more concentrated in the estates without showing why.

Turnout in the centre

In 2025 turnout was lowest in the centre, 66.8 % within two kilometres of Můstek, and about 73 % from four kilometres out, and it barely changed with the share of panel flats (71–72 % across the four groups). One possible reason is that the centre has more registered voters who live somewhere else, which the election files alone cannot show. Czech permanent residence is an address in a register, which need not be where a person sleeps, and people with no address of their own are registered at the district office. The registered test compared turnout counted against the register with turnout counted against the census's Czech adults who actually live there. Precincts containing a district office, a hospital, a care home or a prison were excluded, 158 in all.

Against the register, turnout rises by 1.9 points per doubling of the distance from Můstek. Against resident Czech adults it falls by 4.3, so that, measured against the people who live there, turnout in the centre is higher than further out, the opposite of what the registration explanation predicted. The second test agrees that registration does not account for the gap: envelopes scale with the register at an elasticity of 0.99, and residents add nothing once the register is known.

The turnout result is the least secure in the text, for a reason found only after the data were joined. The register count the Statistical Office publishes per precinct includes voters who came with a voting pass, and passes are used most where people are away from home. In all 14 819 precincts in the file, abroad included, envelopes never exceed that count, which is what one would see if pass voters are added to it. The centre's count is therefore not the register alone, and the published files do not allow the two to be separated.

Robustness checks

CheckANO θSPOLU θmetro

Across the 48 specifications in figure 6, composition explains between 31 and 44 % of ANO's estate gradient and never reaches half. Dropping any one of the 57 districts moves θ by at most 0.02, and fractional logit, which respects that vote shares lie between 0 and 119, gives 46 %. At the level of single precincts, where composition is coarser than housing and the leftover gradient is inflated by construction, composition explains 30 %. The clusters' residuals are only faintly clustered in space (Moran's I = 0.06), and a spatial error model gives the same answers (Hausman test20 p = 0.62). Shuffling panel shares within districts leaves an apparent attenuation of 0.19 points rather than zero, because districts differ in both housing and people, and the observed 0.42 lies outside that range. The placebo with the planned line D stations was not run, because official station coordinates could not be found in open data.

What changed after registration

Two changes were made before any outcome was joined to the data. The design-stage power check found that the metro test was underpowered from the start; this was recorded then, and the margin was kept. The publication-timing rule was dropped before estimation.

The design also left some operational details open, and they were settled as follows. Only five-year age groups are published, so 15–17 year olds are estimated as three fifths of the 15–19 group. Dormitories are not flagged, because no open list of them exists, and the bootstrap is unrestricted. The 2017 precinct geometry was not recovered from the cadastre's change files, so the registered matching rule is used alone.

The full list is in the design file8. The 2025 description is produced from the published files by tools/praha/metro.py, and the registered study by four scripts:

  • tools/praha/rings_data.py
  • rings_power.py
  • rings_extended.py
  • rings_robust.py

What this does not show

No result here says how any resident votes, because every measurement is of areas6. The census is from March 2021, four and a half years before the 2025 election, taken during distance teaching and before the refugees of 2022. The grid is coarse, which caps what composition can explain. The areas are Prague's, and a different grid would give somewhat different numbers21. The leftover estate gradient is what the census does not measure, and the place itself is only one candidate for it: income, tenure and how long people have lived there are not in the grid, and any of them could carry it. None of the estimates is a causal effect of living in a panel building.
