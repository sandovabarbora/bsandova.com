Prague, measured · Part 3, the long version · pre-registered · the city's grants to its districts, 2015–2023

Does alignment pay?

Part 3 found that Prague's districts live mostly on money they are given. Much of it arrives during the year, as grants the city council and assembly decide one by one. This version asks whether a district receives more of those grants when its mayor's party joins the city's governing coalition. It reads every grant in the city's closing accounts, tests two hypotheses written down before any amount was added up, and finds no alignment premium. The design could only have detected a very large one, so the honest answer is "not detected", not "none".

Prague's 57 districts get most of their money from the city. Part of it follows a formula the city publishes each December: residents, area, pupils, green space and roads. The rest comes during the year as targeted grants for a school roof, a park, a fire brigade or a playground. Each is decided by the city council or the city assembly, and each is listed, with its resolution and purpose, in part III of the city's closing accounts1.

Discretionary money of this kind is where studies elsewhere have found an alignment premium: municipalities governed by the same party as the level above receive more. The evidence comes from Spain2, Brazil3, Italy4 and Germany5, and the published premiums are of the order of 20–40 % of the grants in question. This text asks the same of Prague, under rules fixed in advance: a design reviewed by three referees and committed before any grant was added up6.

A caveat frames everything below. In Prague, a district "becomes aligned" when the city coalition changes, and the coalition changed only twice in the period. Parties, not districts, switch sides. What follows can therefore separate the effect of alignment from what else happened to one party's districts only as far as two events allow.

How this was done

The grants. The district lists of the city's closing accounts for 2014–2025 were downloaded and parsed: 18 311 lines, and at least 98 % of the item lines in every list. The outcome is the city's own grants. Grants the state passes through the city and EU operational programmes are left out, and so are year-end settlements and refunds, which carry no purpose code.

Each grant is dated by the session of the assembly that approved it, using the session dates of the assembly's roll calls from Part 1. The date decides the regime it belongs to. Grants the outgoing, caretaker council decided in January and February 2023 count for the old coalition. A district's grants are measured against the formula money it received in 2016, so a small district and a large one can be compared. Per resident is shown alongside.

Who governs a district. Two coders, working apart, recorded every mayor of every district from the 2014 elections to September 2026, with sources. They named the same mayor for 98.9 % of the 627 district-years, and the seven that differed were settled before any grant was added up. A district is aligned in a year if its mayor on 30 June belongs to, or was nominated by, a party of the city coalition that day.

The comparison. The coalition changed in November 2018, when the Pirates, Praha sobě and TOP 09 replaced ANO, ČSSD and the Greens, and in February 2023, when ODS replaced Praha sobě. At each change, districts that turned aligned are compared with districts unaligned throughout, before and after. This is a difference-in-differences stacked by event7. The p-values come from randomisation inference: which districts switched is reshuffled within each tier of districts, 9 999 times8. Intervals are from the method for few treated units9.

What the design could see. A power simulation, run on the real switches before any grant was added up, put the smallest detectable premium at about 100 % of a district's usual grants. That is two and a half times the top of the published range. The design says in advance that a non-rejection is to be read as "not detected", never as "no premium".

Percentages are of the control districts' usual grants in the years before each change. p-values are one-sided, from randomisation inference. Holm thresholds are 0.025 and 0.05, and neither test passes. The equivalence test (is any premium within ±20 %?) is inconclusive, p = 0.55.

What the city gives

The city gives its districts between 2.2 and 5.8 billion crowns a year this way, beyond the formula. Investment is between 48 and 73 % of it. For a typical district in a typical year, the in-year grants come to about as much again as its formula money: the median is 1.09 times its 2016 allocation. They are lumpy. In a typical district the year-to-year spread is 59 % of its average, because a single school extension can arrive in one year.

Becoming aligned

Seven districts turned aligned in 2018 and stayed so for the three years after, against 31 that stayed unaligned. In 2023 it was 23 against 17. That change made 34 of the 57 districts aligned, up from 14.

Before each change the two groups moved together: the joint pre-trend test gives p = 0.96. After the 2018 change, districts that turned aligned received more in the first year (+62 %) and about the same in the next two: +17 % over the three years against the three before. After the 2023 change they received less in 2023: −61 % against 2022, −70 % against the three years before. Pooled, the change on becoming aligned is −38 % of the controls' usual grants, with a 95 % interval from −112 to +18 %.

The interval leaves room for a premium of up to about 18 %, which is below the published range. It leaves room for a large shortfall too, and the registered equivalence test cannot rule out either side. The design could detect only a change of about 100 %, and nothing that size is there.

The party caveat, plainly. Thirty districts had an ODS mayor at some point in 2016–2023, and ODS is the party that entered the coalition in 2023. Leave them all out and the estimate turns to about +13 %. Leaving out the districts of any other party keeps it negative, between about −37 and −56 %. So the negative pooled number is, in the main, what happened in 2023 to the districts of one party. That may be alignment. It may equally be something else those districts shared that year, and this design cannot tell the two apart. A permutation that pretends other parties had entered the coalition instead ranks the observed result in the middle of the 20 possible sets (p = 0.95).

What kind of money. H2 expected any premium to come as investment, the visible kind. The 2023 shortfall is in investment grants (−39 % of usual grants), while non-investment grants did not change (0 %). But with no premium to split, H2 is not supported (p = 0.77).

How far it holds

  • The specification curve. Six of the 54 specifications give a positive estimate. None has a randomisation p-value below 0.05, and none has a Conley–Taber interval above zero. Defining alignment by who holds most assembly seats, rather than by the mayor, makes the estimate more negative (−63 %). Counting a district as aligned when its largest list merely includes a coalition party brings it close to zero (−5 %).
  • Other estimators agree in direction:
    • a regression form of the same comparison, −15 % (clustered p = 0.70; wild bootstrap p = 0.67);
    • an estimator robust to effects that differ between switches11, −13 % (95 %: −74 to +34 %).
  • A placebo change between 2020 and 2021, when no coalition changed, gives −22 % (p = 0.87).
  • No single district decides it: dropping any one moves the pooled estimate by at most 35 points.
  • The district side. The districts' own reports to the Ministry of Finance, for 2022–2025, show the unbudgeted in-year transfers each received. Districts that became aligned in 2023 received 2 100 crowns less per resident a year than the controls (p = 0.76), and 2 200 less after adjusting for their slower population growth.

Two things measured along the way

Plans and outcomes. Nearly every district ends the year better off than its approved budget said: 54 of 57, by a median of 4 448 crowns per resident a year, 2022–2025. The gap is the in-year money itself. Take out the transfers that arrived during the year and were not in the approved budget, and the typical district ends slightly worse than planned (−1 096 crowns per resident). This is descriptive: it shows that districts do not budget the city's in-year grants, not whether they save them.

Size and building. The median district spends 47 % of its amended capital budget within the year. Larger districts do no better than smaller ones in the same tier; the slope on size is zero (p = 0.98).

The formula itself. The new coalition replaced the allocation formula for 2024 with last year's amount plus a top-up tied to the tax forecast. Allocations rose by a median of 10 %. The average rise was 19 % in districts not aligned in 2024 and 12 % in aligned ones. This is shown, not tested, because under the new rule the change follows each district's previous allocation.

What changed after registration

  • The years after 2023 were dropped from the test. The city's lists change format in 2024. The registered check compared the two formats where both exist (2019–2023). Their agreement across district-years was 0.72, below the 0.9 required, so the 2023 change is tested on 2023 alone, as pre-registered. Later years are shown in fig. 2 but not tested.
  • A parsing error was found and corrected after the results were first seen. In the 2023 list the amount is printed in the city's column and the district's column reads zero. The first run read the zeros, and gave 2023 grants of 25 million crowns instead of 4.1 billion, and a positive H1 estimate. Both runs, and the reason, are in the design file.
  • Choices the design left open were made and recorded:
    • the exact form of the equivalence test;
    • a simplified HonestDiD interval;
    • a two-year placebo window;
    • the set of parties used in the party-level permutation.
  • Four exploratory analyses were not run.

What this does not show

The lists record the city's grants to districts. They do not record what the city builds in a district itself or through its companies, and that is another way to favour a place. Two coalition changes, and one party's districts in the second, cannot separate alignment from anything else those districts shared. The design could detect only a very large premium, so a modest one, of the size found elsewhere, may exist and go unseen. The alignment coding was checked by two coders against public sources, not yet against every constituent-session resolution. No result here says anything about any individual mayor or councillor, and none is a causal effect of a party.
