Prague, measured · Part 3, the long version · pre-registered · the city's grants to its districts, 2015–2023

Does alignment pay?

Part 3 found that Prague's districts live mostly on money they are given. Much of it arrives during the year, as grants the city council and assembly decide one by one. This version asks whether a district receives more of those grants when its mayor's party joins the city's governing coalition. It reads every grant in the city's closing accounts and tests two hypotheses written down before any amount was added up. It detects no alignment premium. The design could only have detected a very large one, so the honest answer is "not detected", not "none".

Prague's 57 districts get most of their money from the city. Part of it follows a formula the city publishes each December: residents, area, pupils, green space and roads. The rest comes during the year, as grants for a school roof, a park, a fire brigade or a playground. Each is decided by the city council or the city assembly, and each is listed, with its resolution and purpose, in part III of the city's closing accounts1.

Discretionary money of this kind is where studies elsewhere have found an alignment premium: municipalities governed by the same party as the level above receive more. Studies of Spain2, Brazil3 and Italy4 find that aligned municipalities receive roughly a third to a half more (about 40 %, 26–41 % and 36–47 %), and Portugal 19 %5. In Germany the sign depended on which bloc governed the state: +25 % under one, −20 to −40 % under the other6. This text asks the same of Prague, under rules fixed in advance: a design reviewed by three referees and committed before any grant was added up7.

A caveat frames everything below. In Prague, a district "becomes aligned" when the city coalition changes, and the coalition changed only twice in the period. Parties, not districts, switch sides. So what follows can tell the effect of alignment apart from what else happened to one party's districts only as far as two events allow.

How this was done

The grants. The district lists of the city's closing accounts for 2014–2025 were downloaded and parsed: 18 311 lines, and at least 98 % of the item lines in every list. The outcome is the city's own grants, the money the city pays a district. Money a district pays back to the city is left out: loan repayments, returned grants and transfers, 162 lines and 1.1 billion crowns in 2016–2023. So are grants the state passes through the city, EU operational programmes and the year-end settlements.

The city's own grants still include three kinds of money that are not discretionary in the usual sense. The shares below are of all the city's own grants in 2016–2023:

  • income-tax refunds passed on to districts: 11–28 % a year;
  • shares of the gambling levy: 4–10 %;
  • repayable loans: 1–13 %.

Together they are 20–39 % a year. The registered outcome keeps them, and a check without them is reported below.

Each grant is dated by the assembly resolution that approved it, matched by number to the session dates in the roll calls of Part 1. The date decides the coalition it belongs to. Grants the outgoing, caretaker council decided in January and February 2023 count for the old coalition. A district's grants are measured against the formula money it received in 2016, so a small district and a large one can be compared. Per resident is shown alongside.

A correction made after the first results. A parsing error found after the first results changed 2023 from 25 million to 4.1 billion crowns and moved H1 from +19 % (p = 0.21) to −38 %. The error was that the 2023 list prints its amounts in the column the parser did not read. It was found because the 2023 total was implausible, not because of the direction of H1. An independent audit then found two more problems:

  • the parser counted money districts paid the city as grants;
  • it dated some approvals to the wrong day of a session held over several days.

With both corrected, H1 is −37 %. All three runs are recorded in the design file7.

Who governs a district. Every mayor of every district from the 2014 elections to September 2026 was recorded, with sources, in two independent coding passes by a language model. The two passes named the same mayor for 98.9 % of the 627 district-years, and the seven that differed were settled by hand before any grant was added up. The party comes from the official candidate registers. The independent audit checked about 40 spells against official sources, and all matched. A district is aligned in a year if its mayor on 30 June belongs to, or was nominated by, a party of the city coalition that day.

The comparison. The coalition changed twice:

  • in November 2018, when the Pirates, Praha Sobě and TOP 09 replaced ANO, ČSSD and the Greens;
  • in February 2023, when ODS replaced Praha Sobě.

At each change, districts that turned aligned are compared with districts unaligned throughout, before and after. This is a difference-in-differences stacked by event8. The p-values come from randomisation inference: which districts switched is reshuffled within each tier of districts, 9 999 times9. The intervals use a method for comparisons with few treated units10. Effects are stated in % of the control districts' usual grants: the average, over the two changes, of their level in the three years before.

What the design could see. A power simulation, run on the real switches before any grant was added up, put the smallest detectable premium at about 100 % of a district's usual grants. That is two to three times the published premiums. The design says in advance that a non-rejection is to be read as "not detected", never as "no premium".

Percentages are of the control districts' usual grants in the years before each change. p-values are one-sided, from randomisation inference; the smallest attainable is 0.0001. Holm thresholds13 are 0.025 and 0.05, and neither test passes. The equivalence test (is any premium within ±20 %?) is inconclusive, p = 0.53. A parsing error found after the first results changed 2023 from 25 m to 4.1 bn CZK and moved H1 from +19 % (p = 0.21) to −38 %. The corrections for flow direction and dating then moved it to −37 %.

What the city gives

The city gives its districts between 2.2 and 5.4 billion crowns a year this way, beyond the formula. Investment is between 49 and 73 % of it (2016–2023). Over all district-years, the median is 1.04 times the district's 2016 formula allocation. The grants are lumpy: in the median district the year-to-year spread is 60 % of its average, because a single school extension can arrive in one year.

Becoming aligned

At each change, the comparison takes the districts that turned aligned and stayed so, and the districts that stayed unaligned throughout.

  • 2018: 7 districts turned aligned for the three years after, against 31 that stayed unaligned.
  • 2023: 23 turned aligned, against 17. That change made 34 of the 57 districts aligned, up from 14.

Before each change the two groups moved similarly: the joint pre-trend test gives p = 0.97.

  • After the 2018 change, districts that turned aligned received more in the first year (+70 %) and about the same in the next two. Over the three years, against the three before, that is +20 %.
  • After the 2023 change, they received less: −59 % against 2022, and −67 % against the three years before.

Pooled, the change on becoming aligned is −37 % of the controls' usual grants, with a 95 % interval from −115 to +21 %.

The equivalence test does not rule out a 20 % premium (upper side p = 0.11), nor a large shortfall. The design could detect only a change of about 100 %, and nothing that size is there.

The party caveat, plainly. Thirty districts had a mayor elected as an ODS member or nominee at some point in 2016–2023, and ODS is the party that entered the coalition in 2023. Leave them all out and the estimate turns to about +14 %. Leaving out the districts of any other party keeps it negative, between about −35 and −55 %. The sign depends on the districts whose mayors come from the party that entered in 2023. This design cannot say whether that is alignment or anything else they shared. A permutation that pretends other parties had entered the coalition instead places the observed result below 19 of the 20 possible sets (p = 0.95).

What kind of money. H2 expected any premium to come as investment, the visible kind. The pooled shortfall is all in investment grants (−39 %); non-investment grants changed by +2 %. With no premium to split, H2 is not supported (p = 0.76).

How far it holds

  • The specification curve. Six of the 36 specifications give a positive estimate. None has a p-value below 0.05, whether from randomisation, the wild cluster bootstrap14 or small-sample clustered errors1516. The definition of alignment moves the estimate:
    • by who holds most assembly seats, rather than by the mayor, it is more negative (−63 %);
    • counting a district as aligned when its largest list merely includes a coalition party, it is close to zero (−10 %).
  • Other ways to measure the same thing:
    • without the loans, gambling-levy shares and tax refunds: −43 %;
    • counting money districts paid the city as negative grants: −36 %;
    • alignment by the coders' recorded party membership, which treats non-members nominated by a coalition party as unaligned: −36 %.
  • Other estimators agree in direction:
    • a regression form of the same comparison: −13 % (clustered p = 0.68; wild bootstrap p = 0.64);
    • an estimator robust to effects that differ between switches12: −14 % (95 %: −76 to +32 %).
  • A placebo change between 2020 and 2021, when no coalition changed, gives −18 % (p = 0.79).
  • One district matters a great deal. Dropping one control district, Troja, brings the estimate to about 0 %. No other district moves it by more than 9 points.
  • Turning unaligned. At the two changes, 12 districts left alignment (8 in 2018, 4 in 2023). They received about as much as those aligned throughout (6 and 10): −7 %, p = 0.50. Twelve further switches happened mid-term, outside the two changes. They enter only the heterogeneity-robust estimator.
  • The district side. The districts' own reports to the Ministry of Finance for 2022–202517 show the unbudgeted in-year transfers each received. Districts that became aligned in 2023 received 2 100 crowns less per resident a year than the controls (p = 0.77), and 2 200 less adjusted for their slower population growth.

Three things measured along the way

Plans and outcomes. Nearly every district ends the year better off than its approved budget said: 54 of 57, 2022–2025. The median district does better by 4 448 crowns per resident a year. The gap is the in-year money itself. Take out the transfers that arrived during the year and were not in the approved budget, and the median district ends slightly worse than planned (−1 096 crowns per resident). This is descriptive. It shows that districts do not budget the city's in-year grants, not whether they save them.

Size and building. The median district spends 47 % of its amended capital budget within the year. The slope on size within the same tier is indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.98).

The formula itself. The new coalition replaced the allocation formula for 2024 with last year's amount plus a top-up tied to the tax forecast. Allocations rose by a median of 10 %: on average 19 % in districts not aligned in 2024, and 12 % in aligned ones. This is shown, not tested, because under the new rule the change follows each district's previous allocation.

What changed after registration

  • The years after 2023 were dropped from the test. The city's lists change format in 2024. The registered check compared the two formats where both exist (2019–2023) and found an agreement of 0.72, below the 0.9 required, so the 2023 change is tested on 2023 alone. Recomputed after the corrections below, the agreement is 0.79, still below the bar.
  • Power is weaker than simulated. The simulation assumed three years after the 2023 change. The test has one year, with 23 switching districts against 17 controls.
  • Three corrections were made after the results were seen. Each is disclosed with the runs before and after:
    • the 2023 amounts;
    • the direction of flows;
    • the dating of approvals when a session sat on several days.
  • The district-side covariate adjustment. It was triggered in the first run and not in the corrected one. Its trigger, a population-growth imbalance with p below 0.1, gave p = 0.098 with 1 999 draws and p = 0.11 with 9 999. The adjusted estimate is shown either way. An imbalance in the share of children (p = 0.086) is not adjusted for, because the registered rule does not name it.
  • Choices the design left open were made and recorded:
    • the denominator of the percentages;
    • the exact form of the equivalence test;
    • a simplified HonestDiD interval;
    • a two-year placebo window;
    • the set of parties in the party-level permutation;
    • the heterogeneity-robust estimator, implemented directly rather than with its authors' package.
  • The first specification curve counted 18 specifications twice (Praha 1 is in neither comparison) and computed the bootstrap and clustered errors for only 18. The curve above has 36 unique specifications, each with all three.
  • Now reported:
    • switch-out;
    • the always-aligned districts;
    • the mid-term switches;
    • the smallest attainable p;
    • the HonestDiD interval at half the bound.
  • Not done:
    • the alignment coding has not been checked against every constituent-session resolution;
    • four exploratory analyses were not run.
  • One figure was seen before registration: one 2025 total, for one district, while the lists were being parsed. It is outside every test window.

What this does not show

The lists record the city's grants to districts. They do not record what the city builds in a district itself or through its companies, which is another channel through which a district can benefit. Two coalition changes, and one party's districts in the second, cannot separate alignment from anything else those districts shared. The design could detect only a very large premium, so a modest one, of the size found elsewhere, may exist and go unseen. The alignment coding was checked against registers and, for about 40 spells, against official sources, but not yet against every constituent-session resolution. No result here says anything about any individual mayor or councillor, and none is a causal effect of a party.
