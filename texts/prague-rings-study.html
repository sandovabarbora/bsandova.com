Place or people: Prague's estates, measured again

Part 2 found that Prague votes by distance from the centre and by how many of a precinct's flats are in panel buildings, and hardly at all by the metro. It could not say whether the estates mattered as places or only for who lives in them. This version answers with the 2021 census and three elections. It tests seven hypotheses written down before the data were joined, and reports every one whichever way it fell.

Where people vote from and who they are are hard to tell apart. A panel estate is a housing stock, but it is also a population: people who were allocated flats there in the 1970s and 1980s and grew old in them, their children, and later buyers of the cheapest flats in the city. When ANO does better in the estates, that can be because of the estates (their distance, their shops, their neighbours, what the place does to what people think) or because the estates hold more of the people who vote ANO anywhere. Electoral geography has argued about this for four decades, as context against composition12. For immigration attitudes in large European cities, the best recent panel evidence finds that most of what looks like place is people3.

Aggregate data cannot settle this for individuals45. What they can do is measure how much of the estates' gradient is left once what the census knows about their residents is held equal. This text does that, under rules fixed in advance.

How this was done

The design was written, sent to three reviewers (a statistician, an electoral geographer and a census-data specialist), revised, and committed to the site's public repository before any census count or earlier election was joined to a precinct6. It fixes the questions, the hypotheses, the one test for each, the decision rules and fourteen robustness checks. Everything that changed afterwards is listed, dated, at the end of that file and summarised below.

The census counts people on a grid of 1 km squares7. Prague's median precinct is an eighth of a square, so every cell's counts were spread over the precincts it overlaps, in proportion to the flats standing in each on census day, from the cadastre's building register8. This is dasymetric allocation9. It puts 1 301 482 residents into Prague's precincts against the census's 1 301 432. An independent allocation from the census's 958 basic settlement units in Prague agrees within 25 % for all but 13 precincts.

A precinct still only gets the composition of the square it sits in, while its housing is measured building by building. Comparing the two at different resolutions would flatter housing, so the main models add precincts up into the square that holds most of their flats: 282 clusters, each measured at one resolution. The regressions weight by valid votes. Uncertainty comes from a wild cluster bootstrap over Prague's 57 districts1011, with conventional, small-sample-corrected12 and spatial13 standard errors computed alongside. The six confirmatory tests share a 5 % error rate through Holm's procedure14.

Place or people

Across the 282 clusters, ANO's share rises by 0.99 points for every ten percentage points more flats in panel buildings, holding the distance from the centre, the distance to the metro and the share of family houses equal. Add what the census knows about education and citizenship and the gradient falls to 0.56 points (95 % interval 0.47 to 0.66). The census block raises the share of variation explained from 70 % to 87 %.

Education does almost all of the work. A decomposition of the gap15 puts 0.44 of the 0.42 points on education and nothing on the share of foreign citizens. What remains is not small: 57 % of the gradient survives, and the test in the other direction (is more than half left?) rejects at p = 0.03. For SPOLU the pattern is the same and weaker: 42 % explained, with neither direction significant.

Two things qualify the result, and both were written down before it was seen. First, the census measures a square kilometre, not a street. Measuring composition coarsely under-controls for it, so the gradient left over is an upper bound on what the estates contribute as places, and 43 % is a lower bound on what composition explains. Second, the registered primary block leaves out age, because the estates' age structure is itself partly a product of the housing: flats were allocated to young families in one decade, and those families aged in place. With the share of over-65s and under-15s added, composition explains 52 %, and the test can no longer tell the answer from one half.

The 2021 election, a replication fixed in advance, gives 44 % for ANO and 31 % for SPOLU.

The metro

Part 2 found no effect of the metro. Finding none is not the same as showing there is none, so this version asked the stronger question: is the metro's effect small enough not to matter? The threshold was set at a quarter of a point per doubling of the distance to a station. Across the middle half of Prague's precincts that is half a point, a fortieth of ANO's vote in the city.

Every estimate is small and every interval crosses zero, but every interval also reaches past −0.25. The design-stage power check, run before any outcome was joined, had predicted this: with 282 clusters the standard error was bound to be larger than the margin. The margin was not widened afterwards, because the 2025 estimate was already known and a margin chosen after seeing it would prove nothing. The answer is the honest one: the data cannot tell a negligible metro effect from a small one. If anything, ANO does slightly better closer to stations once the rest is held equal.

The estates are pulling away

Precinct boundaries move between elections and the cadastre keeps only the current ones. The comparison over time therefore uses the 819 precincts that exist under the same number in 2017, 2021 and 2025 and whose register grew or shrank no more than 10 % beyond what new flats inside them predict. Matched precincts have more panel flats than the rest (40 % against 27 %), because the unmatched are mostly where the city is still building.

ANO's estate gradient grew by 0.26 points per 10 pp of panel flats between 2017 and 2025 (p < 0.001), from 0.55 to 0.80, with 2021 in between. It is the one registered hypothesis that passes the family-wise threshold. It holds under the stricter and the looser matching rule (+0.24 and +0.30) and with weights that correct for which precincts could be matched (+0.26). The census comes from between the elections, so this comparison holds housing fixed but not people. It shows that ANO's vote became more concentrated in the estates, not why.

Turnout in the centre

Turnout is lowest in the centre, and Part 2 guessed why: the centre may have more registered voters who live somewhere else. Czech permanent residence is an address in a register, not where a person sleeps, and people with no address of their own are registered at the district office. The test compared turnout counted against the register with turnout counted against the census's Czech adults who actually live there. Precincts containing a district office, a hospital, a care home or a prison were excluded, 158 in all.

The guess fails, and in an unexpected direction. Against the register, turnout rises by 1.9 points per doubling of the distance from Můstek. Against resident Czech adults, it falls by 4.3. Measured against the people who live there, the centre votes more, not less. The second test agrees that registration is not the story: envelopes scale with the register at an elasticity of 0.99, and residents add nothing once the register is known.

This is the least secure result in the text, for a reason found only after the data were joined. The register count the Statistical Office publishes per precinct includes voters who came with a voting pass, and passes are used most where people are away from home. In all 14 819 precincts in the file, abroad included, envelopes never exceed that count, which is what one would see if pass voters are added to it. The centre's count is therefore not the register alone, and the published files cannot separate the two.

How far it holds

CheckANO θSPOLU θmetro

Nothing registered turns the result. Dropping any one of the 57 districts moves θ by at most 0.02. Fractional logit, which respects that vote shares lie between 0 and 116, gives 46 %. At the level of single precincts, where composition is coarser than housing and the leftover gradient is inflated by construction, composition explains 30 %. The clusters' residuals are only faintly clustered in space (Moran's I = 0.06), and a spatial error model gives the same answers (Hausman test17 p = 0.62). Shuffling panel shares within districts leaves an apparent attenuation of 0.19 points, not zero, because districts differ in both housing and people. The observed 0.42 lies outside that range. The placebo with the planned line D stations was not run, because official station coordinates could not be found in open data.

What changed after registration

  • The metro test was underpowered from the start. The design-stage check found this before any outcome was joined. It was recorded then, and the margin was kept.
  • Operational details the design left open:
    • 15–17 year olds are estimated as three fifths of the 15–19 group, because only five-year groups are published.
    • Dormitories are not flagged, because no open list exists.
    • The bootstrap is unrestricted.
    • 2017 precinct geometry was not recovered from the cadastre's change files; the registered matching rule is used alone.
  • The publication-timing rule was dropped before estimation.

The full list is in the design file6. The numbers come from four scripts:

  • tools/praha/rings_data.py
  • rings_power.py
  • rings_extended.py
  • rings_robust.py

What this does not show

These are areas, not people: no result here says how any resident votes4. The census is from March 2021, four and a half years before the 2025 election, taken during distance teaching and before the refugees of 2022. The grid is coarse, which caps what composition can explain. The areas are Prague's, and a different grid would give somewhat different numbers18. The leftover estate gradient is what the census does not measure, and the place itself is only one candidate. Income, tenure and how long people have lived there are not in the grid, and any of them could carry it. Nothing here is a causal effect of living in a panel building.
